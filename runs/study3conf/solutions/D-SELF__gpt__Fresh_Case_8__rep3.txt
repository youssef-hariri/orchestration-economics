# CBAM Supplier Partnership Strategy  
## Supporting compliance while preserving supply relationships and shared competitiveness

## Executive thesis

The buyer should manage CBAM as a **supplier partnership, resilience and industrial-transition programme**, not as a one-way compliance mandate.

The immediate objectives are to:

1. Protect legal and customs compliance.
2. Preserve critical supply from India, Türkiye and selected North African markets.
3. Share the cost of reporting capability fairly.
4. Reduce future carbon exposure through credible, bankable projects.
5. Maintain supplier trust where CBAM is perceived as asymmetric or protectionist.

CBAM’s legal obligations remain with the relevant EU importer or authorised declarant. Suppliers provide production and emissions information, but contractual arrangements cannot transfer the buyer’s public-law responsibility. The programme should therefore combine supplier accountability with buyer-funded capability building, transparent commercial treatment and proportionate remediation.

This strategy is a framework for approval. Before implementation, the buyer’s authorised declarant, customs, legal, tax, finance and country-risk owners must validate the applicable rules, reporting periods, calculation methods, evidence standards and commercial mechanics.

---

## 1. Guiding principles

### 1.1 Separate three objectives

| Workstream | Purpose | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **CBAM compliance data** | Support the legally applicable declaration and certificate process | Mandatory where relevant |
| **Product-carbon and low-carbon claims** | Support customer reporting and voluntary product differentiation | Voluntary unless separately required |
| **Physical decarbonisation** | Reduce energy use, emissions, operating cost and future exposure | Strategic and commercial |

Data sufficient for a voluntary low-carbon claim is not automatically sufficient for the statutory CBAM calculation. Renewable-energy certificates, recycled-content claims and environmental-management certifications should not be assumed to reduce CBAM liability without confirming the applicable rules.

### 1.2 Apply a clear decision hierarchy

When objectives conflict, decisions should prioritise:

1. Legal, customs, sanctions and integrity requirements.
2. Worker safety, human rights and community protection.
3. Continuity and diversification of critical supply.
4. Total landed cost and financial exposure.
5. Verified emissions performance and reduction potential.
6. Voluntary product differentiation.

Exceptions should document the trade-off, financial exposure, alternatives considered, duration and approving authority.

### 1.3 Distinguish incapability from non-cooperation

A supplier that initially lacks meters, staff or allocation systems should receive support and a time-bound improvement path. Fraud, deliberate concealment, serious safety or human-rights risks, and refusal to identify relevant production sites require a different escalation response.

### 1.4 Share value, not only cost

The buyer should not simply pass through an unsupported carbon surcharge. It should share appropriate value from:

- energy savings;
- reduced reliance on applicable default values;
- lower verified emissions exposure;
- qualified low-carbon attributes;
- improved market access; and
- longer-term demand visibility.

### 1.5 Avoid unnecessary coercion

Supplier participation in buyer-funded support should be commercially voluntary. However, the buyer may still need usable information for in-scope imports. Any resulting pricing, volume or sourcing decision must identify its legal, risk or commercial basis and must not retaliate against suppliers for raising fairness concerns.

## 2. Scope and regulatory responsibility

### Scope control

The buyer should maintain a controlled register linking every relevant product flow to:

- CN code and product description;
- product form and SKU;
- country of origin;
- producing installation;
- production route;
- importer of record;
- authorised CBAM declarant;
- applicable emissions boundary;
- evidence requirements; and
- applicable regulatory source and methodology version.

Steel and aluminium should not be treated as uniformly covered commodities. Scope must be confirmed at product, shipment and origin level against the rules applicable to the relevant reporting period.

### Responsibility map

| Activity | Accountable owner |
|---|---|
| CN-code, origin and scope determination | Customs/trade compliance, with legal review |
| Installation and production-route identification | Supplier, validated by buyer |
| Production, energy, precursor and allocation data | Supplier |
| Applicable calculation basis | Authorised declarant, with legal/customs support |
| CBAM calculation and declaration | Authorised declarant |
| Certificate procurement and surrender | Authorised declarant and treasury |
| Carbon-price deduction approval | Authorised declarant, legal and tax |
| Supplier grants and co-investment | Procurement and finance |
| Commercial true-ups and premiums | Procurement and finance |
| Worker and community safeguards | Supplier, monitored by buyer |
| Regulatory change control | Legal/customs programme owner |
| Internal audit and board reporting | Internal audit and executive sponsor |

The buyer should maintain a regulatory source register covering legislation, implementing rules, official guidance, templates, methodology versions, effective dates, interpretations and superseded requirements. No methodology, deduction, premium, pass-through formula or public claim should become operational until approved by the authorised declarant and legal/customs owner.

## 3. Supplier segmentation

Segmentation should occur at **site, product and route level**, not only at corporate or country level.

### Assessment dimensions

| Dimension | Indicative evidence |
|---|---|
| Data capability | Metering, production records, allocation method, named data owners |
| Strategic importance | Volume, specification, qualification lead time, alternative capacity |
| Transition potential | Identified measures, technical feasibility, financing pathway |
| Production route and emissions profile | Integrated steel, EAF, DRI, primary aluminium, remelt or other route |
| Financial capacity | Ability to fund contributions and recurring controls |
| Social, environmental and integrity risk | Safety, labour, water, land, sanctions and anti-corruption controls |
| Cooperation | Timeliness, transparency and correction behaviour |

The programme office should use a standard 1–5 scorecard with defined evidence anchors, red-line exclusions and supplier challenge rights. Legal, sanctions, fraud, severe safety and serious human-rights concerns cannot be offset by commercial importance.

### Supplier pathways

#### Segment A — Strategic leaders

**Profile:** Strong data controls, capable environmental teams and credible reduction plans.

**Support:**

- accelerated qualification;
- limited, purpose-specific data requests;
- protection of proprietary process information;
- multi-year demand visibility where justified;
- co-investment in high-value projects;
- qualified low-carbon premiums; and
- access to market-development opportunities.

#### Segment B — Technically capable but data-constrained

**Profile:** Adequate production capability but incomplete installation mapping, allocation, precursor or assurance controls.

**Milestones:**

- producing-site and installation identification;
- CN-code and product-family linkage;
- named data owner;
- meter and records inventory;
- documented allocation method;
- monthly reconciliation;
- mock calculation; and
- readiness review.

#### Segment C — High-emissions suppliers with credible transition potential

Retention requires a financed plan covering:

- route- and product-level baseline;
- no-regret efficiency measures;
- capital requirements and funding sources;
- energy, cost and emissions assumptions;
- infrastructure, water and permit dependencies;
- worker and community impacts;
- accountable management;
- milestones; and
- volume-reduction or exit triggers.

Large hydrogen, DRI, furnace-conversion or primary-aluminium projects require separate investment approval.

#### Segment D — Small or low-readiness suppliers

Provide:

- local-language guidance;
- offline spreadsheets or low-cost tools;
- regional technical clinics;
- group training;
- subsidised meters and baseline assessments;
- shared consultants;
- fixed site grants; and
- additional funding against milestones.

Manual records or allocation proxies may be used temporarily only where approved by the buyer’s calculation owner and authorised declarant. Each proxy must have an evidence basis, owner, approval date, expiry date and upgrade plan.

#### Segment E — Non-cooperative or high-risk suppliers

Use a proportionate sequence:

1. Clarification or technical support.
2. Corrective-action plan.
3. Independent review.
4. Temporary holdback, risk pricing or volume cap.
5. Alternative-source qualification.
6. Volume reduction.
7. Responsible exit.

Fraud, deliberate concealment, severe harm or serious legal exposure may justify accelerated restriction or exit.

## 4. Supplier support package

### Minimum viable data package

For each in-scope flow, suppliers should provide:

- legal entity and producing installation;
- origin;
- product and CN-code mapping;
- production route;
- reporting period and output;
- relevant fuel and electricity activity data;
- relevant process emissions;
- applicable precursors;
- allocation method;
- source records;
- named data owner;
- known limitations;
- default-value contingency; and
- evidence for any claimed qualifying carbon price actually paid.

The buyer should label every request as legally necessary, buyer-required for a stated purpose, or voluntary.

### Readiness stages

| Stage | Minimum standard | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Site baseline | Site, route, output, fuel and basic production records | 90 days |
| 2. Controlled activity data | Meter inventory, reconciliation and named owners | 6 months |
| 3. Product and precursor allocation | Product-family allocation and relevant upstream mapping | 12 months |
| 4. Assured reporting | Complete evidence pack and assurance where required | 18 months |

Extensions should be risk-based. Equipment delays, infrastructure disruption and force majeure may justify extensions; suspected fraud or serious safety and human-rights risks should not receive routine extensions.

### Practical support

The buyer should provide:

- multilingual guidance;
- calculation templates;
- approved factor references;
- meter and data-system assessments;
- technical helpdesk and clinics;
- mock assurance;
- reconciliation of invoices, meters and production records;
- corrective-action tracking; and
- training matched to supplier maturity.

The programme should accommodate unreliable connectivity, power and metering infrastructure through controlled offline reporting and defined temporary procedures.

## 5. Commercial and financing model

### Four cost accounts

The buyer should track separately:

1. **Legal compliance:** certificates, declaration administration and importer controls.
2. **Data readiness:** meters, systems, training and assurance.
3. **Decarbonisation:** equipment, energy and process improvements.
4. **Commercial value:** energy savings, avoided exposure, premiums and market access.

### Cost allocation

- The buyer should pay or substantially co-pay for buyer-required reporting capability, particularly for smaller suppliers.
- Suppliers remain responsible for accurate operational data and deliberate misrepresentation.
- Decarbonisation costs should reflect strategic importance, expected benefit, supplier capacity, asset ownership and social risk.
- Buyer-controlled filing failures and penalties should not be passed to suppliers.
- Unconfirmed future policy benefits should not be priced into contracts.

### Support governance

Finance should approve:

- annual programme budget;
- per-site and per-supplier exposure limits;
- regional concentration limits;
- support bands by supplier size and readiness;
- approval thresholds for grants, loans and offtake;
- support tapering rules; and
- asset-recovery and clawback principles.

Every funding agreement should define eligible expenditure, milestones, payment conditions, ownership, audit rights, tax and currency treatment, termination consequences and limits on exclusivity.

### Pass-through and true-up

Contracts should specify:

- provisional emissions quantity;
- approved methodology and version;
- certificate-price reference and date;
- treatment of supplier-specific and default values;
- qualifying carbon-price deductions;
- corrections after invoicing;
- currency, tax and administration costs;
- caps, collars or review points; and
- audit evidence.

The buyer may use a provisional adjustment based on the best approved information available at invoice date, followed by a true-up after the relevant declaration process. Undisputed amounts remain payable; disputed adjustments may be held back pending review.

### Carbon-price deductions

A deduction should be recognised only after legal review and evidence that a qualifying carbon price was effectively paid for the relevant emissions. Evidence should establish the legal basis, liable entity, installation, emissions covered, amount paid, payment records, exemptions or free allocation, linkage to the goods and absence of double counting.

Renewable certificates, voluntary credits, internal carbon prices, general environmental fees and climate-policy expenditure should not automatically be treated as deductible.

### Premiums and gain sharing

A low-carbon premium is separate from avoided CBAM cost. It should be paid only where the attribute is:

- clearly defined;
- methodologically comparable;
- traceable;
- assured where appropriate;
- not double counted; and
- commercially valuable.

The buyer should maintain a claim register recording tonnes claimed, reporting period, customer use, evidence, assurance, retirement or transfer status, and correction or withdrawal history.

## 6. Illustrative value-sharing examples

These examples are internal illustrations, not regulatory forecasts.

### Steel

A supplier ships 10,000 tonnes annually. For commercial planning, the approved provisional basis is 1.8 tonnes CO₂e per tonne and a reference certificate price of €75:

- provisional exposure: 10,000 × 1.8 × €75 = **€1.35 million**.

Readiness investment:

- metering and data controls: €80,000;
- buyer grant: €50,000;
- supplier contribution: €30,000.

A €1.2 million efficiency project is funded:

- supplier: 40% (€480,000);
- buyer: 30% (€360,000);
- external finance: 30% (€360,000).

After completion, approved data show intensity falling to 1.62 tonnes CO₂e per tonne:

- post-project exposure at €75: 10,000 × 1.62 × €75 = **€1.215 million**;
- illustrative avoided exposure: **€135,000**;
- annual energy savings: **€140,000**.

Illustrative allocation:

- supplier retains 60% of energy savings;
- buyer receives 40% until its investment is recovered;
- verified avoided exposure is shared 50:50, subject to the final declarant calculation;
- no low-carbon premium is paid unless a separate attribute is qualified.

At a €50 certificate price, the illustrative avoided exposure falls to €90,000; at €100, it rises to €180,000. The true-up should therefore use the approved price basis rather than guarantee a fixed amount.

### Aluminium

A supplier ships 5,000 tonnes:

- 3,000 tonnes primary;
- 2,000 tonnes remelt.

Readiness costs:

- electricity metering: €45,000;
- alumina and anode traceability: €25,000;
- recycled-content controls: €20,000.

For a small, strategically important site, the buyer funds 70% and the supplier 30%.

A renewable-power arrangement may support a voluntary product claim, but no statutory CBAM benefit is assumed without legal confirmation. A €12 per tonne premium is payable only for output meeting the approved claim standard. If 2,000 tonnes qualify:

- potential premium: 2,000 × €12 = **€24,000**.

The premium is separate from any statutory carbon-cost effect and is withheld until evidence and assurance requirements are met.

## 7. Decarbonisation priorities

### Steel

Prioritise:

- route and precursor mapping;
- metering and allocation;
- yield and furnace optimisation;
- process-gas recovery;
- waste-heat recovery;
- scrap quality and availability;
- electricity efficiency;
- DRI or hydrogen only where technically and financially credible.

### Aluminium

Prioritise:

- separation of primary, remelt and fabricated output;
- electricity and smelting efficiency;
- alumina and anode traceability;
- anode-effect reduction;
- scrap collection and sorting;
- recycled-content controls;
- electricity procurement evidence; and
- careful distinction between statutory CBAM data and voluntary footprint claims.

Projects should be categorised as:

1. no-regret operational measures;
2. medium-term equipment or energy projects; and
3. transformational projects requiring separate investment governance.

## 8. Worker, community and human-rights safeguards

No emissions reduction should be accepted if achieved through unlawful layoffs, unsafe work, retaliation, wage suppression or displacement of environmental harm.

Supported projects should include safeguards proportionate to risk:

- consultation with permanent, temporary, subcontracted and migrant workers;
- confidential worker interviews;
- health and safety change assessments;
- training and reskilling;
- contractor controls;
- non-retaliation protections;
- water, land, energy, traffic and pollution assessments;
- community consultation;
- permit and rights review;
- grievance access in relevant local languages; and
- independent review for serious allegations.

Community consultation does not replace legally required permits, consent, rights recognition or regulatory approvals.

### Grievance channels

1. **Operational:** data corrections and technical questions.
2. **Commercial:** pass-through, premiums, grants and volume decisions.
3. **Integrity and human rights:** bribery, coercion, retaliation, unsafe work or community harm.

Minimum standards should include acknowledgement within five working days, initial assessment within 15 working days, resolution within 30–60 days where practicable, written reasons, anonymity where requested, non-retaliation protection and independent escalation.

## 9. Data governance and assurance

The buyer should adopt a data charter covering:

- supplier ownership and buyer licence;
- purpose limitation;
- permitted users;
- commercially sensitive information;
- aggregation and anonymisation;
- cross-border transfer;
- cybersecurity;
- retention and deletion;
- correction rights;
- third-party access; and
- breach notification.

### Evidence and correction process

Where records conflict:

1. the issue is identified;
2. the supplier data owner assesses the evidence;
3. the buyer calculation owner reviews it;
4. professional judgement is documented;
5. the corrected value is approved; and
6. legal/customs determines whether regulatory resubmission is required.

Corrections may also trigger a commercial true-up, claim withdrawal or customer notification. Historical versions should be retained, and portal data should reconcile with ERP and customs records before submission.

### Assurance layers

| Layer | Purpose | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Regulatory calculation | CBAM declaration and certificates | Buyer/authorised declarant |
| Supplier controls | Production, energy, precursor and allocation evidence | Supplier |
| Voluntary claims | Product carbon, recycled content and low-carbon claims | Claim owner, assured as appropriate |

Readiness consultants may conduct gap assessments and mock reviews but should not be presented as statutory verifiers. The programme target is zero known unresolved material misstatements, with documented investigation and remediation; it does not guarantee regulatory acceptance.

## 10. Country-sensitive playbooks

### India

Assess by site, state and production route:

- integrated steel;
- EAF;
- DRI;
- secondary production;
- primary aluminium;
- remelt; and
- downstream fabrication.

Diagnostics should cover coal, gas and electricity exposure, precursor and subcontractor mapping, product allocation, scrap quality, furnace efficiency, metering, local assurance capacity, labour requirements and data-transfer restrictions.

Do not assume Indian climate programmes or renewable certificates qualify for a CBAM deduction.

### Türkiye

Prioritise:

- EAF electricity consumption and sourcing;
- scrap quality and traceability;
- product-family allocation;
- existing EU supply-chain integration;
- logistics and lead-time advantages;
- verified lower-emission pilot lanes; and
- transparent gain sharing.

Monitor Turkish carbon-pricing developments, but do not price in future deductions until legal treatment and payment evidence are confirmed.

### North Africa

Use regional programme administration only where efficient; assess each country and site separately. The initial country list and selection criteria should be approved by country-risk and legal teams.

Diagnostics should cover:

- production route and energy source;
- electricity, gas and infrastructure reliability;
- local assurance capacity;
- currency, payment and political risk;
- foreign funding and exchange controls;
- beneficial ownership and sanctions;
- labour and consultation rules;
- data-export restrictions;
- permits;
- water availability;
- renewable-power deliverability;
- hydrogen or DRI economics; and
- community impacts.

Solar, wind and hydrogen potential should be treated as hypotheses requiring a credible power, water, infrastructure and offtake case.

Country assumptions should be reviewed annually and after material political, regulatory, energy or infrastructure changes.

## 11. Governance, gates and escalation

### Governance

Create a cross-functional CBAM Supplier Partnership Office involving:

- procurement;
- sustainability;
- customs and trade compliance;
- legal;
- finance and treasury;
- tax;
- operations and engineering;
- information security;
- human rights and HSE; and
- internal audit.

The executive steering committee should approve budgets, major investments, exceptional supplier retention, volume restrictions and exits.

Supplier forums should operate under competition-law rules: no discussion of prices, bids, customers, capacity or allocation; only aggregated and anonymised benchmarking; pre-approved agendas; legal review; and formal minutes.

### Implementation roadmap

#### 0–30 days: immediate control

- confirm scope, origin, importer and installation mapping;
- identify incomplete or default-dependent flows;
- establish interim calculation and financial scenarios;
- assign owners;
- issue the minimum data request;
- launch the regulatory source register; and
- establish incident escalation.

**Gate:** every high-risk flow has an interim treatment, financial scenario, owner and contingency.

#### 31–90 days: critical-flow stabilisation

- complete critical-flow assessments;
- agree interim commercial treatment;
- fund priority meters and data controls;
- implement evidence and data-governance standards;
- issue cure periods and dispute rights; and
- identify alternative capacity.

**Gate:** each critical flow has either an approved supplier-specific pathway or a documented default-value contingency, owner, exposure and remediation date.

#### 3–9 months: capability and contracting

- approve supplier methodologies;
- conduct mock assurance;
- amend contracts;
- approve funding agreements;
- implement worker and community safeguards;
- qualify alternatives; and
- test process controls.

**Gate:** no strategic flow relies indefinitely on an unowned data gap or provisional price formula.

#### 9–24 months: measurable reduction

- execute efficiency, metering, scrap, remelt and electricity projects;
- introduce product-family data where appropriate;
- review premiums and true-ups;
- test contingency supply; and
- publish aggregate outcomes.

Transformational projects should follow separate investment gates.

### Adverse commercial action

Every volume restriction, pricing change, suspension or exit must document:

- specific trigger;
- evidence;
- legal or contractual basis;
- support offered;
- cure period;
- proportionality assessment;
- decision-maker;
- supplier appeal rights; and
- review or expiry date.

## 12. KPIs and management triggers

The programme office should maintain a KPI dictionary with definitions, formulas, baselines, data sources, owners, frequency, targets, tolerances, assurance and management action.

### Compliance

- 100% of known in-scope flows mapped to an approved scope and origin basis.
- 100% of critical-flow volume linked to a producing installation.
- 100% of in-scope volume covered by an approved supplier-specific or default-value calculation basis.
- 100% of strategic suppliers with named data owners.
- No known unresolved critical finding at the declaration gate.
- All material corrections assessed for regulatory and commercial consequences.

### Supplier capability

- priority energy streams with calibrated meters or approved interim controls;
- evidence-pack completeness;
- mock-assurance completion;
- default-value reliance adjusted for product mix and import volume;
- corrective actions closed on time;
- supplier fairness and satisfaction; and
- number and value of suppliers receiving support.

### Environmental and commercial

- energy use per tonne;
- emissions intensity by route and product;
- verified reduction against a controlled baseline;
- CBAM cost per tonne;
- energy-cost savings;
- qualified low-carbon supply;
- total landed cost;
- qualified alternative capacity; and
- supply disruption incidents.

### Social

- workers trained or redeployed;
- safety performance during projects;
- contractor and migrant-worker coverage;
- grievances received, substantiated and resolved;
- retaliation incidents; and
- community impacts and mitigation.

Metrics must control for outsourcing, site switching, route change, product mix, allocation changes and reduced EU sales.

## 13. Principal risks and safeguards

| Risk | Safeguard and trigger |
|---|---|
| Incorrect scope or origin classification | Controlled customs register; material error triggers correction and legal review |
| Regulatory or methodology change | Source register, regulatory watch and contract change clauses |
| Unusable or conflicting supplier data | Evidence hierarchy, reconciliation, mock assurance and remediation |
| Unsupported carbon-price deduction | Legal approval and payment evidence before recognition |
| Certificate-price or liquidity volatility | Scenario modelling, treasury controls, caps and true-ups |
| Supplier disengagement or insolvency | Staged support, exposure limits and alternative qualification |
| Grant misuse or corruption | Objective eligibility, milestone payments, audit and anti-bribery controls |
| Commercially sensitive data breach | Data minimisation, access controls, encryption and incident response |
| Worker or community harm | Project risk tiering, consultation, independent review and suspension triggers |
| Stranded investment | Stage gates, sensitivity analysis, asset ownership and exit plans |
| Customer rejection of claims | Claim register, assurance, approval and withdrawal procedure |
| Supply disruption | Dual sourcing, safety stock, qualification lead times and contingency triggers |
| Competition-law exposure | Forum charter, legal review, restricted agendas and minutes |
| Dispute over true-up or premium | Transparent formula, evidence rights, holdback and appeal process |

## 14. Fairness and protectionism response

The buyer should communicate:

> CBAM is an EU legal requirement, but its administrative, financial and distributional effects are real. We will distinguish information legally needed for imports from buyer-specific and optional requests. We will not use CBAM as a pretext for arbitrary price reductions or automatic exclusion. We will fund or co-fund proportionate readiness support, protect confidential information, share value from credible improvements and provide correction, dispute and grievance channels. Commercial consequences will be explained in advance and will be proportionate where legally necessary information cannot be obtained.

The buyer should also:

- acknowledge that suppliers may view CBAM as protectionist;
- avoid claiming that the mechanism is unquestionably fair or legally settled;
- engage suppliers before changing data or pricing requirements;
- use local-language materials and trusted technical partners;
- avoid unnecessary exclusivity;
- maintain appeal and grievance channels;
- distinguish legal consequences from ordinary sourcing choices; and
- raise evidence-based concerns with regulators and industry bodies regarding default values, verification access, carbon-price recognition, electricity and precursor treatment, smaller-exporter burden and customs consistency.

The buyer should not treat disagreement with CBAM as non-cooperation. Conversely, suppliers should understand that inability or refusal to provide legally necessary information may create proportionate commercial consequences.

## Executive approvals requested

Senior leadership should approve:

1. A capped multi-year budget with separate readiness, assurance, investment and contingency envelopes.
2. The cross-functional CBAM Supplier Partnership Office and accountable executive sponsor.
3. The controlled scope, installation and regulatory-source registers.
4. The three-workstream architecture separating compliance, voluntary claims and decarbonisation.
5. Supplier segmentation, evidence standards, challenge rights and red-line risks.
6. Funding bands, exposure limits and investment-stage gates after finance, tax, legal and country validation.
7. The interim pass-through, premium, gain-sharing and true-up framework.
8. Contract, data-governance, competition-law, social-safeguard and grievance standards.
9. First-wave assessments for all critical and default-dependent flows.
10. Site- and route-specific pilots in India, Türkiye and selected North African countries.
11. A 90-day performance gate covering compliance exposure, supplier trust, financial commitments, alternative capacity and unresolved risks.

The recommended approach preserves relationships without weakening accountability. It gives suppliers a practical route to improve data quality and emissions performance, shares the costs and benefits of transition, protects the buyer’s regulatory position and maintains competitive emerging-market supply while CBAM enters its financial phase.
