# Global AI Productivity Toolkit Rollout  
## Executive Change-Management Strategy for 2026

## Executive summary

The group should preserve the global mandate while redefining it as **mandatory capability, approved-tool use, and responsible participation—not indiscriminate AI use for every task**.

All in-scope employees must:

- complete role-appropriate training and competence checks;
- use approved tools in designated, validated workflows;
- use approved AI where it is suitable, permitted, and safe;
- follow privacy, security, records, model-risk, professional, and human-review controls;
- report incidents, uncertainty, unsafe outputs, and unclear use cases.

Employees must not be required to use AI where professional judgment, customer protection, confidentiality, regulation, accessibility, or risk makes non-use appropriate. Raw activity must not become a proxy for productivity, loyalty, professional competence, or job worth.

The rollout should distinguish four outcomes:

1. **Capability:** employees can use AI safely and effectively.
2. **Availability:** employees have suitable, accessible approved tools.
3. **Appropriate adoption:** AI is used where it adds value and is permitted.
4. **Impact:** quality, customer, risk, employee, and business outcomes improve.

The central risk is that individual usage dashboards convert a capability program into a perceived surveillance and employment-ranking program. This can drive covert use, metric gaming, unsafe application, shadow tools, professional resistance, and reduced trust.

The desired end state is:

> **High capability, appropriate adoption, accurate reporting, protected expertise, trusted governance, and demonstrably safe business outcomes.**

---

# 1. Guiding principles

1. **Safety before speed.**
2. **Mandatory capability, not universal task-level usage.**
3. **Human accountability remains explicit.**
4. **Customer, policyholder, employee, and confidential business data receive proportionate protection.**
5. **Measure outcomes, competence, and control adherence—not clicks or prompt volume.**
6. **Responsible non-use is acceptable where risk or professional standards require it.**
7. **Good-faith questions, disclosures, and challenge are protected from retaliation.**
8. **Professional expertise is enhanced, not dismissed.**
9. **Global minimum controls apply everywhere; implementation may adapt locally.**
10. **Country and cultural differences are hypotheses to test, not stereotypes to apply.**
11. **Privacy and data minimization apply to both work content and employee telemetry.**
12. **Benefits, failures, workload effects, and workforce impacts are reported honestly.**

# 2. Mandate: mandatory requirements versus local choice

## 2.1 Mandatory globally

The following requirements apply to all in-scope employees and relevant third parties, subject to local legal review:

### Mandatory capability

- Complete assigned foundational and role-specific training.
- Pass a practical competence assessment where required.
- Understand permitted, restricted, and prohibited uses.
- Demonstrate knowledge of data protection, verification, human accountability, and incident reporting.

### Mandatory approved-tool use

- Use approved enterprise tools rather than public or personal tools for business activity.
- Use AI in workflows formally designated as mandatory by the relevant business owner, provided the workflow has been validated, controlled, and supported.
- Follow required documentation, approval, and human-review procedures.

### Mandatory responsible behavior

- Protect confidential, personal, health, financial, claims, employee, privileged, and regulated information.
- Verify material outputs and retain required records.
- Escalate suspected errors, bias, data exposure, unsafe use, or unclear policy.
- Stop or pause a workflow where there is credible risk of customer harm, privacy exposure, regulatory breach, or missing human oversight.

## 2.2 Discretionary or prohibited

Employees may choose not to use AI for tasks where it is unsuitable, including where:

- professional or legal standards require independent judgment;
- confidentiality or data restrictions prohibit use;
- output quality is inadequate;
- the task involves a high-impact decision without approved controls;
- accessibility or language limitations create unacceptable risk;
- human interaction or expertise is essential.

Use is prohibited where the global or local policy says so, including use of unapproved tools for restricted business data.

## 2.3 Exception and responsible non-use process

Each designated mandatory workflow must have:

- a documented rationale;
- a named business owner;
- a risk classification;
- minimum human-review requirements;
- an exception route;
- an escalation owner;
- an appeal or challenge process.

Employees may request an exception or alternative process for professional, legal, accessibility, medical, or other legitimate reasons. Managers may not treat approved non-use as resistance or poor performance.

# 3. Audience and adoption-pattern segmentation

Segmentation should use observed needs, role risk, workflow, trust, access, and sentiment. Country and culture may inform hypotheses but must not be used as a substitute for evidence.

| Audience or pattern | Typical concerns or behavior | Required response |
|---|---|---|
| Enthusiastic adopters | Seek speed, experimentation, and influence | Safe sandboxes, advanced training, validated use cases, peer roles, controls against overconfidence |
| Covert users | See value but fear scrutiny, job loss, reputational harm, or tool restrictions | Confidential advice, safe disclosure, clear telemetry rules, useful approved tools |
| Professional-pride resisters | Fear loss of judgment, craft, status, or autonomy | Expert co-design, explicit human-owned decisions, recognition of review and challenge |
| Privacy and security skeptics | Fear exposure of customer, health, claims, financial, or employee data | Specific data-flow explanations, technical controls, examples, independent assurance |
| Job-security skeptics | Fear redundancy, redeployment, or higher workload | Workforce-impact framework, consultation, reskilling, transparent role analysis |
| Pragmatic adopters | Will adopt when the tool solves a real problem | Role-specific use cases, workflow integration, local support |
| Low-confidence or digitally excluded employees | Face language, accessibility, infrastructure, or confidence barriers | Accessible training, coaching, protected time, alternative learning and access routes |
| High-risk functions | Require traceability, validation, and strict oversight | Risk-tiered controls, restricted features, audit evidence, mandatory human review |
| Managers | May face pressure to improve adoption numbers | Manager guidance, outcome-based performance rules, prohibition on informal targets |
| Control functions | Need assurance, evidence, and clear accountability | Approval standards, monitoring rights, incident ownership, independent review |
| Employee representatives | Focus on monitoring, job impact, fairness, and consultation rights | Early engagement, transparent measurement, documented consultation and responses |
| Contractors and outsourced teams | May have different access, training, and contractual protections | Equivalent controls, defined scope, access parity, contractual accountability |

### Required local diagnosis

Each major country, entity, and business should assess:

- trust in leadership and technology;
- perceived employment risk;
- professional identity concerns;
- tool quality and workflow fit;
- manager behavior;
- language and accessibility;
- privacy and data-sovereignty concerns;
- labor and consultation expectations;
- differences between reported and observed use.

Local plans should describe observable behaviors—for example, “employees are reluctant to challenge senior instructions”—rather than relying on cultural labels.

# 4. Interventions by adoption pattern

## 4.1 Enthusiastic adoption

- Provide approved “safe-to-try” environments.
- Publish validated use cases, prompt patterns, and workflow templates.
- Establish communities of practice across functions and countries.
- Recognize quality, customer outcomes, risk reduction, and knowledge sharing.
- Require the same data, human-review, and documentation controls for champions.
- Monitor whether enthusiasm is leading to overuse, skipped review, or unapproved tools.

## 4.2 Covert use and inaccurate reporting

Covert use should initially be treated as a signal of value combined with low trust, poor usability, or policy ambiguity—not automatically as misconduct.

Actions:

- Operate a time-limited safe-disclosure period for low-risk historical use.
- Provide confidential “is this allowed?” advice outside the direct reporting line.
- Publish a plain-language decision tree for permitted, restricted, and prohibited use.
- Explain what telemetry is collected, who can access it, retention periods, and prohibited uses.
- Prohibit managers from demanding screenshots, prompt histories, or informal proof unless a documented control requires it.
- Improve speed, features, language quality, integration, and availability of approved tools.
- Investigate gaps between self-report and telemetry as potential measurement or trust failures.
- Triage disclosures immediately where they involve sensitive data, customer impact, regulated decisions, or potential harm.

Good-faith disclosure of accidental, low-risk, or previously unclear use should normally lead to guidance and remediation rather than automatic discipline. Deliberate misuse involving material customer, privacy, security, financial, or regulatory harm remains subject to established investigation.

The safe-disclosure program must define its duration, eligible conduct, exclusions, confidentiality limits, legal-hold obligations, regulatory reporting requirements, and continuing non-punitive reporting route after the program ends.

## 4.3 Professional-pride resistance

- Involve respected claims, underwriting, actuarial, legal, compliance, medical, technology, and customer experts in use-case design.
- Define decisions and judgments that remain explicitly human-owned.
- Demonstrate failure modes, including hallucination, bias, false confidence, context loss, and inaccurate citations.
- Give professional communities authority to constrain or reject unsuitable uses.
- Recognize quality assurance, challenge, judgment, and responsible non-use.
- Protect junior employees’ learning and progression.
- Require peer review for high-impact AI-assisted work.

## 4.4 Privacy and security resistance

Provide concrete information on:

- processing locations;
- retention and deletion;
- vendor model-training practices;
- cross-border transfers;
- administrator and manager access;
- employee rights;
- incident handling.

Apply proportionate technical controls, including:

- data classification within the workflow;
- masking and tokenization;
- data-loss prevention warnings or blocking;
- encryption and tenant isolation;
- role-based access;
- retention and deletion controls;
- restricted export and sharing;
- vendor and model due diligence;
- privacy and security impact assessments;
- audit logs proportionate to risk.

## 4.5 Job-security resistance

Publish a workforce-impact framework covering:

- task augmentation;
- role redesign;
- redeployment;
- reskilling;
- workload changes;
- potential role reduction;
- decision criteria;
- consultation, notice, and review points.

The group should:

- provide protected reskilling time;
- report material workforce effects;
- create career paths in AI-enabled domain expertise, quality, assurance, and governance;
- monitor staffing, workload, overtime, absence, turnover, and burnout;
- avoid making AI activity an undisclosed criterion for promotion, pay, job security, or redundancy;
- consult employee representatives where required.

# 5. Global minimum standards versus local adaptation

## 5.1 Global minimum standards

The global center owns:

- approved tools, vendors, and versions;
- prohibited tools and use cases;
- data-classification rules;
- human-review standards;
- model-risk, privacy, security, and records requirements;
- training and competence standards;
- telemetry and dashboard rules;
- incident response;
- minimum accessibility and language standards;
- workforce and consultation principles;
- stop-use criteria.

## 5.2 Locally adaptable choices

Local teams may adapt:

- examples and terminology;
- language and learning format;
- training schedules;
- support channels and operating hours;
- consultation methods;
- local privacy notices;
- role-specific restrictions;
- deployment sequence within the global timetable;
- additional controls required by local law or professional standards.

Local adaptations must not weaken global minimum protections without documented central approval.

## 5.3 Country readiness review

Before launch, each entity should complete a documented review of:

- privacy and employee-monitoring requirements;
- cross-border data handling;
- automated-decision restrictions;
- insurance conduct and regulatory obligations;
- labor consultation, bargaining, or notice requirements;
- professional standards;
- accessibility and discrimination risks;
- records and retention;
- customer communication and appeal requirements;
- vendor contracting and security;
- business continuity.

# 6. Trust and privacy protections

## 6.1 Telemetry boundaries

Telemetry should be separated into tiers:

1. **Service administration:** access, availability, support, and security events.
2. **Aggregate adoption:** team, role, workflow, or country-level patterns.
3. **Control evidence:** information required for high-risk review or audit.
4. **Content-level data:** prompt or output content only where strictly necessary for a documented high-risk control.

For ordinary productivity assistance, content inspection should not be the default.

The group must:

- collect the minimum data necessary;
- publish a plain-language telemetry notice and data dictionary;
- state who can access which data;
- define retention and deletion;
- restrict use to documented purposes;
- provide employee correction and challenge rights;
- suppress small-cell reporting;
- audit actual dashboard use;
- prohibit informal manager surveillance.

Telemetry collected for service administration, safety, learning, or adoption support must not be repurposed for compensation, promotion, redundancy, performance, or disciplinary decisions without a new assessment, required consultation, governance approval, and revised employee notice.

## 6.2 Psychological safety and anti-retaliation

Provide:

- anonymous and confidential reporting;
- independent case ownership;
- good-faith disclosure protection;
- “stop-the-line” authority for unsafe use;
- manager training on skepticism and error reporting;
- monitoring for retaliation after reports;
- consequences for coercive manager behavior;
- feedback and closure to reporters where possible.

Employees should be able to challenge AI outputs, dashboard data, managerial pressure, and policy decisions without fear of reprisal.

## 6.3 Insurance-specific safeguards

Heightened controls apply to uses involving:

- claims assessment or settlement;
- underwriting, pricing, or risk selection;
- fraud indicators;
- customer eligibility or access;
- vulnerable customers;
- medical or health information;
- complaints and appeals;
- regulatory or financial reporting;
- employment decisions;
- legally privileged information;
- advice that may materially affect a policyholder.

Each material use case must document:

- purpose and expected benefit;
- affected customers and employees;
- data categories and flows;
- model, vendor, and version;
- testing and known limitations;
- accountable business owner;
- human reviewer and decision authority;
- override, appeal, and escalation routes;
- records to retain;
- language and accessibility performance;
- workforce impact;
- success and stop criteria.

Customer-facing uses must connect to existing complaints, redress, disclosure, and appeal processes.

# 7. Metrics and dashboard principles

## 7.1 Dashboard rules

For the first 12–18 months:

- do not use raw usage as an individual performance measure;
- do not rank employees by prompts, logins, messages, or time saved;
- report primarily at aggregate, role, workflow, or team level;
- separate learning analytics from disciplinary systems;
- require governance approval before any individual telemetry is used in an employment decision;
- audit for adverse impact, coercion, and gaming;
- prohibit local shadow targets;
- allow employees to challenge inaccurate data.

Adoption metrics may inform program support and governance. They may become operational requirements only for formally designated, validated workflows with fair comparisons and an exception process.

## 7.2 Balanced scorecard

### Readiness and reach

- access and suitable feature availability;
- training completion and practical competence;
- access parity by country, function, language, contract type, and accessibility need;
- protected learning time.

### Meaningful adoption

- use of approved tools in validated workflows;
- repeat use where appropriate;
- employee-reported usefulness;
- workflow integration;
- approved versus unauthorized tool use.

### Outcomes

- cycle time;
- quality and rework;
- customer outcomes and complaints;
- claims, underwriting, conduct, and financial-control outcomes;
- capacity and cost effects;
- employee workload and experience.

### Trust and safety

- privacy and security incidents;
- human-review compliance;
- error, correction, and override rates;
- trust and psychological-safety scores;
- safe-disclosure and escalation trends;
- evidence of manager coercion or metric gaming.

Every metric must have a definition, owner, data source, baseline, threshold, review frequency, limitation, comparison group, and escalation action. Claimed productivity benefits should use before-and-after analysis, control comparisons where practical, workload adjustment, and explicit attribution limitations.

# 8. Implementation roadmap

## Phase 0: Mobilize, define, and diagnose

- Establish executive sponsor and governance council.
- Define scope, including employees, contractors, outsourced teams, subsidiaries, and relevant intermediaries.
- Approve the three-tier mandate.
- Inventory tools, workflows, data flows, shadow use, and telemetry quality.
- Baseline trust, workload, professional concerns, job concerns, and psychological safety.
- Complete country legal, privacy, labor, and regulatory registers.
- Engage employee representatives.
- Define funding, staffing, technical dependencies, and launch criteria.

**Gate:** No broad launch until approved tools, access, controls, support, escalation, consultation, and exception processes are ready.

## Phase 1: Design and pilot

Pilot across:

- high- and low-reported-adoption locations;
- high- and low-risk functions;
- multiple languages;
- different accessibility needs;
- different labor and consultation environments;
- customer-facing and internal workflows.

Test:

- tool usability and workflow fit;
- competence;
- telemetry accuracy;
- safe disclosure;
- dashboard effects;
- quality and customer outcomes;
- workload;
- covert use;
- metric gaming;
- reporting confidence.

**Gate:** Scale only when safety, usability, access, support, trust, and outcome thresholds are met.

## Phase 2: Controlled global rollout

- Provide access and mandatory foundational training.
- Launch role-specific pathways, local champions, clinics, and manager support.
- Operate the safe-disclosure period.
- Introduce restricted, balanced dashboards.
- Monitor customer, privacy, security, conduct, quality, and employee outcomes frequently.
- Provide reasonable accommodations and alternative learning routes.

## Phase 3: Embed and optimize

- Integrate validated uses into workflows.
- Retire unsafe or low-value use cases.
- Refresh training and controls.
- Review model and vendor changes before continued use.
- Report workforce, workload, reskilling, and redeployment effects.
- Reassess any proposal to use telemetry in employment decisions.
- Maintain tested manual fallback for critical claims, customer, underwriting, and reporting processes.

# 9. Governance and accountability

## 9.1 Global governance council

Membership should include:

- business and operations;
- technology and security;
- privacy and legal;
- risk and compliance;
- HR and employee relations;
- model-risk specialists;
- customer and conduct functions;
- local implementation leads;
- professional and employee representatives where appropriate.

## 9.2 Core decision rights

| Decision | Accountable owner |
|---|---|
| Global policy and minimum controls | Global AI governance council |
| Tool and vendor approval | Technology, security, privacy, legal, and risk owners |
| High-impact use-case approval | Business owner with risk, privacy, legal, and model-risk approval |
| Mandatory workflow designation | Business owner, HR, risk, and employee-relations review |
| Individual telemetry access | Designated data governance owner |
| Employment use of telemetry | Executive governance approval and required consultation |
| Local adaptation | Country implementation panel within global guardrails |
| Exception or responsible non-use | Designated business and risk owner with employee appeal route |
| Incident response | Named incident lead with legal, privacy, security, risk, and business participation |
| Workflow suspension | Authorized risk or business owner under documented stop-use criteria |
| Customer remediation | Business owner with conduct, legal, and customer-service leadership |

## 9.3 Stop-use triggers

Suspend, restrict, or redesign a use case when there is:

- material privacy, security, or data-loss exposure;
- unacceptable customer, claims, underwriting, conduct, or financial harm;
- unexplained adverse impact;
- inadequate human review;
- excessive error or override rates;
- materially inaccurate telemetry;
- manager coercion or widespread metric gaming;
- inability to audit or explain the workflow;
- unresolved legal, regulatory, or consultation requirements;
- material language or accessibility disadvantage;
- tool outage or vendor change affecting safety.

# 10. Success measures

The rollout is successful when:

- employees complete required training and demonstrate competence;
- approved AI is used openly where it adds value;
- employees can choose responsible non-use where risk requires it;
- covert use and reporting gaps decline;
- managers assess outcomes, judgment, quality, and control adherence rather than activity counts;
- professional expertise remains respected;
- employees can report mistakes and challenge decisions safely;
- customer, privacy, regulatory, and quality outcomes remain stable or improve;
- workload and workforce impacts are monitored and addressed;
- access is equitable across countries, functions, languages, contract types, and accessibility needs;
- local differences are addressed through evidence rather than stereotypes;
- validated use cases produce measurable benefits without unacceptable harm.

Illustrative targets, to be confirmed after baseline measurement, may include:

- near-universal access or documented exception;
- high completion and competence rates;
- strong employee understanding of telemetry and privacy;
- sustained human-review compliance for high-risk workflows;
- no material increase in customer, privacy, security, conduct, or regulatory incidents;
- measurable improvement in selected quality, cycle-time, or employee-experience outcomes;
- documented reskilling or redeployment progress for materially affected roles.

# 11. Key risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Mandate drives superficial or unsafe use | Separate mandatory capability from validated workflow requirements; measure outcomes and controls |
| Employees conceal use | Safe disclosure, confidential advice, clear telemetry rules, better approved tools |
| Surveillance damages trust | Data minimization, aggregation, purpose limitation, access controls, correction rights |
| Professional expertise is devalued | Expert co-design, human-owned decisions, recognition of judgment |
| Job anxiety reduces performance | Transparent workforce framework, consultation, reskilling, workload monitoring |
| Sensitive data leakage | Classification, DLP, masking, tenant isolation, access controls, training, incident response |
| AI errors affect customers | Risk tiers, testing, substantive human review, records, appeals, stop-use authority |
| Local adaptation becomes inconsistent | Global minimum standards, local panels, central exception governance |
| Cultural assumptions distort intervention | Use behavioral evidence and test alternative explanations |
| Managers create shadow mandates | Explicit prohibition, manager training, audits, escalation, accountability |
| Adoption benefits are uneven | Monitor access, language, accessibility, role, contract, and location disparities |
| Vendor outage or model change disrupts work | Continuity plans, fallback processes, reapproval triggers, vendor exit planning |
| Productivity gains intensify workload | Track volume, quality, overtime, wellbeing, and staffing before changing targets |
| Metrics are gamed | No raw-count incentives, balanced scorecards, anomaly reviews, limited access |
| Critical workflows cannot continue during suspension | Tested manual fallback and business-continuity procedures |

## Executive decisions required

Senior leadership should approve:

1. The revised three-tier mandate.
2. The prohibition on raw usage as a standalone employment measure.
3. The global minimum control framework.
4. The exception and responsible non-use process.
5. The safe-disclosure and anti-retaliation approach.
6. The country-level legal, privacy, labor, and regulatory review model.
7. The global governance council and decision-rights structure.
8. Protected training time, local support, and implementation funding.
9. The balanced dashboard and purpose-limitation policy.
10. The workforce-impact, reskilling, and workload-monitoring framework.
11. The stop-use and business-continuity arrangements.
12. Independent review of trust, safety, customer outcomes, workforce effects, and disparate impacts.

The rollout should be judged not by maximum visible usage, but by whether employees are capable, supported, transparent, and safe in using AI—and whether the group protects customers, professional judgment, employee trust, and business performance while work changes.
