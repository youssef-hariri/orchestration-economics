# Global Faith-Based and Cultural Seasonal Communications Framework — 2026

## 1. Purpose and scope

This framework applies to campaigns connected to:

- Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, and Eid al-Adha
- Chinese New Year, Tết, Seollal, and other Lunar New Year observances
- Diwali, Deepavali, Bandi Chhor Divas, and other relevant regional or faith-specific observances

It covers advertising, packaging, promotions, retail, creators and influencers, PR, partnerships, experiential activity, employee communications, customer care, and social media.

The goal is not one global “festival campaign.” It is a recognizable brand platform expressed through specific, locally credible ideas.

The framework must ensure that the brand:

1. Defines the exact audience, market, language, and observance involved.
2. Avoids treating any faith, nation, region, ethnicity, or diaspora as internally uniform.
3. Gives the brand a credible and proportionate commercial role.
4. Separates cultural, religious, language, legal, certification, safety, and communications review.
5. Gives local teams authority to pause work.
6. Detects and addresses scrutiny quickly and fairly.
7. Documents decisions, disagreement, remediation, and learning.

---

# 2. Universal requirements and locally adaptable choices

## 2.1 Requirements for every market

The following are mandatory:

- specific market, audience, language, and observance definition;
- documented product role and commercial rationale;
- local cultural and language review;
- accurate, substantiated product and certification claims;
- market-specific legal and regulatory clearance;
- final-asset review, not only concept review;
- named approval owners and alternates;
- social, customer-care, and escalation readiness;
- media-context and adjacency review;
- safe and dignified representation;
- documented response and replacement plan for elevated-risk assets;
- post-campaign learning.

## 2.2 Choices that may vary locally

Local teams may adapt:

- story and creative premise;
- casting and household structure;
- language, dialect, greeting, and terminology;
- food, setting, wardrobe, music, and visual symbols;
- creator and partner selection;
- media channels, timing, and format;
- degree of religious or cultural specificity;
- retail and community activation;
- whether the brand addresses a faith audience, a broader public audience, or both.

Global consistency should generally reside in:

- brand identity;
- product truth;
- ethical and safety standards;
- core business objective;
- required legal information;
- agreed brand promise.

It should not require identical cultural imagery, scripts, symbols, or rituals.

# 3. Operating principles

## 3.1 Respect before relevance

A seasonal reference is not appropriate merely because it is popular or commercially valuable.

**Test:** If the reference were removed and the idea remained substantially unchanged, it may be decorative rather than meaningful.

The brand must not use faith or culture as a visual shortcut, sales trigger, or claim of spiritual authority.

## 3.2 Specificity over generic representation

Every asset must identify:

- the country or market;
- the intended community or audience;
- the observance or tradition;
- the language and register;
- the household, social, or consumption context.

“Muslim,” “Asian,” “Indian,” “Chinese,” “North African,” “Gulf,” and “Southeast Asian” are planning categories, not sufficient creative audiences.

## 3.3 Local evidence, not a single approval

Authenticity must be supported by evidence showing:

- what local insight informed the work;
- what practices or symbols are represented;
- what varies within the audience;
- which audiences are outside scope;
- who reviewed the work and their relevant expertise;
- what advice changed the work;
- why unresolved advice was accepted or rejected.

No individual advisor, employee, agency, or community partner can certify an asset for an entire population.

## 3.4 Commercial proportionality

Before proceeding, ask:

- Is the product genuinely used in the depicted occasion?
- Does it provide utility, convenience, access, or a verified community benefit?
- Would the idea remain respectful if people did not purchase?
- Does the campaign imply that purchase is part of religious duty or cultural belonging?
- Is the brand borrowing significance it has not earned?

## 3.5 Inclusion without tokenism

Representation should give people agency rather than use them decoratively.

Review whether:

- people prepare, host, decide, speak, and participate;
- workers, migrants, disabled people, older people, and children are treated with dignity;
- household diversity is plausible for the market;
- inclusion creates avoidable safety or legal risk;
- one household type is presented as morally or culturally superior.

Not every asset must include every demographic. The campaign portfolio should, however, avoid systematic exclusion.

## 3.6 Consultation must influence decisions

Consultation is not complete because a meeting occurred or an advisor approved the final asset. Material advice must be recorded, resolved, and linked to final approval.

## 3.7 Accuracy and engagement are different

Reach, sales, and positive sentiment do not prove cultural accuracy. Automated sentiment tools may misread sarcasm, dialect, transliteration, code-switching, and religious vocabulary. Material issues require human review.

## 3.8 Accountable moderation

Good-faith criticism should remain visible. Threats, hate speech, doxxing, targeted harassment, impersonation, and spam may be removed under a published moderation policy. Criticism should not be mass-deleted or dismissed simply because it is inconvenient.

# 4. Campaign architecture

## 4.1 Global platform

Owned by the Global Brand Team:

- business objective;
- brand role;
- broad human need;
- product and brand truths;
- global ethical, legal, and safety standards;
- prohibited territory;
- shared measurement approach;
- resources for research, consultation, and response readiness.

**Example**

- Platform: “Make room for togetherness.”
- Brand role: Help people share food more easily during meaningful seasonal moments.
- Guardrail: Do not imply that all households, meals, or observances are the same.

## 4.2 Regional interpretation

Owned jointly by regional marketing, regional agencies, and relevant market reviewers:

- market differences;
- audience segments;
- local occasion insight;
- product role;
- terminology and language;
- casting and creator strategy;
- media context;
- community, retail, or charitable partnerships;
- regional risk controls.

## 4.3 Market execution

Owned by the country team and local agency:

- final concept and script;
- talent, creators, locations, and production;
- food, wardrobe, props, and visual details;
- packaging and point of sale;
- local claims and disclosures;
- publication and media timing;
- customer care and social response;
- market-level go/no-go decision.

# 5. Planning and approval gates

## Gate 1: Occasion and Audience Charter

Before briefing an agency, document:

1. Exact observance and preferred public terminology.
2. Countries, languages, communities, and audience segments in scope.
3. Audiences and practices outside scope.
4. Brand role and product relevance.
5. Cultural, religious, legal, certification, safety, privacy, and political sensitivities.
6. Required expertise.
7. Whether separate market executions are needed.
8. Contribution or partnership plans.
9. Local date ranges and timing uncertainties.
10. Asset-level risk rating.
11. Safety risks for talent, creators, employees, beneficiaries, and consumers.
12. How affected assets can be paused or replaced.

For Ramadan and Eid, dates must be managed as local ranges and confirmed through current local sources. The charter should distinguish pre-Ramadan, Ramadan, the final ten nights, Eid preparation, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, and post-Eid where relevant.

## Gate 2: Cultural discovery

Use appropriate combinations of:

- local qualitative research;
- customer and community interviews;
- social listening;
- search and trend data;
- retail observation;
- agency and creator expertise;
- ethnographic or diary research;
- language specialists;
- qualified religious or certification experts;
- food-safety, accessibility, child-safety, or safeguarding specialists.

The **Cultural Insight and Guardrails Brief** must identify:

- shared behaviors and meaningful variation;
- local language and register;
- credible behaviors to depict;
- high-risk practices or symbols;
- socioeconomic and household realities;
- representation and safety considerations;
- relevant media contexts;
- rejected or unsuitable creative territory.

“Local” must be granular. For example:

- UAE should not automatically stand for all Gulf markets.
- Morocco should not automatically stand for all North Africa.
- Indonesia should not automatically stand for Malaysia or Singapore.
- India should be segmented where language, food, ritual, or regional identity affects the work.
- Chinese New Year, Tết, and Seollal should be planned separately.
- Muslim-minority, migrant, expatriate, and diaspora audiences may require different approaches from Muslim-majority or origin-country audiences.

## Gate 3: Creative development and testing

Agencies should develop routes that differ in cultural premise, not merely color or translation.

Each route must state:

- intended market and audience;
- exact observance;
- local insight;
- product role;
- representation choices;
- terminology and language;
- consultation completed;
- assumptions and unresolved risks;
- whether and how the idea can travel.

Testing should ask:

- What do people think is happening?
- Which community does the asset appear to represent?
- Is the product role credible?
- What feels inaccurate, stereotyped, or exploitative?
- Does the wording imply universal observance?
- Would the work remain respectful without a purchase?

## Gate 4: Concept approval

The panel should include, as relevant:

- global brand;
- regional marketing;
- market lead;
- local and regional agency;
- legal and regulatory;
- communications and corporate affairs;
- social and customer care;
- market-specific cultural and language reviewers;
- religious, certification, food-safety, accessibility, child-safety, or safeguarding specialists.

Approval requires:

- a defined audience and observance;
- credible product relevance;
- documented risk controls;
- appropriate local adaptation;
- safe and plausible representation;
- identified final approvers.

## Gate 5: Pre-production review

Review:

- scripts, storyboards, copy, and translations;
- greetings, pronunciation, transliteration, and typography;
- casting, creator history, and safety;
- wardrobe, food, music, choreography, and locations;
- prayer, worship, scripture, religious objects, or sacred spaces;
- household and gender dynamics;
- donation and community claims;
- media adjacency;
- labor, health, child-safety, and safeguarding conditions.

Do not require actors to simulate fasting, prayer, or religious behavior without a clear justification, informed consent, and specialist review. Production must protect fasting and non-fasting staff equally.

## Gate 6: Localization and final QA

Every market version requires named approval for:

- language, dialect, and register;
- pronunciation and subtitles;
- script, calligraphy, and layout;
- claims and disclosures;
- cultural plausibility;
- food and product presentation;
- accessibility;
- media context;
- platform formatting.

Each asset should have a **Market Language Sheet** containing preferred terminology, greeting, register, pronunciation audio, prohibited or ambiguous terms, typography requirements, and named primary and backup approvers.

## Gate 7: Release readiness

No asset may publish without a record containing:

- asset and market risk ID;
- final asset;
- named market and brand approvers;
- cultural and language sign-off;
- legal and regulatory clearance;
- claims and certification evidence;
- talent, music, and location rights;
- media-adjacency review;
- moderation and customer-care plans;
- escalation contacts;
- response templates;
- paid-media pause authority;
- replacement asset where appropriate;
- exceptions and rationale.

## Gate 8: Live monitoring and learning

Monitoring begins during concept testing or when creative, casting, creators, or partnerships become public or semi-public. It continues through launch, retail, paid and organic distribution, local peak periods, press coverage, and post-campaign reposting.

Within 30 days, complete a review of performance, cultural resonance, criticism, response quality, consultation impact, language and claims errors, partnership outcomes, and process improvements.

# 6. Risk model

Risk is assessed at asset level.

| Level | Typical content | Required controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | Ordinary hosting, sharing, gifting, or product utility without sacred content | Market, language, legal, claims, social, and customer-care review |
| **Elevated** | Explicit seasonal references, local food traditions, cultural dress, creators, certification, authenticity claims, or cross-market adaptation | At least two relevant perspectives; documented consultation; regional review; final-asset and media-context review |
| **Exceptional** | Prayer, scripture, sacred spaces, religious leaders, ritual simulation, vulnerable groups, historical or political symbols, charitable activity involving beneficiaries, or high-risk cross-border master assets | Independent reviewer; qualified specialists; audience testing; global, regional, market, legal, and communications approval; crisis and replacement plan |

## Red-line triggers

Pause and redesign when:

- the idea depends on a stereotype;
- sacred material is decorative, comedic, or commercially exploited;
- a credible factual or religious error remains unresolved;
- the product role is forced;
- the asset implies universal observance;
- “authentic,” “traditional,” “halal,” “pure,” “healthy,” or similar claims are unsupported;
- translation strengthens or changes the claim;
- representation creates avoidable legal or social risk;
- consultation occurred only after creative lock;
- the brand cannot explain why a symbol, behavior, or setting is appropriate.

# 7. Occasion-specific safeguards

## 7.1 Ramadan and Eid

Ramadan differs by country, city, climate, work pattern, observance, household, class, and public context. Do not treat iftar, suhoor, prayer, or family gatherings as universal set pieces.

### Market and audience review

Separate relevant markets such as Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, Oman, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt, Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei, Singapore, and southern Thailand. Review national identity, dialect, migrant and expatriate populations, class, food practice, urban and rural context, and public-behavior expectations separately.

Also consider Muslim-minority and diaspora markets, where Ramadan may have different public visibility, media timing, workplace dynamics, retail patterns, and safety considerations.

### Timing and media

- Confirm dates and public announcements locally.
- Use market-specific launch times.
- Review food advertising during fasting hours.
- Review adjacency to religious programming and prayer-related content.
- Avoid excessive promotional pressure during sensitive observance periods, including the final ten nights.
- Check whether religious or ethnic targeting is permitted under privacy, advertising, and platform rules.

### Fasting and iftar depictions

Food shown before sunset must have clear context, such as preparation, a non-fasting character, travel, illness, or another legitimate circumstance. Do not use hunger as a joke or depict fasting as a consumer problem.

Confirm whether characters are fasting, exempt, or not observing. Protect actors and crews from coercion or unsafe working conditions.

### Sacred content

Prayer, Qur’anic text, mosques, religious leaders, religious blessings, and ritual acts require separate language, religious, cultural, legal, and production review. Do not assume all Muslim audiences share one view on music, dress, gender interaction, photography, or humor.

Sacred content is not automatically prohibited, but it must be necessary, accurate, respectful, and specifically justified.

### Halal claims

For each halal claim, document:

- applicable certification authority;
- current certificate;
- permitted logo and wording;
- ingredient, facility, and manufacturing coverage;
- import documentation;
- whether the claim applies to the product, recipe, facility, or serving context;
- required local or retailer approval.

“Halal-friendly” and “suitable for iftar” are not substitutes for substantiation.

### Eid

Specify Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, or another observance. Review prayer, visiting, travel, clothing, gifting, and meals separately. Do not carry fasting, deprivation, or “reward after restraint” messaging into Eid without review.

## 7.2 Lunar New Year

“Lunar New Year” is an internal umbrella term. The public term should reflect the relevant audience, such as Chinese New Year, Spring Festival, Tết, or Seollal.

Review:

- calendar and date cutoffs;
- relevant zodiac system;
- greetings and honorifics;
- simplified or traditional characters;
- calligraphy direction and accuracy;
- pronunciation and diacritics;
- colors, numbers, homophones, gifts, and symbols;
- reunion, workplace, gifting, ancestor, or religious context;
- diaspora and second-generation audiences.

Do not use Chinese symbols as generic representation for Vietnamese, Korean, or other audiences. Ancestor offerings, altars, and ritual objects require specialist review and must not be used as decoration.

## 7.3 Diwali and Deepavali

Specify the relevant community, region, language, and observance. Diwali, Deepavali, Bandi Chhor Divas, Hindu, Jain, Sikh, and other traditions must not be collapsed into one ritual template.

Review:

- regional food, dress, language, and household practices;
- preferred name and spelling;
- lamps, rangoli, gifting, prayer, and decoration;
- deities, temples, scripture, and puja;
- fireworks, environmental, animal-welfare, air-quality, safety, and regulatory concerns;
- class and household diversity.

Do not use lamps, rangoli, sweets, fireworks, luxury, and shopping as a checklist. Do not place products on sacred designs or use religious imagery as decoration. Avoid implying that spending, wealth, large homes, or fireworks define a meaningful celebration.

# 8. Consultation and representation safeguards

## 8.1 Required expertise

Depending on the asset, involve:

- market-specific cultural leads;
- native-language and pronunciation specialists;
- qualified religious or faith specialists;
- halal or other certification experts;
- food-safety and claims specialists;
- accessibility, child-safety, and safeguarding experts;
- local retailers or distributors;
- migrant, expatriate, or diaspora representatives;
- community organizations;
- creator and talent-safety specialists.

Advisors should be selected for relevance to the exact market and audience. They should be paid fairly, protected from pressure, and not publicly described as endorsing authenticity without consent.

## 8.2 Consultation disposition log

| Issue raised | Relevant expertise | Risk identified | Team response | Change or reason for rejection | Final approver | Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

For elevated content, seek at least two relevant perspectives. For exceptional content, include an independent reviewer not appointed by the creative agency.

## 8.3 Representation safety

Before casting or publishing, ask:

- Could the representation expose talent, creators, employees, or consumers to avoidable risk?
- Is identity being shown safely and with informed consent?
- Are workers, beneficiaries, children, older people, and disabled people given agency?
- Does the story avoid presenting one household type as superior?
- Are public activations safe in the local legal and social context?

## 8.4 Cultural ownership and partnerships

Check permissions, compensation, community ownership, creator safety, conflict of interest, and whether the brand receives disproportionate commercial benefit from sacred or community-owned material.

Community contributions must include partner due diligence, verified amounts, beneficiary consent, safeguarding, geographic scope, and clear separation from any implication that purchase is a religious duty.

---

# 9. Agency collaboration expectations

Global, regional, and local agencies must:

- involve local expertise before concept lock;
- develop market-specific ideas where cultural premises differ;
- document assumptions, evidence, and unresolved risks;
- distinguish translation from transcreation;
- disclose agency-appointed advisors and potential conflicts;
- provide final-asset review in every market;
- build modular assets and pre-approved alternatives;
- avoid generic cultural shorthand and tokenistic casting;
- maintain creator, talent, and production safety plans;
- rework the idea when a market needs a different premise, not merely a different language.

The Global Creative Agency must not treat a global master asset as the default where it contains high-context religious or cultural content.

Regional teams should maintain a paid roster of qualified reviewers, translators, religious specialists, certification experts, and monitoring resources so smaller markets can meet requirements without unnecessary delay.

---

# 10. Governance and decision rights

## 10.1 Core responsibilities

**Global Brand:** standards, platform, risk taxonomy, funding, shared tools, cross-border coordination, and learning.

**Regional Marketing:** segmentation, agency coordination, regional review, global-local conflict resolution, and cross-market escalation.

**Market Lead:** local brief, local approvals, publication, customer care, and market-level go/no-go. The Market Lead or delegate may immediately pause paid and scheduled organic activity for credible cultural, legal, safety, or certification concerns.

**Legal and Regulatory:** claims, food, labeling, promotion, privacy, certification, platform, and local regulatory compliance.

**Communications and Corporate Affairs:** reputational assessment, media and stakeholder engagement, employee communications, and crisis coordination.

**Social and Customer Care:** monitoring, issue classification, moderation, responses, logging, and escalation.

**Agencies:** local insight, creative development, execution, risk disclosure, and correction of inaccurate or stereotyped work.

## 10.2 Immediate stop rights

The following may pause affected activity immediately within their scope:

- Market Lead or delegate: local paid and scheduled organic activity;
- Social Lead: scheduled social publishing;
- Legal: content with legal, food-safety, privacy, or certification exposure;
- Communications Lead: content creating material reputational or safety risk;
- Regional Lead: shared regional assets;
- Global Crisis Lead: cross-border or enterprise-wide activity.

Stopping activity does not require prior executive permission. The decision and rationale must be escalated afterward.

## 10.3 Disagreement protocol

1. Document the concern, affected audience, evidence, and likely interpretation.
2. Document the business, brand, or consistency requirement.
3. Convene the relevant regional, global, legal, communications, cultural, and language owners.
4. Consider local adaptation, modular assets, or removal of the risky element.
5. If unresolved, the accountable regional or global marketing director decides.
6. Log dissent, rationale, and follow-up action.

A local objection must not be dismissed as excessive conservatism; a global concern must not be dismissed as lack of local understanding.

# 11. Social listening and escalation

## 11.1 Monitoring

Monitor local-language and transliterated variants of:

- brand, product, campaign, creator, and partner names;
- occasion names and greetings;
- terms associated with offense, disrespect, stereotyping, appropriation, mistranslation, boycott, halal, certification, and inaccuracy;
- screenshots, edited clips, reaction videos, memes, reposts, press, employee posts, retailer content, and creator commentary.

Monitoring begins during testing or early seeding and continues through post-campaign circulation. Coverage must specify market hours, handoffs, weekend coverage, and alternates.

## 11.2 Issue classification

Classify issues as:

- factual or visual error;
- religious or theological concern;
- cultural stereotyping;
- language or pronunciation problem;
- food, halal, health, or certification claim;
- political, ethnic, or historical sensitivity;
- community-partnership concern;
- general dislike;
- misinformation;
- abuse, hate speech, threat, or doxxing.

“Coordinated” or “inauthentic” criticism requires evidence. A small number of affected-community voices may be more relevant than a larger volume of general commentary.

## 11.3 Response targets

Targets should be scaled by market capability and risk, but the operating expectation is:

- **15 minutes:** alert named owners for priority issues;
- **30 minutes:** preserve content and begin classification;
- **60 minutes:** decide whether to pause paid or scheduled activity;
- **90 minutes:** convene the relevant incident team;
- **2 hours:** issue a holding response where silence could increase harm;
- **4 hours:** decide on correction, replacement, withdrawal, or continued monitoring;
- **8 hours:** publish a substantive response or document the reason for delay;
- **24 hours:** complete cross-market review where relevant.

A holding statement may be issued before all facts are confirmed. A substantive apology, claim, or correction requires verified information.

## 11.4 Issue tiers

**Tier 0 — Routine:** questions, availability, isolated dislike. Customer Care responds and logs themes.

**Tier 1 — Credible limited concern:** language, food, visual, or stereotyping issue. Consult the relevant reviewer, correct where appropriate, and notify regional owners if the asset travels.

**Tier 2 — Significant reputational issue:** sustained affected-community criticism, creator or media amplification, inaccurate religious depiction, misleading claim, or cross-market spread. Pause paid amplification, convene the incident team, prepare local-language communication, and correct, replace, or withdraw as appropriate.

**Tier 3 — Crisis:** desecration or mockery, discrimination, serious food-safety or certification issue, regulatory involvement, major boycott, or threats. Activate crisis governance, stop affected distribution, preserve records, appoint one spokesperson, coordinate markets, and assess corrective remedies.

## 11.5 Moderation

- Preserve good-faith criticism.
- Remove threats, hate speech, doxxing, targeted harassment, impersonation, and spam under the published policy.
- Do not mass-block affected-community critics.
- Preserve removed content where legally permissible.
- Do not disable comments solely to avoid scrutiny.
- Brief moderators on local-language nuance and escalation rules.

# 12. Response guidance

## Holding statement

> We are aware of concerns about [specific asset or element]. We are reviewing the content with our [market] team and relevant language, cultural, religious, or technical specialists. We will share an update once the facts and appropriate action are confirmed.

## Correction and apology

> We got this wrong. The [language/visual/ritual/claim] in our content did not accurately reflect [specific market or community], and we understand why this caused concern. We have [paused/replaced/updated] the affected content and are reviewing our process with qualified local experts.

## Claim clarification

> Our earlier wording was unclear. In [market], [product] is [precise substantiated claim], supported by [certificate or documentation]. It does not mean [unsupported implication]. We have updated the content to make this distinction clear.

## Interpretive concern

> We understand that people within [community] may hold different views about this depiction. Our intention was [specific intention], but we recognize that the execution may have suggested [problematic interpretation]. We are reviewing whether the asset is appropriate for this audience and will act accordingly.

Do not blame agencies, translators, creators, or local teams publicly. Do not argue with affected communities about whether they are entitled to object.

---

# 13. Community and charitable activity

Before announcing a partnership or contribution, document:

- partner due diligence;
- beneficiary and geographic scope;
- verified amount or activity;
- delivery mechanism and timing;
- consent for filming and photography;
- safeguarding;
- disclosure of paid relationships;
- separation from purchase requirements;
- whether the brand is implying religious duty or moral reward.

Do not use poverty, hunger, refugees, migrant workers, beneficiaries, or religious communities as emotional props. Contributions should not be presented as a substitute for accurate representation or as an automatic remedy for criticism.

---

# 14. Measurement

## Business and media

- awareness and consideration;
- reach and frequency;
- product trial and sales;
- retail performance;
- creator and earned-media effectiveness;
- production efficiency.

## Cultural resonance

Measure by market and relevant audience segment:

- perceived respect;
- relevance;
- accuracy;
- authenticity;
- inclusion;
- credibility of product role;
- clarity of language;
- commercial proportionality;
- audience interpretation.

## Risk and accountability

- time to detect, classify, acknowledge, decide, and resolve;
- complaints by issue type and market;
- corrections, replacements, pauses, and removals;
- claims and language errors;
- consultation recommendations adopted;
- unresolved concerns at launch;
- creator, employee, retailer, and customer-care feedback;
- moderation decisions;
- effectiveness of replacement assets;
- completion of remediation actions.

Success is not zero criticism. Success means the brand creates work that is specific and respectful, detects concerns fairly, responds proportionately, and improves its practice.

# 15. Practical operating calendar

## 20–24 weeks before launch

- Confirm markets and observances.
- Complete the Occasion and Audience Charter.
- Assign owners, alternates, and stop authority.
- Begin research, consultation, and listening.
- Establish asset-level risk IDs.

## 14–20 weeks before launch

- Complete cultural guardrails.
- Develop market-specific routes.
- Identify language, religious, certification, safety, and accessibility specialists.
- Begin claims and partnership due diligence.

## 10–14 weeks before launch

- Test audience interpretation.
- Approve concepts.
- Confirm whether separate executions are required.
- Confirm date ranges and timing assumptions.
- Prepare modular and replacement assets.

## 6–10 weeks before launch

- Complete consultation logs.
- Lock scripts, casting, creators, and locations.
- Complete claims, certification, rights, safeguarding, and media review.
- Train social and customer-care teams.

## 3–6 weeks before launch

- Produce assets.
- Complete cultural, language, legal, religious, accessibility, and claims QA.
- Run response simulations.
- Confirm monitoring and handoffs.

## One week before launch

- Complete named final approvals.
- Confirm local dates, publishing schedules, media adjacency, and escalation coverage.
- Verify replacement assets and local-language statements.

## During campaign

- Monitor teaser, launch, retail, paid, organic, creator, employee, and press activity.
- Hold formal issue reviews during peak periods.
- Log decisions and material changes.
- Re-review any change affecting cultural meaning, claims, safety, or representation.

## Within 30 days after campaign

- Conduct performance and cultural review.
- Debrief agencies, advisors, creators, customer care, retailers, and partners.
- Complete root-cause analysis for material incidents.
- Assign owners and deadlines for improvements.
- Update the learning library and “do not reuse” records.

# 16. Implementation standard

Every campaign must be able to answer:

1. Which exact market, community, language, and observance are represented?
2. Which audiences and practices are outside scope?
3. What evidence establishes local relevance?
4. Who is qualified to assess the cultural, religious, and language elements?
5. What is the brand’s legitimate and proportionate role?
6. What legal, certification, privacy, safety, and media-context risks exist?
7. What advice was received and how was it resolved?
8. Which assets require enhanced review?
9. Who can approve, pause, replace, remove, apologize, or correct?
10. How will scrutiny be detected and assessed within hours?
11. What remedy will be used if the brand is wrong?
12. What learning and accountability will carry forward?

Authenticity is not a visual style or a single local sign-off. It is established through precise audience definition, credible local participation, specialist review where necessary, commercial restraint, safe representation, rapid accountability, and documented learning.
