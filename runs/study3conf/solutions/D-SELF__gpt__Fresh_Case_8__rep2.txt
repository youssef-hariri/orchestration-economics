# CBAM Supplier Partnership Strategy  
## Steel and Aluminum Supply from India, Turkey, and North Africa | August 2026–2028

## Executive recommendation

Establish a **segmented, time-bound CBAM Supplier Partnership Program** combining firm compliance controls with targeted technical, financial, commercial, and relationship support.

The buyer should:

1. Confirm the responsible EU importer, CBAM declarant, customs representative, and certificate administrator for every import flow.
2. Require installation-, product-, process-, precursor-, and reporting-period-specific emissions information under contract.
3. Segment facilities by data readiness, emissions exposure, strategic criticality, financial capacity, supply-chain complexity, and relationship health—not by country alone.
4. Treat missing or defective information for 2026 imports as an immediate compliance and financial-exposure issue.
5. Fund metering, data systems, assurance, and selected abatement projects where they reduce avoidable certificate exposure or protect strategically important supply.
6. Allocate recurring economic costs according to causation, controllability, transparency, and contractual responsibility. Use ability to pay to determine support or financing, not the allocation of inherent carbon costs.
7. Reward accepted data, approved emissions reductions, investment delivery, quality, and reliable supply through volume visibility, financing access, premiums, or preferred-supplier status.
8. Protect continuity through alternative-source qualification, inventory measures, route flexibility, and defined remediation deadlines.
9. Recognize suppliers’ concerns that CBAM may be protectionist or unfair without requiring them to endorse the buyer’s policy position.
10. Make binding implementation conditional on current EU and local legal, customs, tax, accounting, competition, data, financing, and contract reviews.

The objective is not to eliminate suppliers’ CBAM exposure or provide indefinite reimbursement. It is to reduce avoidable certificate cost, preserve EU market access, improve supplier competitiveness, and maintain a resilient lower-emissions supply base.

> **Regulatory caveat:** This is a commercial and control framework, not a legal opinion. CBAM scope, emissions methodology, verification, certificate administration, deductions, customs treatment, and importer responsibility must be confirmed for each product, reporting period, installation, import flow, and legal entity using current official EU guidance and applicable local law.

## 1. Partnership principles

### Firm on compliance, collaborative on implementation

The buyer should communicate:

> “CBAM is an EU legal requirement and we must comply. We recognize that its effects outside the EU may be viewed as unfair or protectionist. We will not ask you to adopt a particular political judgment. We will work with you to produce reliable data, reduce avoidable cost, improve efficiency, and preserve your access to our market. We will be transparent about cost allocation, protect confidential information, and reward measurable progress.”

The program should separate:

- **Legal compliance:** mandatory and non-negotiable.
- **Capability building:** supportable through training, technical assistance, and financing.
- **Economic allocation:** negotiable within legal, tax, accounting, competition, and commercial constraints.

Fairness does not require identical treatment. Facilities with greater readiness gaps, strategic importance, financial constraints, or credible abatement opportunities may appropriately receive more support, provided the criteria are published, documented, and consistently applied.

Competitiveness should be assessed using total landed cost and supply performance, including price, certificate exposure, quality, delivery, logistics, qualification time, working capital, emissions trajectory, financing needs, and concentration risk.

## 2. Supplier segmentation

Segmentation should be performed at installation level and refreshed annually or after material changes in ownership, product, route, energy source, or production process.

| Segment | Typical profile | Partnership response |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Accepted-data and collaborative** | Reliable facility-level data and credible reduction plan | Fast-track data acceptance, selective multi-year volume visibility, financing access, performance incentives |
| **B. Capable but data-incomplete** | Operational capability exists, but electricity, precursor, allocation, or evidence gaps remain | Data-gap assessment, templates, metering, pre-verification, 6–12 month remediation |
| **C. Constrained but strategic** | Limited systems or capital, but important volume or difficult qualification | Intensive technical support, targeted grants or guarantees, senior governance, continuity planning |
| **D. High-emissions or structurally exposed** | High expected CBAM cost and limited near-term abatement | Route and product scenarios, efficiency measures, substitution, transition milestones, alternatives |
| **E. Fairness-sensitive or relationship-fragile** | CBAM viewed as protectionist or data requests as intrusive | Senior listening, transparent economics, independent review, data safeguards, cure periods |
| **F. Persistently unreliable** | Refusal, material misrepresentation, or repeated failure to remediate | Formal escalation, controlled default treatment, reduced new allocation, backup sourcing, possible exit |

Each facility should be assessed on:

- technical readiness and metering;
- data completeness and traceability;
- emissions intensity and expected certificate exposure;
- abatement potential;
- commercial criticality and substitutability;
- financial capacity;
- precursor and subcontracting complexity; and
- relationship health and fairness concerns.

A facility may fall into more than one segment. Technical constraints and trust concerns should be managed separately.

### Remediation limits

High-risk suppliers should normally have no more than 12 months to achieve basic data readiness. Extensions require:

- a funded action plan;
- interim data controls;
- a named executive owner;
- a maximum extension period;
- a final retain-or-replace decision date; and
- a backup-supply plan.

Criticality must not justify indefinite reliance on unsupported data.

## 3. Supplier support offerings

### 3.1 Service tiers

| Tier | Eligibility | Support |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | Low exposure or readily replaceable suppliers | Templates, training, helpdesk, regulatory guidance |
| **Priority** | Material data gap or moderate continuity risk | Data-gap assessment, metering, pre-verification, local consultancy |
| **Strategic** | High exposure, critical supply, or major abatement opportunity | Financing, guarantees, senior governance, investment support, alternative-source planning |

Eligibility should consider import volume, certificate sensitivity, criticality, substitutability, qualification lead time, readiness gap, and expected return on support.

### 3.2 Data and technical support

Provide a common data standard mapped to current CBAM requirements, with product-specific annexes. Each product record should link:

- CN code and customs declaration;
- importer and declarant;
- exporting installation;
- production route and period;
- product grade and quantity;
- direct emissions;
- electricity and fuel data;
- precursors and semifinished inputs;
- allocation methodology;
- calculation version;
- supporting evidence; and
- assurance or verification status.

Use defined data categories:

- actual and accepted;
- actual awaiting assurance;
- estimated;
- default;
- rejected; and
- under remediation.

“Actual” must not be treated as equivalent to “accepted.”

Support should include:

- standard calculation workbooks;
- secure supplier portals and interfaces;
- metering and submetering;
- production and energy reconciliation;
- local technical consultants;
- evidence-retention procedures;
- data-quality clinics; and
- local-language materials.

### 3.3 Steel and aluminum controls

For steel, capture relevant information on:

- blast-furnace/basic oxygen furnace, electric-arc furnace, direct-reduced iron, and other routes;
- coal, coke, gas, oxygen, process gases, and captive utilities;
- scrap content and origin;
- slabs, billets, blooms, pig iron, DRI, ferroalloys, and other precursors;
- shared furnaces, rolling mills, finishing lines, and tolling.

For aluminum, capture relevant information on:

- alumina and other precursors;
- primary and secondary production;
- smelting electricity;
- anode and process emissions;
- remelting and scrap;
- purchased billets, slabs, and semifinished inputs;
- allocation among alloys and grades; and
- captive, open-access, purchased, and contracted electricity.

### 3.4 Indirect-emissions and assurance controls

Approve a product-by-product matrix covering:

- whether indirect emissions are included;
- the applicable calculation method;
- acceptable electricity evidence;
- treatment of captive, open-access, purchased, and renewable electricity;
- treatment of certificates, guarantees, and contractual instruments; and
- responsible data provider.

No price incentive, deduction, or low-carbon claim should rely on a power arrangement until the applicable methodology and evidence have been approved.

Use a proportionate assurance ladder:

1. supplier self-check;
2. buyer plausibility review;
3. independent pre-verification;
4. formal verification where legally required;
5. corrective-action closure; and
6. annual revalidation after material process changes.

Buyer-required assurance should be identified as buyer policy unless confirmed as a legal requirement.

### 3.5 Precursor governance

Finished-product suppliers should:

- identify relevant precursor suppliers and production locations;
- obtain required emissions and traceability information;
- use approved declaration formats;
- retain supporting evidence;
- notify the buyer when upstream data are unavailable; and
- use estimates or defaults only through an approved escalation process.

Direct buyer engagement with precursor suppliers should occur only where necessary, commercially justified, and protected by confidentiality arrangements.

### 3.6 Training and financing

Establish a Supplier CBAM Academy covering:

- current requirements;
- steel and aluminum emissions boundaries;
- electricity and precursor accounting;
- allocation and traceability;
- assurance preparation;
- carbon-cost economics;
- efficiency and power-procurement options;
- financing proposals;
- contract rights; and
- data confidentiality.

Potential support mechanisms include:

- baseline studies;
- grants for meters, sensors, software, and laboratory work;
- co-funded efficiency projects;
- supply-chain finance;
- guarantees;
- blended finance;
- advance purchase commitments; and
- selective volume commitments.

Every project should have a validated baseline, measurement plan, business case, funding cap, milestones, defined asset and data rights, and continuing compliance monitoring. Public or multilateral finance requires separate reviews for eligibility, subsidy, procurement, sanctions, anti-bribery, environmental and social safeguards, local-content requirements, and double financing.

## 4. Regional considerations

### India

Prioritize facility-level validation of:

- integrated, electric-arc, direct-reduced, and other production routes;
- captive, open-access, purchased, and renewable electricity;
- coal, coke, gas, oxygen, scrap, and output reconciliation;
- purchased billets, slabs, DRI, pig iron, and ferroalloys;
- tolling, group-company, and subcontracting structures;
- local assurance and engineering capacity; and
- financing and foreign-exchange constraints.

Use local technical providers, plant data clinics, metering, process-efficiency studies, waste-heat recovery, scrap use, and qualifying power procurement. Do not assume that national carbon credits or voluntary schemes qualify as CBAM carbon-price deductions.

### Turkey

Prioritize:

- EAF electricity and scrap records;
- facility-level rather than group-level data;
- energy-price and exchange-rate scenarios;
- cross-border processing and customs structures;
- current Turkish carbon-pricing developments, without assuming CBAM recognition; and
- concentration among export-oriented facilities.

For suppliers delivering accepted data and approved reductions, consider selective multi-year volume visibility, financing support, and lower-carbon product development.

### North Africa

Use separate playbooks for Morocco, Egypt, Algeria, Tunisia, and other relevant countries. Validate:

- electricity and gas structures;
- state ownership and approval processes;
- financing and foreign-exchange conditions;
- digital reporting and metering maturity;
- assurance-provider availability;
- customs, tolling, and precursor arrangements; and
- evidence supporting renewable electricity.

Regional support may be delivered in French, Arabic, and English. Funding should be based on objective need, strategic value, and measurable outcomes—not nationality. Renewable-resource potential is an investment opportunity, not evidence of lower reportable emissions.

Each country playbook should record its assumptions, evidence requirements, legal questions, owner, source date, and next review date.

## 5. Commercial and governance mechanisms

### 5.1 Certificate and treasury controls

Treasury should establish a certificate lifecycle procedure covering:

- exposure forecasting by product, installation, importer, and reporting period;
- account ownership and administration;
- purchase authority and approval limits;
- price and liquidity risk ownership;
- accrual and accounting treatment;
- inventory and goods-in-transit treatment;
- allocation to products, suppliers, and customers;
- provisional-to-final reconciliation;
- corrections for returned, rejected, downgraded, or reclassified goods;
- FX treatment; and
- supplier and customer recovery.

The procedure should name the system of record, data cut-off dates, correction rules, escalation thresholds, and residual-risk owner. All legal mechanics remain subject to current official confirmation.

### 5.2 Carbon-price deductions

Use country- and scheme-specific checklists covering:

- legal basis of the charge;
- liable installation;
- payment evidence;
- applicable period;
- link to covered goods or emissions;
- currency conversion;
- rebates, exemptions, free allocation, and refunds;
- reimbursement by another party; and
- potential recognition under current rules.

Classify claims as approved, provisionally recognized, pending clarification, or rejected. Until approved, deductions should remain provisional in pricing, forecasts, and accruals.

### 5.3 Cost allocation

| Cost driver | Indicative treatment |
|---|---|
| EU certificate-price movement | Shared through an agreed index or periodic adjustment |
| Emissions inherent in supplier production | Reflected primarily in product economics |
| Buyer-requested low-carbon specification | Buyer contribution or agreed premium |
| Supplier late, false, or unsupported data | Supplier responsibility after cure period |
| Buyer’s classification or import-structure error | Buyer responsibility |
| Initial implementation uncertainty | Time-limited transitional sharing |
| Approved emissions reduction | Shared through premium, bonus, or volume preference |
| Rejected domestic carbon-price deduction | Provisional treatment and later reconciliation |

Use a three-part commercial structure:

1. normal base price;
2. transparent, formula-based CBAM adjustment; and
3. performance mechanism for accepted data and approved reductions.

Transitional sharing must specify a start date, end date, maximum amount or percentage, review point, extension authority, and exit condition. No cost may be recovered twice.

### 5.4 Contract requirements

Contracts should address:

- installation, product, route, and declarant mapping;
- data definitions, timing, evidence, and retention;
- precursor flow-down;
- audit and assurance;
- estimates, defaults, and rejected data;
- process, energy, ownership, and subcontractor changes;
- data liability and reasonable liability caps;
- carbon-price deduction evidence;
- price indexing and reconciliation;
- confidentiality and cybersecurity;
- cure periods;
- change-in-law and force majeure;
- continuity during disputes; and
- termination or step-in rights for persistent material non-compliance.

Local-law schedules should cover India, Turkey, Morocco, Egypt, Algeria, Tunisia, and other relevant jurisdictions, including audit rights, set-off, clawback, currency restrictions, arbitration, termination, and required approvals.

### 5.5 Data and claims governance

Underlying operational data should remain the supplier’s property. The buyer should receive limited rights to use submitted data for CBAM, assurance, procurement, financing, forecasting, and agreed reporting.

Use:

- data minimization;
- purpose limitation;
- role-based access;
- encryption and audit logs;
- approved hosting locations;
- cross-border transfer safeguards;
- defined retention and deletion rules;
- verifier and financier access controls; and
- incident-notification procedures.

Every environmental claim should identify its boundary, baseline, methodology, evidence, assurance status, claimant, approved wording, validity period, and consent of the other party. Supplier, buyer, and customer claims must not double count the same reduction.

## 6. Partnership governance and relationship management

The executive steering committee should include procurement, sustainability, customs, trade compliance, finance, treasury, legal, tax, engineering, quality, IT, risk, and regional leaders.

The CBAM Program Office should own:

- regulatory monitoring;
- supplier segmentation;
- data standards;
- assurance coordination;
- financing controls;
- contract implementation;
- continuity planning; and
- executive reporting.

Maintain registers for:

1. products and CN codes;
2. installations and production routes;
3. methodologies and versions;
4. data deficiencies and corrective actions;
5. carbon-price deductions;
6. supplier support and funding;
7. assumptions and validation actions;
8. regulatory interpretations; and
9. environmental claims.

### Relationship principles

The buyer should:

- listen before negotiating;
- acknowledge fairness concerns;
- share its own exposure and constraints;
- avoid duplicated data requests;
- provide reasons for rejected data or support applications;
- use reasonable cure periods;
- continue undisputed payments;
- avoid suspending supply during good-faith methodology disputes;
- avoid unrelated confidential-information demands; and
- avoid coercive exclusivity or common pricing arrangements.

### Escalation

| Level | Owner | Scope | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Operational | Supplier CBAM owner and category manager | Missing records, calculations, invoice issues | 10 business days |
| 2. Technical | Independent specialist or verifier | Allocation, electricity, precursor, methodology disputes | 20 business days |
| 3. Executive/legal | Steering committee | Material cost, continuity, contract, or regulatory disputes | 30 business days |

During disputes, provisional values may be used for forecasting and settlement, but downside exposure should be accrued where appropriate. Each matter must have a remediation owner and final decision date.

## 7. Implementation roadmap

### Day 0–30: Current 2026 exposure control

- Map all covered 2026 imports.
- Confirm importers, declarants, representatives, and certificate responsibilities.
- Identify missing, rejected, estimated, provisional, and default data.
- Quantify certificate, default, deduction, FX, and liquidity exposure.
- Establish provisional settlement, accrual, and correction procedures.
- Identify critical single-source risks.
- Assign owners and escalation dates.
- Determine how earlier 2026 shipments will be corrected or reconciled under applicable processes.

### Day 31–90: Stabilize

- Complete facility segmentation.
- Finalize product-specific data protocols.
- Approve indirect-emissions and verification matrices.
- Launch supplier listening sessions and the CBAM Academy.
- Complete priority data-gap assessments.
- Establish the certificate and treasury procedure.
- Sign interim data, confidentiality, pricing, and continuity protocols.
- Identify backup sources.

**Gate:** no major support or preferred-volume commitment without validated scope, baseline, responsibility matrix, funding case, and continuity assessment.

### Months 4–6: Control and assure

- Install priority meters.
- Implement shipment-to-emissions reconciliation.
- Conduct pre-verification for critical sites.
- Establish carbon-price deduction workflows.
- Execute priority local-law contract schedules.
- Implement continuity plans.
- Begin recurring data acceptance and exposure forecasting.

**Gate:** every critical import must have accepted data or controlled provisional data with a funded, time-bound remediation plan. Provisional data do not replace legal requirements.

### Months 7–12: Verify and optimize

- Complete required assurance or verification.
- Reduce default and rejected-data exposure.
- Reconcile carbon-price claims.
- Introduce performance-linked pricing and financing.
- Implement low-cost efficiency measures.
- Integrate CBAM-adjusted landed cost into sourcing decisions.
- Qualify alternatives for exposed products.

**Gate:** preferred allocation requires accepted data, corrective-action progress, competitive total landed cost, reliable delivery, and acceptable concentration risk.

### 2027–2028: Decarbonize and institutionalize

- Scale validated abatement projects.
- Expand lower-carbon purchasing where technically and commercially justified.
- Use selective multi-year commitments.
- Embed emissions data quality and performance in sourcing awards.
- Review concentration, route economics, and supplier performance.
- Publish only approved and consented claims.

### End state

Suppliers should independently operate reliable reporting systems and finance a meaningful share of their transition. Buyer support should shift from basic compliance to strategic innovation, resilience, and high-return abatement.

## 8. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Incorrect CN code or declarant mapping | Joint customs-engineering review and flow-specific responsibility matrix |
| Defective or rejected emissions data | Evidence standards, plausibility checks, pre-verification, audit rights |
| Excessive default exposure | Priority data-gap funding, financial escalation, remediation deadlines |
| Missing precursor information | Flow-down requirements, controlled estimates, upstream engagement where justified |
| Supplier perceives CBAM as protectionist | Senior listening, transparent economics, independent review, appeal process |
| Opaque or excessive pass-through | Causation-based allocation, indexed formula, transitional cap and end date |
| Certificate-price, FX, or liquidity volatility | Treasury scenarios, accruals, approval limits, reconciliation |
| Rejected domestic carbon-price deduction | Scheme-specific evidence checklist and provisional accounting |
| Buyer-funded project fails | Baseline, milestones, measurement, co-funding, staged release, clawback |
| Data breach or misuse | Data minimization, secure platform, transfer safeguards, access controls |
| Competition-law exposure | Counsel review, anonymized benchmarking, no price coordination or coercive exclusivity |
| Supplier withdrawal or insolvency | Dual sourcing, qualification, inventory, route flexibility, transition plan |
| Assurance bottlenecks | Early scheduling, approved provider panel, proportionate assurance |
| Public-finance or subsidy concern | Eligibility, sanctions, anti-bribery, procurement, subsidy, and double-financing review |
| Unsupported environmental claims | Formal claim-approval record and consent controls |
| Indefinite remediation | Maximum periods, limited extensions, final retain-or-replace decision |
| Regulatory change | Named owner, source-controlled register, supplier communication protocol |
| Failed assumptions | Risk owner, validation date, consequence, and contingency |

## 9. KPIs

Every KPI should define its denominator, baseline, reporting period, materiality threshold, owner, data source, and unit of measurement.

### Exposure and finance

- CBAM cost per tonne by product, installation, route, and importer.
- Certificate-requirement forecast accuracy.
- Exposure attributable to defaults, data errors, and rejected deductions.
- Working-capital requirement in cash and days.
- Provisional-to-final exposure variance.
- Unrecovered or duplicated CBAM-related charges.
- Support cost per tonne of emissions avoided.
- Total landed cost versus qualified alternatives.

### Data and control effectiveness

- Percentage of imports mapped to the correct CN code, installation, route, and declarant.
- Percentage of volume with complete accepted records.
- Precursor-data completeness for priority products.
- Percentage of records rejected or materially revised.
- Number and value of unsupported assumptions.
- Average time from shipment to accepted emissions record.
- Assurance findings and closure time.
- Approved, provisional, pending, and rejected carbon-price deductions.

### Environmental and operational

- Direct and indirect emissions intensity, where applicable.
- Approved reductions normalized for product mix, route, volume, and facility changes.
- Energy intensity and scrap share.
- Abatement projects delivered on time and budget.
- Support cost per tonne of approved or verified reduction.
- Quality and on-time delivery against pre-program performance.
- Share of strategic suppliers with approved reduction roadmaps.
- Number of claims with approved boundary, methodology, evidence, and ownership.

### Relationship and resilience

- On-time data submission rate.
- Supplier perception of transparency and proportionality.
- Number and resolution time of methodology and pricing disputes.
- Percentage of suppliers independently capable of reporting.
- Single-source exposure by product and installation.
- Inventory coverage for high-risk products.
- Qualified backup capacity and qualification lead time.
- CBAM-related disruption incidents, lost volume, recovery time, and attribution status.

Provisional objectives—such as reducing default exposure below 10–15%, achieving 95% accepted records for critical volume, or reaching 80% roadmap coverage—should be calibrated after the 2026 baseline and must not be presented as regulatory benchmarks.

## 10. Executive approvals required

Executives should approve:

1. The CBAM Supplier Partnership Program and budget.
2. Facility-level segmentation and country playbooks.
3. Targeted, milestone-based support rather than blanket reimbursement.
4. A causation- and controllability-based cost-allocation framework.
5. Treasury controls for certificate-price, liquidity, FX, and provisional-settlement exposure.
6. Backup-sourcing requirements for high-criticality products.
7. Legal, customs, tax, accounting, competition, data, public-finance, and local-law validation.
8. Supplier data-protection and cybersecurity controls.
9. Maximum remediation periods and extension rules.
10. The certificate lifecycle and carbon-price deduction procedures.
11. The KPI dictionary and quarterly executive dashboard.
12. The transition plan from buyer assistance to supplier self-sufficiency.

## Final position

CBAM should be managed as a **shared competitiveness challenge**, not a unilateral supplier penalty.

The buyer should be firm on compliance but collaborative on implementation. Suppliers must provide reliable, supportable information and deliver agreed improvements. The buyer must provide clear requirements, protect confidential data, avoid opaque surcharges and unnecessary requests, share transitional burdens transparently, finance only measurable improvements, and preserve alternative supply options.

The program should proceed through defined gates toward a resilient supply base in which suppliers independently report reliable data, manage their underlying emissions exposure, and finance a meaningful share of their transition while remaining commercially competitive in the EU market.
