# Global AI Productivity Toolkit Rollout  
## Executive Change-Management Strategy

### Executive position

The group should mandate **access, training, approved-tool use in suitable workflows, and compliance with safeguards**—not arbitrary interaction volume or visible enthusiasm.

> Every in-scope employee must have access to approved AI, complete appropriate training, and use AI-enabled workflows where those workflows are formally assessed as suitable, lawful, accessible, secure, and beneficial. Employees are not required to use AI where professional, regulatory, privacy, accessibility, or quality considerations make it inappropriate.

This approach reduces shadow-AI risk while protecting trust, professional judgment, customer confidentiality, and fair performance management.

---

## 1. Objectives

The rollout will:

1. Reduce use of unapproved AI tools and associated data-security risk.
2. Improve quality, service, documentation, and capacity in suitable workflows.
3. Preserve human accountability for consequential insurance decisions.
4. Provide transparent and equitable implementation across countries and functions.
5. Address covert use, professional skepticism, privacy concerns, and fear of job loss without retaliation or stereotyping.
6. Ensure usage metrics support enablement and assurance rather than becoming an employee-surveillance system.
7. Support workforce transition through reskilling, redeployment where feasible, and fair process.

---

## 2. Global non-negotiables and local flexibility

| Global non-negotiables | Locally adaptable practices |
|---|---|
| Approved tools, vendors, and secure configurations | Rollout timing and sequencing |
| Data classification, confidentiality, and security controls | Local-language communications and examples |
| High-risk and prohibited use cases | Training format and delivery channels |
| Human accountability and review requirements | Consultation and notification processes |
| Minimum training and accessibility standards | Works-council, union, and employee-representative engagement |
| AI Metrics Charter and metric firewall | Local support models and credible messengers |
| Incident reporting and escalation | Country-specific exceptions required by law or professional standards |
| Non-retaliation, correction, and appeal rights | Local workflow examples and professional validation |
| Minimum privacy and employee-monitoring safeguards | Local workforce-transition arrangements |
| Minimum support and employee experience standards | Country-specific data residency and transfer controls |

Local adaptation may add protections or requirements, but should not weaken the global baseline unless legally necessary and explicitly approved.

Country differences should be investigated through evidence about law, leadership, trust, language, access, professional norms, and prior change experience—not explained through assumptions about national character.

---

## 3. Operating principles

### Mandatory capability, not forced enthusiasm

Employees may be required to:

- obtain access to approved tools;
- complete paid, role-appropriate training;
- use approved AI in formally designated workflows;
- follow data, security, confidentiality, and human-review requirements;
- document material AI assistance where required;
- report material errors, incidents, and near misses;
- use approved alternatives rather than public AI tools for company work.

Employees should not be required to:

- use AI for every task;
- meet prompt, login, time-in-tool, or generated-content quotas;
- upload confidential information merely to demonstrate usage;
- provide complete prompt histories to line managers without authorized cause;
- use AI where it is prohibited, unsuitable, inaccessible, or inconsistent with professional duties;
- accept AI output without appropriate human review.

### Outcomes over activity

Low usage alone is not evidence of poor performance, disengagement, disloyalty, or misconduct. Prompt counts, login frequency, time in the tool, generated words, and estimated AI-written content must not be used as standalone performance measures.

Where an AI-enabled workflow is mandatory, assessment should focus on:

- whether the employee followed the approved process;
- whether access, training, time, and support were provided;
- whether required human review occurred;
- whether the work met quality, customer, and control standards.

### Human judgment and professional dignity

AI assists; accountable professionals remain responsible. Employees must retain sufficient time and authority to challenge, correct, override, or reject an AI output.

Responsible non-use, skepticism, source checking, and escalation of a poor output are positive control behaviors—not failures of adoption.

## 4. Governance and accountability

Establish a **Global AI Adoption and Assurance Council** with representatives from:

- technology and business functions;
- privacy and data protection;
- legal;
- information security;
- risk and compliance;
- HR and employee relations;
- learning and accessibility;
- communications;
- internal audit;
- professional bodies;
- employee representatives where appropriate.

The Council owns:

- global policy and minimum controls;
- tool and vendor approval;
- use-case risk classification;
- mandatory workflow standards;
- the AI Metrics Charter;
- training and accessibility standards;
- incident taxonomy and escalation;
- workforce-transition principles;
- independent assurance and board reporting.

Each country or entity should establish an **AI Implementation Cell** including local business, HR, privacy, legal, security, risk, learning, communications, accessibility specialists, professional representatives, and employee representatives where applicable.

The cell is responsible for:

- local legal and labor clearance;
- consultation and employee notices;
- local use cases and exceptions;
- language and accessibility;
- rollout timing;
- support and listening;
- local escalation.

A documented RACI must assign one accountable owner for:

- approving mandatory workflows;
- granting exceptions;
- approving access to individual telemetry;
- deciding safe-migration eligibility;
- classifying incidents;
- suspending a tool or workflow;
- handling appeals;
- approving workforce-impact actions;
- retiring harmful or misleading metrics.

## 5. Mandatory workflow approval

No workflow becomes mandatory merely because the toolkit is available.

Each mandatory workflow must document:

1. Business purpose and expected benefit.
2. Evidence that AI is suitable and proportionate.
3. Approved tool, model, configuration, and integrations.
4. Permitted and prohibited data classes.
5. Required human review and sign-off.
6. Quality, fairness, and control thresholds.
7. Professional and regulatory obligations.
8. Accessibility and accommodation alternatives.
9. Training and support requirements.
10. Legitimate non-use and exception criteria.
11. Records, retention, and audit requirements.
12. Local legal, privacy, employment, and consultation clearance.
13. Review date and withdrawal criteria.

Mandatory status should expire unless reapproved. A workflow must be paused if it creates material customer harm, unacceptable quality degradation, unlawful processing, discriminatory outcomes, or uncontrolled confidentiality risk.

## 6. Stakeholder segments and interventions

### 6.1 Enthusiastic early adopters

**Value:** experimentation, peer learning, and rapid discovery.  
**Risks:** unsafe overconfidence, overclaiming, and pressure on cautious colleagues.

**Interventions:**

- controlled sandbox access;
- advanced training;
- practitioner-led demonstrations;
- recognition for safe experimentation, verification, and knowledge sharing;
- clear limits on unapproved use and autonomous decision-making.

### 6.2 Covert or external-tool users

Covert use may reflect better functionality outside the approved tool, unclear disclosure expectations, fear of surveillance or job loss, professional stigma, language limitations, or workflow gaps.

**Interventions:**

- establish a time-limited confidential safe-migration period;
- allow low-risk historic experimentation to be regularized without automatic discipline;
- improve approved-tool functionality and connectors;
- ask what capability gap drove external use;
- provide help to recreate useful workflows securely;
- investigate only credible security, fraud, deliberate exfiltration, customer-harm, or repeated control breaches.

Security logs must not automatically feed performance dashboards.

### 6.3 Professional skeptics

Underwriters, actuaries, claims specialists, lawyers, compliance professionals, investigators, and other experts may be concerned about judgment, authorship, confidentiality, or accountability.

**Interventions:**

- involve respected practitioners in use-case validation;
- demonstrate quality and control benefits, not just speed;
- position AI as support for professional judgment;
- require named human accountability;
- allow documented non-use where appropriate;
- recognize source checking, challenge, and responsible rejection.

### 6.4 Privacy, labor, and monitoring skeptics

**Interventions:**

- publish a plain-language data map;
- explain what is and is not monitored;
- disclose retention, access, deletion, and transfer arrangements;
- involve privacy officers, works councils, unions, and employee representatives early;
- provide confidential questions and independent appeals;
- complete required impact assessments before launch.

### 6.5 Employees concerned about job loss or work intensification

Leadership should explain:

- which tasks may change;
- which accountabilities remain human;
- how productivity benefits will be used;
- what reskilling and redeployment support exists;
- how material role changes will be consulted on and governed.

The group should commit that:

- individual AI activity will not be a hidden redundancy proxy;
- productivity gains will not automatically become higher quotas or reduced review time;
- workload, overtime, stress, quality, and customer outcomes will be monitored;
- workforce changes will follow transparent and fair processes.

### 6.6 Genuine or justified non-users

Non-use may be appropriate because work is prohibited, unsuitable, inaccessible, confidential, language-constrained, or inconsistent with professional duties.

Legitimate non-use should be formally recognized and should not be displayed as a “resistance” label. Employees must be able to request an exception without disclosing unnecessary medical or personal information.

### 6.7 Unsafe high users

High activity may indicate inappropriate data entry, inadequate review, or unsafe automation.

The response should be to contain risk, coach, restrict the workflow if necessary, and investigate actual control concerns—not high usage itself.

## 7. Country and function adaptation

### Country implementation

Before each rollout wave, assess:

- privacy, employment, AI, insurance, and data-transfer requirements;
- employee-monitoring restrictions;
- works-council, union, or consultation obligations;
- prior experiences with restructuring or surveillance;
- trust in local and central leadership;
- language, accessibility, connectivity, and tool performance;
- local professional and customer expectations.

A country must not launch while a material legal or consultation requirement remains unresolved.

### Function-specific examples

| Function | Suitable applications | Key safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| Claims and service | Summarization, correspondence drafts, knowledge retrieval, triage support | Customer-data minimization, human review, no unapproved claims decisions |
| Underwriting | Document comparison, portfolio analysis, scenario support | No autonomous risk acceptance; professional accountability |
| Actuarial and analytics | Coding assistance, documentation, model exploration | Validation, reproducibility, source controls |
| Legal and compliance | Research and drafting | Privilege protection, source verification, jurisdictional review |
| Finance | Reconciliations, narrative reporting, control documentation | Segregation of duties and auditability |
| HR | Administrative drafting and knowledge support | No unapproved employee inference or automated employment decisions |
| Technology and security | Coding and analysis assistance | Secure-development, secrets protection, vulnerability controls |
| Executives and managers | Briefing and synthesis | No opaque AI scores replacing leadership judgment |

## 8. Leadership, communications, and listening

### Leadership responsibilities

Executives must:

- explain the business and risk case without exaggerating benefits;
- state what AI will and will not be used for;
- publish the Metrics Charter before broad telemetry collection;
- fund training, support, privacy controls, and reskilling;
- report limitations, incidents, and remediation;
- review quality, workload, trust, and role impacts quarterly.

Managers must:

- provide protected learning time;
- discuss suitable workflows and barriers;
- focus on outcomes and quality;
- avoid public comparisons and usage shaming;
- recognize responsible challenge and non-use;
- diagnose access, capability, workflow, privacy, and trust issues before escalating low use.

Managers may not create unofficial quotas, demand screenshots or full prompt histories, pressure employees to use confidential data, or treat skepticism as disloyalty.

### Communications cycle

Use a repeated cycle:

1. **Explain** purpose, scope, mandate, timeline, and safeguards.
2. **Demonstrate** realistic role-specific workflows using non-sensitive data.
3. **Listen** through public, confidential, and representative channels.
4. **Respond** with clear answers and visible changes.
5. **Reinforce** outcomes, limitations, incidents, and improvements.

Every employee must understand:

- what is mandatory, optional, prohibited, or exempt;
- what data may be entered;
- what is logged and who can access it;
- retention and deletion;
- performance-management boundaries;
- human-review requirements;
- reporting, correction, complaint, and appeal routes;
- job-impact and reskilling principles.

Use local languages, written FAQs, manager briefings, small-group sessions, employee-representative meetings, office hours, short videos, and anonymous Q&A. Silence in a town hall must not be treated as consent.

### Listening and feedback service levels

Use baseline surveys, interviews, focus groups, workflow observation, accessibility testing, pulse surveys at 30/60/90 days, help-desk analysis, champion feedback, and employee panels.

Every material concern must have:

- a named owner;
- acknowledgement within a defined period;
- severity classification;
- response deadline;
- escalation route;
- closure explanation;
- protection against retaliation.

Publish periodic “You said, we changed” reports, including unresolved issues and reasons for decisions not to adopt proposed changes.

## 9. Training and support

### Minimum global curriculum

All in-scope employees complete paid, accessible, role-appropriate training covering:

- approved tools and access;
- practical prompting and workflow use;
- hallucinations, bias, and verification;
- confidentiality and data classification;
- intellectual property and copyright;
- human accountability;
- prohibited and high-risk use;
- incident and near-miss reporting;
- monitoring, telemetry, and employee rights.

Completion is not treated as competence. Use practical exercises, coaching, and reassessment.

### Support infrastructure

Provide:

- searchable use-case libraries;
- prompt and verification templates;
- AI-skilled help-desk support;
- privacy and security advisory routes;
- local-language office hours;
- rapid incident reporting;
- service-level targets for access and defects;
- approved connectors, redaction, and data-loss-prevention tools.

The secure approved path must be sufficiently useful that employees do not need to circumvent it.

## 10. Privacy, security, and confidentiality

### AI Metrics Charter and metric firewall

Before broad telemetry collection, publish a binding Charter defining:

- data collected;
- purpose of each metric;
- whether content or metadata is retained;
- aggregation and small-cell suppression;
- authorized users;
- retention and deletion;
- employee notice;
- correction and appeal;
- prohibited uses;
- audit and access logging.

Separate:

1. **Developmental measurement:** access, training, workflow fit, support, trust, and migration.
2. **Operational assurance:** approved workflows, human review, data handling, incidents, and controls.
3. **Employment or disciplinary evidence:** only for a specific, documented concern and subject to fair process.

Individual telemetry must be excluded from routine performance dashboards. No usage-volume metric may influence compensation, promotion, discipline, redundancy selection, or routine ratings.

### Information classification

| Data type | Default treatment |
|---|---|
| Public data | Permitted in approved tools |
| Internal non-sensitive data | Permitted subject to policy |
| Confidential business data | Approved enterprise environment only |
| Customer personal data | Restricted; minimize, mask, and use approved workflows |
| Health, financial, biometric, or special-category data | Prohibited by default unless specifically approved |
| Legal privilege and investigation material | Restricted to protected environments |
| Credentials, secrets, and security tokens | Never enter general-purpose prompts |
| Material non-public information | Restricted with information-barrier controls |

Implement encryption, role-based access, tenant isolation, retention limits, regional processing where required, no unauthorized vendor model training, masking, redaction, administrative-access logging, model and connector version control, change testing, manual fallback for critical workflows, and workflow suspension procedures.

Prompts and outputs must be treated as potentially personal, confidential, privileged, or regulated information.

---

## 11. Insurance, regulatory, and workforce controls

Classify use cases as low risk, controlled assistance, high impact, or prohibited. For claims, pricing, underwriting eligibility, fraud, customer vulnerability, complaints, and employment decisions, require:

- a named accountable owner;
- source and evidence checking;
- meaningful human review;
- audit trails;
- override and appeal processes;
- bias and disparate-impact testing;
- validation and monitoring;
- incident reporting;
- customer correction where applicable.

Local legal, privacy, compliance, and employee-relations reviews must confirm requirements for:

- data-protection and employee-monitoring assessments;
- automated-decision restrictions;
- insurance-supervisory expectations;
- data transfer and residency;
- works-council or union consultation;
- accessibility and employment protections;
- recordkeeping and legal holds.

Create a funded workforce-transition plan covering skills assessment, paid learning time, internal mobility, redeployment, accommodation, role-impact assessments, consultation, and workload monitoring.

---

## 12. Appeals, incidents, and non-retaliation

Employees must be able to challenge:

- inaccurate usage data;
- a mandatory workflow classification;
- refusal of an exception;
- an allegation of unsafe use;
- a performance interpretation;
- an accommodation decision;
- retaliation or discrimination.

Appeals should provide written notice, access to relevant evidence subject to confidentiality limits, an opportunity to respond, independent review outside the direct management chain, defined timelines, correction of inaccurate records, and protection against retaliation.

For incidents:

1. Contain or stop the affected process.
2. Notify the accountable owner.
3. Correct customer or business impact.
4. Record the incident or near miss.
5. Assess policy, model, workflow, or training changes.
6. Communicate lessons without blaming the reporter.

A just-culture standard should distinguish good-faith reporting, coaching, negligence, reckless conduct, deliberate circumvention, and serious security, fraud, or customer-harm events.

## 13. Measurement and rollout gates

### Balanced scorecard

**Readiness**

- access and service reliability;
- training and demonstrated competence;
- workflow-assessment coverage;
- support response times;
- employee understanding of policy and monitoring;
- language and accessibility readiness.

**Responsible adoption**

- approved-workflow use at aggregate level;
- data-handling compliance;
- human-review completion;
- safe migration from unapproved tools;
- incident and near-miss reporting;
- legitimate non-use and exception patterns.

**Business outcomes**

- cycle time;
- quality and rework;
- customer experience;
- claims and underwriting outcomes;
- control effectiveness;
- workload and overtime;
- realized value net of review, correction, support, and control costs.

**Trust and inclusion**

- privacy confidence;
- perceived fairness;
- psychological safety;
- willingness to report errors;
- fear of job loss;
- unequal access, benefit, or burden across functions, languages, work arrangements, and accessibility needs.

Use small-cell suppression and avoid identifiable reporting of sensitive demographic groups.

### Stop conditions

Pause a rollout wave or workflow where there is:

- an unresolved critical privacy or security issue;
- material customer harm;
- unacceptable quality or rework;
- inadequate human review;
- unresolved accessibility barriers;
- incomplete required consultation;
- evidence of metric misuse;
- insufficient support capacity;
- a significant unexplained disparity;
- unacceptable workload or work-intensification effects.

## 14. Implementation phases

### Phase 0: Prepare and consult

Complete governance, decision rights, data mapping, privacy and regulatory assessments, workflow classification, vendor due diligence, Metrics Charter, records design, workforce-transition planning, local consultation, and baseline measurement.

**Gate:** no broad deployment until critical controls, employee notices, support capacity, and required consultations are complete.

### Phase 1: Co-design and pilot

Pilot across varied countries, functions, languages, trust levels, professional groups, accessibility needs, and adoption patterns. Use aggregated dashboards only. Evaluate quality, workload, trust, safety, accessibility, and outcomes.

**Gate:** expand only when critical privacy, security, quality, legal, consultation, and support issues are resolved.

### Phase 2: Controlled rollout waves

Sequence by workflow readiness, risk, legal clearance, consultation, support capacity, and tool reliability—not geography alone. Review each wave at 30, 60, and 90 days.

### Phase 3: Stabilization

Audit dashboard access and manager behavior; review workload, quality, customer results, and unequal outcomes; retire misleading metrics; redesign or withdraw ineffective workflows; publish outcomes and unresolved concerns.

### Phase 4: Continuous improvement

At least quarterly, review policies, metrics, incidents, controls, accessibility, localization, vendor and model changes, workforce impacts, and whether mandatory workflows remain lawful, proportionate, useful, and safe.

The calendar must remain conditional. A country, function, or workflow should remain in preparation or pilot if readiness gates are not met.

---

## Definition of success

The rollout is successful when employees can truthfully say:

- “I know what is mandatory, optional, prohibited, exempt, and monitored.”
- “The approved tool is safe and useful enough that I do not need to work around it.”
- “My professional judgment and accountability still matter.”
- “I can challenge or reject an AI output without retaliation.”
- “My data and customer information are protected.”
- “If my role changes, the organization will be honest and provide fair support.”
- “The program measures better work and safer use—not performative activity.”

**Governing principle:**

> Measure whether employees are enabled to use AI safely and whether approved workflows improve outcomes. Do not measure employee worth by how much they interact with a system.
