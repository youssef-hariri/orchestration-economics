# Global Compensation Policy  
**Effective date:** 2026  
**Owner:** Chief People Officer / Total Rewards  
**Applies to:** Employees in all company locations, including employees hired through local entities or employer-of-record providers, subject to applicable law, contracts, collective agreements, and employee-representation rights.

---

## Executive summary

The company will use a **globally consistent, market-informed, and explainable compensation system** for its distributed workforce.

The policy combines:

- one global job architecture;
- labor-market-based geographic pricing;
- consistent range-placement and progression standards;
- meaningful salary-range transparency;
- regular pay-equity and compression reviews;
- controlled exceptions;
- defined mobility rules;
- privacy-conscious data governance;
- country-specific legal implementation;
- formal employee inquiry and appeal processes.

The company will not promise identical salaries or identical purchasing power in every location. It will promise that:

1. comparable work is assessed using consistent job and level standards;
2. geographic differences reflect evidence about relevant labor markets, not employee worth;
3. pay ranges and placement factors are understandable;
4. unexplained pay and progression gaps are investigated;
5. employees can raise concerns without retaliation;
6. local legal, contractual, consultation, privacy, payroll, and reporting requirements are addressed before implementation.

This policy is a global framework. It is not legal advice and does not, by itself, establish compliance in every jurisdiction. Each country requires a reviewed local addendum and readiness certification before launch.

## 1. Policy status, hierarchy, and assumptions

### 1.1 Policy hierarchy

The following order governs implementation:

1. Applicable law and enforceable employee rights;
2. Collective agreements, works-council or union rights, and employment contracts;
3. Country-specific compensation and transparency addenda;
4. This global policy;
5. Management discretion within approved limits.

Where requirements conflict, the company must pause the affected decision, obtain local advice, document the narrowest appropriate deviation, and obtain the required approval.

### 1.2 Assumptions

This policy assumes that:

- roles can be mapped to common job families and levels;
- the company uses a combination of local entities, EOR arrangements, and potentially contingent workers;
- compensation may be paid in local currencies;
- compensation includes base salary and may include bonus, commission, equity, benefits, allowances, and statutory payments;
- market data is available for major job families, although confidence may vary by location;
- some jurisdictions restrict the collection, use, transfer, or disclosure of employee compensation or demographic data;
- local legal, tax, payroll, privacy, employment, and employee-relations advice will be obtained before implementation.

Where these assumptions are not valid, the country addendum must define an alternative process.

# 2. Compensation philosophy

> We pay for the scope and value of the role, the employee’s sustained contribution, and the relevant labor market. We use a consistent global job architecture and transparent geographic methodology where labor-market differences materially affect hiring and retention. We aim for comparable pay and opportunity for comparable work, investigate unexplained gaps, and apply local legal requirements through country-specific implementation.

The philosophy has six principles.

### 2.1 Role and contribution come before location

Base pay is primarily determined by:

- job family and specialty;
- career level;
- scope, complexity, and decision authority;
- relevant capabilities and experience;
- sustained contribution and performance;
- the relevant labor market;
- internal equity and legal requirements.

Location may affect the market range, but it does not measure an employee’s value, capability, commitment, or quality of work.

### 2.2 Market-informed, not market-controlled

External data informs compensation ranges but does not automatically determine them. The company will consider:

- reputable compensation surveys;
- accepted and declined offers;
- candidate expectations;
- time to fill;
- offer acceptance;
- voluntary attrition;
- recruiter and talent-market intelligence;
- comparable public postings;
- official wage and inflation data;
- currency conditions.

Data must be evaluated for role comparability, sample size, age, employer type, compensation components, remote-work practices, currency, and selection bias.

### 2.3 Labor-market cost is not cost of living

The company primarily prices base salary according to the **cost of labor**: the amount required to attract and retain qualified talent in the relevant labor market.

Cost-of-living and purchasing-power data may inform:

- relocation support;
- hardship or temporary allowances;
- benefits;
- minimum global pay floors;
- employee-welfare programs.

They do not automatically determine base salary. The company will not describe the policy simply as “cost-of-living pay.”

### 2.4 Comparable work should have comparable opportunity

Employees performing substantially comparable work should have:

- consistent job and level definitions;
- comparable performance expectations;
- access to progression and development;
- consistent placement criteria;
- a defensible explanation for geographic differences;
- access to a review process.

This does not require identical nominal pay across countries. It requires a consistent and reviewable methodology.

### 2.5 Transparency with privacy

The company will explain:

- its compensation philosophy;
- job architecture and level definitions;
- applicable ranges;
- geographic methodology;
- placement factors;
- progression and promotion rules;
- review and appeal processes;
- variable pay and equity practices.

The company will not disclose identifiable compensation information about another employee unless legally required and appropriately authorized.

### 2.6 Locally adapted total rewards

Base-pay architecture is globally coordinated. Benefits, statutory payments, leave, pensions, severance, taxes, payroll practices, allowances, and some incentive arrangements may vary by jurisdiction.

Communications must distinguish clearly between base salary, guaranteed cash, variable pay, equity, benefits, and statutory compensation.

# 3. Job architecture and compensation markets

## 3.1 Global job architecture

Total Rewards will maintain a common architecture covering:

- job family;
- specialty or discipline;
- career track;
- level;
- role scope;
- decision-making authority;
- expected impact;
- technical, professional, and leadership capabilities.

Titles alone do not determine level. Role profiles, evidence, and calibration must be used.

Example engineering levels may include:

- Software Engineer I;
- Software Engineer II;
- Senior Software Engineer;
- Staff Engineer;
- Principal Engineer.

Management roles will be assessed using organizational scope, decision authority, and impact.

## 3.2 Market families

A single geographic factor will not automatically apply to every job. Where supported by evidence, the company may use market families such as:

- globally mobile technical roles;
- locally competitive operational roles;
- sales and revenue roles;
- regulated or country-specific roles;
- scarce specialist roles.

Any departure from the standard methodology requires documented evidence and Total Rewards approval.

## 3.3 Market reference points

For each significant job family and level, the company will establish:

- a reference labor market;
- a target market percentile;
- a global reference midpoint or range;
- the applicable geographic factor;
- a data-confidence rating;
- an effective date;
- the applicable payroll currency.

The company should generally target approximately the 50th percentile for broad roles and a higher percentile for scarce or business-critical roles. **Leadership must approve the target percentile by job family.**

# 4. Geographic pricing methodology

## 4.1 Geographic market groups

The company will use a limited number of geographic market groups for administration, supported by underlying country or metropolitan-market analysis.

Markets may be grouped only when relevant evidence is sufficiently similar, including:

- pay distributions for comparable roles;
- hiring and offer outcomes;
- talent availability;
- attrition;
- market direction;
- currency and economic conditions;
- data confidence.

Group labels must not imply that employees in one location are less valuable. Internal systems may use coded market identifiers. Employee communications should use neutral terms such as “compensation market” or “market reference group.”

## 4.2 Geographic factors

For each job family and level:

\[
\text{Local midpoint} =
\text{Global reference midpoint}
\times \text{Geographic factor}
\times \text{Approved scarcity factor}
\]

A scarcity factor may be used only when supported by hiring, retention, or external market evidence. It must have:

- a documented rationale;
- an approver;
- an effective date;
- a review date;
- a defined scope.

Illustrative factors only:

| Market group | Illustrative midpoint factor |
|---|---:|
| Premium global technology markets | 110–125% |
| Strong technology markets | 95–110% |
| Competitive distributed-technology markets | 80–100% |
| Emerging or lower-cost technology markets | 65–85% |

These figures are examples, not approved rates.

## 4.3 Adverse-impact review

Before approving or materially changing a geographic factor, Total Rewards, Legal, Privacy, and Employee Relations must assess whether it may:

- correlate with nationality, ethnicity, race, gender, immigration status, language, or another protected characteristic;
- rely on historical pay that may encode prior inequity;
- disadvantage employees recruited from a global rather than local labor market;
- produce materially worse pay, progression, or retention outcomes in a particular group;
- have a less discriminatory and reasonably feasible alternative.

Reliable market data does not, by itself, resolve adverse-impact concerns.

## 4.4 Geographic spread review

Executive review is required when:

- the highest and lowest midpoint for the same role and level differ by more than an approved range, initially expected to be 40–50%;
- a geographic gap increases by more than 10 percentage points in one review cycle;
- a lower-priced market has materially higher adjusted attrition;
- repeated exceptions occur in one market;
- public comparison concerns cluster around a market;
- the geographic factor explains more of the difference than role, level, scope, or contribution.

These are review triggers, not legal safe harbors or automatic equalization requirements.

# 5. Compensation location and remote work

## 5.1 Compensation-location classification

Compensation location must be determined using a documented assessment of:

1. actual work location;
2. legal employing entity;
3. payroll and tax location;
4. recruitment market;
5. local and international talent competition;
6. time-zone and travel requirements;
7. immigration constraints;
8. mobility status;
9. protected circumstances;
10. whether the role is local-market, hub-aligned, globally recruited, or multi-location.

Approved categories may include:

- local-market remote role;
- globally recruited remote role;
- hub-aligned role;
- temporary cross-border assignment;
- permanent transfer;
- multi-location role.

Total Rewards and Legal must approve classifications that differ from the employee’s primary work location or standard market.

## 5.2 Default rule

The default compensation location is the employee’s primary, legally approved work location. It is not determined solely by citizenship, nationality, tax residence, or company headquarters.

## 5.3 Permanent relocation

A company-approved permanent relocation may change the applicable compensation market prospectively after review of:

- payroll and tax;
- employing entity;
- immigration;
- benefits;
- minimum compensation;
- consultation requirements;
- employment contract;
- compensation location.

A relocation does not automatically reduce existing base salary. Any proposed reduction requires separate Legal, Employee Relations, Finance, and executive approval and may require consultation or consent.

A move to a higher-priced market does not automatically create a salary increase unless required by policy, contract, law, or an approved business decision.

## 5.4 Temporary cross-border work

Temporary work from another country requires prior approval. Compensation will normally remain tied to the approved compensation location during a short temporary assignment unless:

- local law requires different treatment;
- the assignment becomes permanent;
- a formal mobility package applies;
- the company incurs materially different employment obligations.

Managers may not approve international work based solely on compensation considerations.

## 5.5 Mobility matrix

The local addendum must define separate rules for:

- company-directed relocation;
- employee-requested relocation;
- permanent transfer;
- temporary assignment;
- EOR or entity change;
- business travel;
- safety or humanitarian relocation;
- work-location change without entity change.

For each category, it must specify:

- salary protection;
- adjustment eligibility;
- effective date;
- review period;
- approval level;
- tax and payroll review;
- employee notice;
- appeal route.

# 6. Salary ranges and total rewards

## 6.1 Range structure

Each job-family, level, and market combination must have:

- minimum;
- midpoint;
- maximum;
- payroll currency;
- effective date;
- target market percentile;
- data-confidence rating;
- range width.

Ranges should generally be approximately 40–60% wide from minimum to maximum, unless a wider range is justified by role complexity, seniority, or compensation variability.

A range must be meaningful. It must not be so broad that it conceals how pay is actually determined.

## 6.2 Currency and inflation

The company will distinguish:

1. benchmark currency;
2. band-display currency;
3. payroll currency;
4. accounting currency.

Market analysis may use a stable reference currency, but employees and candidates will receive the applicable local-currency range where required or appropriate.

The currency methodology must define:

- exchange-rate source;
- averaging period;
- effective date;
- treatment of extreme volatility;
- range-recalculation rules;
- whether salaries are protected from currency-driven reductions.

A rolling three- or six-month average is recommended for modeling. Daily exchange rates will not independently determine salary bands.

Inflation and market movement must be reported separately. An interim review should occur after material inflation or currency movement, initially expected at approximately 8–10%, or after a material change in legal minimum pay.

The company does not guarantee that salary will match consumer inflation.

## 6.3 Range placement

Placement within a range must consider:

- demonstrated scope;
- sustained contribution and performance;
- relevant experience;
- critical skills;
- internal comparators;
- market position;
- role scarcity;
- development expectations;
- time in role, where relevant.

Prior salary must not be the primary placement factor where it could reproduce historical inequity or is restricted by law.

## 6.4 Range position monitoring

The company will monitor employees:

- below minimum;
- in the lower quartile;
- near midpoint;
- in the upper quartile;
- above maximum.

Employees below minimum must have a documented remediation plan. Employees above maximum must receive an individualized review and must not be automatically frozen indefinitely.

# 7. Progression, promotion, and pay decisions

## 7.1 Separate decision types

Every pay decision must be classified as one or more of:

- merit adjustment;
- market adjustment;
- promotion adjustment;
- equity or compression adjustment;
- mobility adjustment;
- legal or remediation adjustment.

## 7.2 Progression

Progression within a level should reflect:

- achievement against role expectations;
- increasing independence;
- broader scope;
- demonstrated capabilities;
- sustained impact;
- readiness for additional responsibility.

Time in role is not an automatic right to promotion or a specific increase. However, the company must monitor whether similarly performing employees in particular locations, demographic groups, or manager populations remain at the bottom of ranges.

The company will publish or provide access to:

- level expectations;
- review timing;
- evidence requirements;
- calibration processes;
- decision-makers;
- review and appeal procedures.

## 7.3 Promotion

Promotion requires evidence that the employee is consistently performing at the next level. Training completion or time served alone is insufficient.

A promotion should normally produce a meaningful compensation outcome. If the increase is limited because the employee is already highly placed in the new range, the reason must be documented.

Promotion and merit decisions require written evidence and cross-country calibration. Headquarters proximity, visibility, time-zone convenience, or informal access to senior leaders may not be used as unapproved factors.

## 7.4 Protected leave, accommodations, and part-time work

Subject to local law:

- protected leave must not disadvantage pay placement, merit, promotion, bonus, or equity eligibility;
- performance assessment periods must account for protected leave and approved accommodations;
- part-time compensation must be analyzed on a full-time-equivalent basis while preserving contractual pay;
- accommodation-related information must remain confidential;
- employees returning from protected leave must receive a documented compensation review;
- protected circumstances must not be used negatively in mobility or placement decisions.

## 7.5 Compression and inversion

The company will review compression when:

- new-hire median pay exceeds the comparable incumbent median by more than an approved threshold, initially expected at 5–8%;
- a new hire is more than 10% above a similarly scoped incumbent without documented justification;
- adjacent-level pay separation falls below an approved threshold;
- more than 10% of a team is below the revised range after a market update.

These thresholds trigger investigation; they do not establish acceptable or lawful disparity.

Remedies may include:

- targeted base-pay corrections;
- promotion review;
- equity refreshes;
- lump-sum payments;
- hiring controls;
- revised ranges;
- level recalibration.

The company should not correct compression by reducing existing or new employees’ pay.

# 9. Exceptions

## 9.1 Permitted exceptions

Exceptions are limited to:

- legal requirements or equal-pay remedies;
- critical skill scarcity;
- business-critical hiring;
- documented retention risk;
- internal-equity correction;
- unusual role scope;
- acquisition or integration protection;
- data limitations in a new market;
- temporary assignment;
- disability accommodation or other protected circumstances.

## 9.2 Required documentation

Every exception must record:

- worker, role, level, and location;
- applicable range;
- proposed compensation;
- reason;
- comparable employees considered;
- cost and duration;
- approver;
- legal or Employee Relations review where required;
- review date;
- whether the exception indicates that the range should change.

Out-of-range offers require approval before communication. Managers and recruiters may not make informal commitments.

Retention awards must be exceptional and time-limited. Repeated retention awards require systemic review.

Any material concentration of exceptions by country, manager, level, worker category, or protected group requires review, even if a numerical threshold is not reached.

# 10. Transparency and employee rights

## 10.1 Global minimum standard

The company will provide information about:

- compensation philosophy;
- job family and level;
- applicable salary range;
- principal placement factors;
- promotion and pay-review processes;
- variable pay and equity;
- geographic pricing;
- mobility rules;
- review and appeal procedures.

As a global practice, candidates should receive a meaningful applicable base-pay range before making a material employment decision, subject to local requirements concerning timing, remuneration scope, format, language, currency, and consultation.

## 10.2 Country-specific implementation

Each country addendum must specify:

- whether ranges must appear in job advertisements;
- disclosure timing;
- whether current employees have information rights;
- required components of remuneration;
- employee or representative access to comparator information;
- reporting and publication requirements;
- consultation obligations;
- local language and currency;
- record retention;
- responsible entity;
- privacy restrictions.

Where local law requires broader remuneration disclosure than base salary, the company must use the legally appropriate format.

## 10.3 Employee information requests

Employees may request information about:

- their job family and level;
- applicable range;
- position within the range;
- principal placement factors;
- recent pay decisions;
- review or appeal options.

Requests must be handled through an approved process with:

- identity verification;
- privacy review;
- local-law routing;
- response deadlines;
- escalation;
- written templates;
- independent review where appropriate.

The company should target a response within 30 calendar days where no shorter local deadline applies and where the request is operationally feasible.

Requests involving identifiable comparator pay must be handled under applicable privacy and labor requirements.

## 10.4 Public salary comparisons

Public salary reports and employee comparisons are data points requiring review, not automatic proof of error.

HR should assess comparability of:

- role and level;
- scope;
- work location;
- worker category;
- currency date;
- total compensation;
- experience;
- performance;
- approved exceptions.

Managers must not dismiss public data, speculate about another employee’s pay, or identify named comparators. They should explain the employee’s applicable range, placement factors, review timing, and escalation process.

## 10.5 Non-retaliation

The company prohibits adverse treatment for:

- a good-faith compensation inquiry;
- lawful discussion of an employee’s own pay;
- a pay-equity concern;
- participation in an investigation;
- assistance to another employee;
- contact with a regulator, union, works council, or representative;
- exercise of a statutory or representative right.

This commitment is subject to applicable law and does not authorize disclosure of another person’s confidential information.

# 11. Pay-equity and fairness reviews

## 11.1 Scope

At least annually, and more frequently where required, the company will conduct pay-equity and progression reviews using legally permissible data.

The review should examine separately:

- base salary;
- guaranteed cash;
- target and actual bonus;
- commission;
- equity;
- retention awards;
- benefits and other relevant remuneration;
- hiring pay;
- merit increases;
- promotions;
- range placement;
- progression time.

The analysis should consider:

- job family;
- level;
- scope;
- location;
- employing entity;
- worker category;
- full-time-equivalent status;
- tenure and relevant experience;
- performance;
- management responsibility;
- variable-pay and equity eligibility;
- hiring cohort;
- approved exceptions.

## 11.2 Fairness dimensions

The company will assess:

- **vertical fairness:** consistency among comparable employees in the same role, level, and market;
- **horizontal fairness:** whether geographic differences are proportionate and defensible;
- **access fairness:** whether employees have comparable access to hiring pay, promotion, bonus, equity, development, sponsorship, and high-value assignments.

A gap within a published range is not automatically fair.

## 11.3 Data sufficiency hierarchy

Where complete data is unavailable or cannot lawfully be used:

1. Use lawful employee-level data where available.
2. Use matched comparators where sample sizes support it.
3. Use aggregate analysis with small-cell suppression.
4. Use process and access metrics where demographic data is unavailable.
5. Use qualitative case review for small populations.
6. Do not infer protected characteristics without express legal approval.
7. Mark findings as low confidence where data is incomplete.
8. Do not treat missing data as evidence that discrimination is absent.

Minimum sample sizes, suppression rules, access controls, retention, and transfer restrictions must be defined before reports are generated.

## 11.4 Remediation

Priority must be given to:

1. employees below legal or published minimums;
2. unexplained or persistent protected-group gaps;
3. misleveling or inconsistent job mapping;
4. material new-hire compression;
5. persistent below-range placement;
6. unequal access to promotion or variable compensation.

Every material finding must be classified as:

- corrected;
- scheduled for correction;
- under investigation; or
- supported by a documented explanation.

Numerical thresholds trigger review and do not define legality or fairness.

---

# 12. Automated compensation tools

Any system that recommends or influences compensation, leveling, promotion, market pricing, scarcity, or employee explanations must meet the following requirements:

- named business and technical owner;
- documented purpose and data sources;
- privacy and security review;
- bias and adverse-impact testing;
- documented model version and change history;
- reproducible outputs;
- vendor audit rights where applicable;
- human approval for every individual pay outcome;
- explainability sufficient for employee review;
- no solely automated adverse compensation decision;
- monitoring for geographic, demographic, manager, and remote-worker bias.

Opaque scarcity, performance, or potential scores may not be used as the sole justification for material pay decisions.

AI-generated employee explanations must be reviewed by trained HR personnel before delivery.

# 13. Legal, privacy, and employee-representation framework

## 13.1 Country readiness certificate

Before launch in any country, the responsible team must complete a **Country Compensation Readiness Certificate** covering:

- country and employing entity;
- worker populations;
- local addendum version;
- legal sources and review date;
- required disclosure timing and content;
- language and currency;
- employee information rights;
- consultation status;
- privacy assessment;
- payroll validation;
- contract-change analysis;
- system-testing evidence;
- employee communication approval;
- unresolved risks and mitigation owners;
- next review date.

Required sign-off must include Legal, HR, Payroll, Privacy, Employee Relations, and local leadership.

## 13.2 Local counsel items

Local counsel must review, as applicable:

- candidate and employee range disclosure;
- scope of remuneration;
- employee comparator or aggregate information rights;
- equal-pay and discrimination rules;
- pay-gap reporting;
- works-council, union, consultation, or bargaining requirements;
- changes to ranges, contracts, benefits, or incentives;
- relocation and cross-border work;
- payroll currency and minimum compensation;
- privacy, sensitive data, transfers, and retention;
- EOR, agency, and contractor treatment;
- recordkeeping;
- lawful pay discussions and retaliation protections;
- local language, format, notice, and acknowledgment requirements;
- whether geographic factors may create indirect discrimination;
- use of automated compensation tools.

No global statement should be treated as a legal conclusion for a particular jurisdiction.

## 13.3 Data governance

Compensation data must be:

- collected for defined purposes;
- limited to what is necessary;
- access-controlled;
- separated from identifying information where practical;
- aggregated or suppressed where required;
- securely transferred;
- retained only as permitted or required;
- subject to employee privacy notices where applicable;
- reviewed before use in automated tools or centralized analytics.

## 13.4 Consultation and representation

Before changing job architecture, geographic factors, ranges, promotion rules, incentive plans, compensation location, or monitoring systems, the company must determine whether consultation, information, bargaining, approval, or employee consent is required.

Managers may not announce a local change before the required process is complete.

# 16. Implementation roadmap

## Phase 1: Diagnose and prepare — 0–90 days

- Inventory all countries, entities, EORs, agencies, contractors, and employee populations.
- Map roles to the global architecture.
- Inventory current ranges, salaries, exceptions, and mobility practices.
- Conduct preliminary pay-equity, adverse-impact, and compression analysis.
- Build the country legal and consultation matrix.
- Complete privacy and data-governance assessment.
- Assess HRIS, payroll, EOR, analytics, and Employee Relations capacity.
- Pause undocumented new geographic premiums.

## Phase 2: Design and validate — 90–180 days

- Select market data sources and target percentiles.
- Establish reference ranges.
- Analyze country and metropolitan labor markets.
- Create geographic groups and factors.
- Conduct adverse-impact review.
- Model cost, retention, inflation, currency, compression, and remediation outcomes.
- Define worker-category and mobility rules.
- Create country addenda and disclosure templates.
- Determine remediation budget.
- Obtain local legal review.

## Phase 3: Consultation and remediation — 180–240 days

- Complete required consultation or bargaining.
- Correct below-minimum and priority equity cases.
- Calibrate levels and review compression.
- Configure HRIS, payroll, recruiting, reporting, and privacy controls.
- Train managers, recruiters, HR, and Employee Relations.
- Complete country readiness certificates.

## Phase 4: Controlled launch — 240–300 days

- Launch by country or legal-readiness group.
- Communicate the policy and applicable ranges.
- Update job postings, offers, and employee statements.
- Activate inquiry, appeal, and escalation processes.
- Monitor complaints, exceptions, attrition, hiring, and payroll integrity.

## Phase 5: Ongoing review

- Monthly payroll and range-integrity checks.
- Quarterly hiring, attrition, currency, opportunity, and exception reviews.
- Annual pay-equity and geographic-factor reviews.
- Annual legal and privacy review, or review after material legal change.
- Full methodology review at least every two years.
- Immediate review after a major acquisition, market disruption, public pay dispute, or material currency event.

# 17. Illustrative location examples

These examples are illustrative only. They do not establish actual company ranges, legal conclusions, or approved geographic factors.

Assume a Senior Software Engineer has a global reference midpoint of 100,000 units in the benchmark currency.

| Location | Illustrative factor | Illustrative midpoint |
|---|---:|---:|
| Warsaw | 0.78 | 78,000 |
| Lisbon | 0.82 | 82,000 |
| Toronto | 1.00 | 100,000 |
| Berlin | 1.05 | 105,000 |

Actual compensation would be set within a local range and paid in the applicable payroll currency.

### Example 1: Warsaw and Berlin

Two employees perform substantially comparable Senior Engineer work. Berlin has a higher midpoint because labor-market evidence indicates a higher recruitment and retention price.

The company must still confirm:

- consistent leveling;
- comparable scope and expectations;
- current and reliable market evidence;
- no employee below a legal or published minimum;
- no material adverse impact;
- comparable access to promotion;
- a clear explanation for the difference.

The explanation must not imply that Warsaw work is less valuable.

### Example 2: Lisbon specialist premium

A Lisbon employee is near the top of range because of scarce infrastructure skills and sustained contribution. A Berlin employee with a different scope is near the middle of the Berlin range.

The comparison must consider role family, level, scope, skill scarcity, experience, and total compensation. If similar Lisbon cases are frequent, the company must review whether the standard range or geographic methodology is inaccurate.

### Example 3: Toronto new-hire compression

A Toronto incumbent earns 92,000. A new hire receives 102,000 after presenting a competing offer.

The offer requires an exception record and incumbent review. If similar offers recur, the company must assess:

- whether the Toronto range is outdated;
- whether a scarcity factor is justified;
- whether incumbent corrections are needed;
- whether further exceptions should be limited.

The company should not automatically reduce the new hire’s pay.

### Example 4: Global recruitment with local payroll

A company recruits an engineer globally for a role requiring specialized skills and places the employee in Warsaw through an EOR. The employee competes for talent against candidates in Berlin and Toronto.

Before placement, the company must determine whether the role is:

- local-market remote;
- globally recruited remote;
- hub-aligned; or
- another approved category.

The decision must consider recruitment evidence, actual work location, payroll and tax requirements, legal entity, market competition, and internal comparators. The classification must be documented and applied consistently to comparable hires.

### Example 5: Relocation from Berlin to Warsaw

An employee approved to move permanently from Berlin to Warsaw may be assigned prospectively to the Warsaw compensation market after payroll, tax, entity, contract, and legal review.

The move does not automatically reduce existing base salary. A higher-market move also does not automatically produce an increase unless required by policy, contract, law, or an approved business decision.

### Example 6: Country not ready for launch

A country has approved salary-range templates, but consultation with an employee representative body is incomplete and the privacy assessment for pay-equity reporting is unresolved.

The country may not launch the new compensation framework until:

- the consultation process is completed or legally resolved;
- privacy controls are approved;
- local communications are finalized;
- the readiness certificate is signed.

If a mandatory disclosure deadline occurs before full implementation readiness, Legal must determine the lawful interim process.

# 18. Leadership decisions required

Leadership must record decisions on:

1. target market percentile by job family;
2. country-, city-, or market-cluster pricing;
3. maximum intended geographic spread;
4. any global minimum pay floor;
5. purchasing-power or welfare support;
6. remediation budget and deadlines;
7. treatment of EOR, agency, and contractor populations;
8. scope of total-rewards comparability;
9. mobility salary-protection periods;
10. limits on counteroffers and retention awards;
11. whether the global disclosure standard exceeds local legal minimums;
12. countries requiring staged launch;
13. data permitted to be centralized;
14. use of automated compensation tools;
15. metrics that trigger modification or replacement of the model.

Until these decisions and the required country certifications are complete, the policy is an approved design framework but not an authorization for full deployment.

## Final policy position

The company will preserve geographic pay differences where reliable labor-market evidence supports them, while limiting unexplained variation and strengthening internal fairness.

It will not use geography as an unexplained discount to employee value. It will use:

- common role and level standards;
- evidence-based market pricing;
- controlled and reviewable geographic factors;
- meaningful ranges;
- consistent progression rules;
- documented exceptions;
- regular pay-equity and opportunity reviews;
- transparent employee explanations;
- mobility and salary-protection rules;
- privacy-conscious analytics;
- local legal and consultation controls;
- independent review and appeal channels.

For Warsaw, Lisbon, Berlin, Toronto, and other markets, the company will not promise identical nominal pay or identical purchasing power. It will promise a consistent, explainable, reviewable process—and action where differences cannot be objectively supported.
