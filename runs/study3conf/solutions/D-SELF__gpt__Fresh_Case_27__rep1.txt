# 2026 Korean-Content Localization and Marketing Strategy

## 1. Executive recommendation

Approve **up to US$12 million** for a gated 2026 program focused on **incremental audience and subscriber value per localized dollar**.

The platform should:

1. **Subtitle broadly and simultaneously** in priority languages, including SDH, translated on-screen text, metadata, title pages, and core promotional assets.
2. **Dub selectively at title–language-pair level**, where local viewing convention, audience scale, retention potential, cost, cultural risk, rights, and capacity justify it.
3. **Reserve cash and studio capacity** for breakout titles, retakes, recasting, vendor failure, and post-launch demand.
4. **Adapt campaigns locally** by language, market, genre, and audience segment without erasing Korean identity.
5. **Preserve Korean social, historical, religious, and identity meaning**, allowing natural performance adaptation but not unapproved narrative substitution.
6. **Make legal viewing faster and easier to find than unofficial alternatives.**
7. Release funds through investment, launch, post-launch, and quarterly portfolio gates.

The US$12 million is an **illustrative planning envelope**, not a validated forecast. Final commitments require verified runtimes, finished-minute rates, rights, accessibility obligations, vendor capacity, subscriber economics, and title-level evidence.

---

## 2. Planning assumptions

Base case:

- Approximately 40 titles: 8 Tier 1, 12 Tier 2, and 20 Tier 3
- Mix of romance, thriller/action, comedy/family, youth, fantasy, historical, reality, and unscripted content
- Existing Korean master materials and platform infrastructure
- Licensing, baseline platform operations, and pre-existing accessibility operations excluded
- Priority subtitles available at launch
- Nonessential dubs may follow within 2–4 weeks only where piracy, genre, and audience economics permit

The budget is most sensitive to:

- Total finished minutes and episode length
- Dialogue density and cast size
- Songs, specials, recaps, and on-screen text
- Vendor rates, minimums, rush premiums, and labor obligations
- Existing licensed audio
- Territory and language rights
- Local ratings and regulatory requirements
- Vendor capacity during release peaks

If total minutes or rates exceed plan by 25%, reduce speculative dubbing and broad media first. Do not reduce simultaneous subtitle quality, cultural review, accessibility, or technical QA.

## 3. Investment model

Score each **title-language pair** from 0 to 5.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Audience impact | 25% |
| Revenue and retention potential | 20% |
| Localization cost efficiency | 15% |
| Cultural/legal risk, scored inversely | 15% |
| Title portability | 10% |
| Fandom leverage | 10% |
| Operational feasibility | 5% |

\[
\text{Priority Score}=.25A+.20R+.15C+.15(5-K)+.10P+.10F+.05O
\]

### Decision bands

| Score | Treatment |
|---:|---|
| 4.0–5.0 | Launch localization, subject to cost, rights, capacity, and quality approval |
| 3.2–3.9 | Simultaneous subtitles; dub selectively or reserve capacity |
| 2.5–3.1 | Subtitle-first; expand only after demand evidence |
| Below 2.5 | Access localization only unless strategically important |

No language receives automatic full-slate treatment. Every guaranteed language pair requires an evidence pack covering:

- Eligible audience
- Comparable-title performance
- Current audio selection and completion
- Fully loaded production cost
- Rights status
- Vendor capacity and lead time
- Cultural and legal risk
- Expected incremental contribution
- Confidence range

A language pair proceeds only if:

\[
\text{Expected incremental contribution margin}
>
\text{Fully loaded localization and marketing cost}
\times \text{risk hurdle}
\]

## 4. Subtitle-versus-dubbing tiers

### Tier 1: Tentpole

- Simultaneous priority-language subtitles and SDH
- Translated on-screen text and forced subtitles
- Localized title page, metadata, key art, trailers, and advisories
- Dubs only for approved title-language pairs
- Full campaign and creator/fandom activation
- Local-language moderation during launch
- Daily monitoring for the first 72 hours

### Tier 2: Commercial growth

- Priority subtitles, SDH, metadata, title page, and trailer
- Targeted paid media and selected creators
- Dubbing only where genre, demand, local convention, capacity, and economics support it

### Tier 3: Long tail

- Professionally reviewed subtitles, SDH, metadata, and translated on-screen text in core languages
- Editorial collections and recommendation support
- Limited paid promotion
- Dubbing only after validated demand

### Genres most likely to justify dubbing

Prioritize dubbing for:

- Family and multigenerational viewing
- Broad comedy
- Children’s content
- Reality and unscripted programming
- Mainstream action
- Accessible romance and melodrama

Favor subtitle-first treatment for:

- Prestige and arthouse titles
- Niche genres
- Highly engaged fandom titles
- Subtitle-dominant markets
- Titles with unusually complex dialect, music, or historical language unless capacity is strong

- Vietnamese

- Malaysian Malay
- English
- Polish
- Czech and Slovak
- Romanian and Hungarian
- Dutch and Nordic languages
- European Portuguese
- French North Africa
- Turkish
- Hebrew
- Hindi and selected Indian languages

Burmese, Khmer, Lao, Greek, Balkan, Persian, and additional Indian languages remain test-only within the base budget.

### Southeast Asia

- **Indonesia:** Indonesian subtitles broadly; dubbing for selected family, comedy, reality, romance, and mainstream drama.
- **Thailand:** Thai subtitles broadly; dubbing for broad romance, family, comedy, and action. Preserve politeness and status distinctions.
- **Vietnam:** Vietnamese subtitles broadly; selective dubbing. Use local review for historical, military, family, and political references.
- **Philippines:** Test Filipino/Tagalog title pages, metadata, trailers, and subtitles against English. Do not assume English or Filipino dubbing is universally superior.
- **Malaysia:** Use Malaysian Malay separately from Indonesian. Assess English, Mandarin, and Tamil discoverability where data supports it.
- **Singapore:** Treat as a high-value test market; English may be sufficient for some viewers, but local-language search and metadata can still matter.
- **Cambodia, Myanmar, Laos, Brunei:** subtitle-first access for breakout titles only under the base plan.

### Middle East and North Africa

Separate five decisions:

1. Subtitle register
2. Marketing register
3. Dub register and performance
4. Country availability and ratings
5. Mandatory legal or platform-policy edits

Use MSA subtitles as the shared baseline. Test a defined neutral/MSA dub brief against dialectal alternatives by genre and market. Do not assume one Arabic campaign or voice performance works equally in Egypt, Gulf markets, North Africa, and the Levant.

Use French subtitles and metadata selectively in Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia. Evaluate Hebrew/Israel, Turkish, and other languages separately rather than treating them as part of Arabic coverage.

### Latin America

- Maintain separate Latin American Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese adaptation, casting, and QA.
- Use one primary Latin American Spanish dub where economically justified, but adapt metadata, ratings, copy, creator selection, and advisories by country.
- Mexico, Brazil, Colombia, Argentina, Chile, Peru, and Central America should have separate marketing economics and audience baselines.
- Do not replace Korean schools, workplaces, family structures, or military institutions with Latin American equivalents.

### Europe

- **Germany, France, Italy, Spain:** subtitles broadly; selected Tier 1 and Tier 2 dubbing.
- **UK, Ireland, Nordics, Netherlands, Portugal:** premium subtitles, SDH, metadata, and editorial placement; minimal routine dubbing.
- **Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania, Greece, Balkans:** define subtitle, dubbing, or voice-over treatment by market and genre. Do not use “selective voice-over” as an undefined fallback.
- Assess India, Turkey, Israel, Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and South Africa separately. They are outside the base regional allocation unless evidence supports reallocation.

## 6. Culturally sensitive adaptation principles

### Preserve meaning; adapt performance

Allowed:

- Natural syntax and target-language rhythm
- Register changes that preserve hierarchy and intimacy
- Brief contextual explanation of Korean institutions
- Humor adaptation that preserves narrative function and power relationships
- Lip-sync changes without material meaning change
- Functional treatment of honorifics where no direct equivalent exists

Prohibited without senior escalation:

- Replacing Korean institutions, religions, workplaces, schools, or family systems with local equivalents
- Changing nationality, ethnicity, religion, class, gender identity, sexual orientation, or political position
- Mapping Korean dialect to a stereotyped foreign ethnic or rural accent
- Making dialogue more explicit, religious, conservative, informal, or political than the source
- Removing culturally meaningful conflict solely because it is unfamiliar
- Adding local political analogies or fast-dated memes that change interpretation

### Korean language controls

Every Tier 1 and sensitive Tier 2 title receives:

- Character relationship and speech-level matrix
- Honorific and terminology glossary
- Name, romanization, and pronunciation sheet
- Dialect and sociolect brief
- On-screen text inventory
- Song and music treatment plan
- Timecoded adaptation change log
- Cross-season continuity record

Terms such as *oppa*, *unnie*, *hyung*, *noona*, *sunbae*, and occupational titles may be retained, functionally translated, or explained through context. The decision must be consistent across episodes, subtitles, dubs, trailers, metadata, and social assets.

### High-risk review

Mandatory escalation applies to:

- Religion, ritual, shamanism, Christianity, Buddhism, and ancestor worship
- Race, ethnicity, foreign workers, multicultural families, and slurs
- LGBTQ+ identity and sexuality
- Gender violence, coercion, pregnancy, and reproductive health
- Disability, mental health, suicide, and self-harm
- Military service, North/South Korea, and national division
- Japanese colonial history and wartime violence
- Dictatorship, protest, labor movements, and contested history
- Alcohol, drugs, school violence, and political institutions

Use the least-distorting hierarchy:

1. Accurate translation
2. Contextual explanation
3. Rating or content advisory
4. Marketing restriction or reframing
5. Legally required edit
6. Necessary platform-policy edit

Every material edit must record its reason, market, approver, and narrative effect. Cultural review must not become automatic suppression of minority or LGBTQ+ representation.

## 7. Localization, accessibility, and QA operating model

### Subtitles

Require:

- Native target-language translator with Korean proficiency
- Independent in-country linguistic reviewer
- Subject-matter specialist for sensitive titles
- Final episodic continuity pass
- SDH, speaker identification, sound effects, and on-screen text
- Search aliases and canonical romanization
- Version control and change logs
- Tier 1 delivery at least seven days before launch

Machine assistance may support segmentation, terminology retrieval, and low-risk metadata. It may not independently produce final dialogue, humor, songs, slurs, historical, religious, sexual, or political material.

### Dubbing

Require:

- Two qualified vendors for each core dubbing language
- Paid auditions using actual title material
- Native dialogue adapter and dubbing director
- Korean pronunciation consultant
- Character-appropriate casting
- Pilot-episode approval
- Retake, recast, rush, and delayed-picture-lock provisions
- Mobile, TV, loudness, mix, and accessibility QA
- Voice continuity across seasons and promotional material
- Secure facilities and backup delivery plans

Reserve **5–10% of dubbing spend** for retakes, recasting, rush work, and vendor failure.

### Accessibility

Define obligations by market and tier:

- SDH for original and dubbed tracks where required
- Audio description for qualifying Tier 1 titles
- Captions and forced subtitles over dubbed audio
- Accessible metadata
- Audio-mix and loudness standards
- Local legal compliance

If a nonessential dub fails certification, release the approved subtitle package on time rather than delaying the title.

## 9. Marketing and transnational fandom

### Global-to-local campaign system

Central assets:

- Teasers and trailers
- Character posters
- Cast and creator interviews
- Behind-the-scenes content
- Approved synopsis and tone guidance
- Title identity and clip rights
- Localizable artwork templates

Local assets:

- Local-language title page and metadata
- Localized trailer
- Country-specific copy and ratings
- Genre-appropriate premise and character emphasis
- Creator and critic content
- Search aliases and cast-name variants
- Local content advisories

Market titles by viewer need—romance, thriller, action, family, horror, youth, reality, or prestige—not only as “Korean content.” Avoid exoticizing language and misleading local comparisons.

### Channel priorities

- **Southeast Asia:** TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Instagram, local creators, K-culture communities, campus and watch-party programs.
- **MENA:** YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, Arabic entertainment media, telco/device partners, carefully reviewed family campaigns.
- **Latin America:** TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, drama and K-culture creators, telcos, smart-TV partnerships, cast-led press.
- **Europe:** Platform editorial, YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Reddit, reviews, festivals, genre communities, and local entertainment media.

### Fandom programs

1. Multilingual cast Q&As and synchronized watch-alongs.
2. Vetted regional creator clusters.
3. Official clip packs, trivia, fan art, reactions, and spoiler controls.
4. Paid fan-advisor roles for terminology and cultural feedback.
5. Cross-title pathways into related Korean, Asian, and local-language content.

Contracts must cover disclosure, spoiler rules, rights, privacy, accurate claims, youth safety, harassment, doxxing, hate speech, synthetic content, and crisis escalation.

Measure incremental starts, completed seasons, new subscribers, retention, audience overlap, cost per qualified starter, and moderation incidents—not likes or hashtag volume alone.

## 10. Piracy and discoverability

For Tier 1 titles:

- Release core subtitles, metadata, and title aliases simultaneously.
- Publish official local-language clips quickly with rights clearance and suitable watermarking.
- Maintain canonical title IDs, alternate romanizations, cast-name variants, and genre search terms.
- Monitor unofficial subtitle and dub terminology from launch.
- Coordinate localization complaints with piracy intelligence.
- Correct material defects rapidly.
- Focus enforcement on commercial redistribution, leaks, impersonation, and harmful misinformation.
- Communicate clearly when rights delay availability in a territory.

### Service levels

- Monitoring begins within six hours of launch.
- Material defects or misleading metadata escalate within 24 hours.
- Critical language defects are corrected within four hours where technically possible.
- Search gaps, unofficial terminology, and fan demand are reviewed weekly.

The objective is not maximum takedown volume. It is to make the official product faster, more searchable, and more satisfying.

## 11. Release and investment gates

### T–24 to T–16 weeks: investment gate

- Confirm rights, runtime, episodes, songs, specials, territories, and accessibility obligations.
- Score title-language pairs.
- Approve service tiers.
- Obtain vendor rates and capacity.
- Complete glossaries, relationship matrices, and risk registers.

**No dubbing commitment without:** evidence pack, full cost, rights, capacity, quality plan, and contribution case.

### T–16 to T–10 weeks: production gate

- Lock terminology, subtitle scripts, trailer scripts, and cultural review.
- Run dubbing auditions and approve pilot episode.
- Reserve 5–10% audio contingency.
- Begin campaign, search, title-page, and clip production.

### T–10 to T–4 weeks: certification gate

- Complete recording, mixing, subtitles, metadata, ratings, and accessibility QA.
- Certify artwork, aliases, promotional assets, moderation, and crisis guidance.
- Confirm subtitle-first fallback for delayed dubs.

### Launch to D+3 days: stabilization

Monitor:

- Defects and complaints
- Audio selection and switching
- Continuation and completion
- Search gaps
- Sentiment
- Piracy
- Moderation incidents
- Customer support

### D+7 to D+28: expansion gate

Release reserve-funded dubs or additional creative only where evidence and contribution economics pass.

### Quarterly portfolio gate

Reallocate uncommitted budget across titles, languages, vendors, campaigns, and reserves.

## 12. KPIs

### Operational

| KPI | Initial target |
|---|---:|
| Tier 1 assets ready seven days before launch | ≥95% |
| Technical pass rate | ≥99.5% |
| Critical defect resolution | <4 hours |
| Material defects per Tier 1 title | <2 |
| Dubbing on-time delivery | ≥95% |
| Vendor rework rate | <8% |
| Title-language cost variance | Within ±10% |

### Commercial

Report against matched controls or pre-registered baselines:

- Incremental starts per 1,000 eligible subscribers
- Cost per qualified starter
- Cost per completed season
- Episode-one-to-two continuation
- Full-season completion
- 30- and 90-day retention
- New-subscriber conversion
- Churn reduction
- Household/co-viewing proxy
- Cross-title Korean-content viewing
- Contribution margin per title-language pair

Directional hypotheses, not commitments:

- 8–12% continuation improvement where effective local-language support is added
- 8–10% completion improvement versus matched subtitle-only launches
- 10–15% localized title-page conversion improvement
- 10% cross-title discovery improvement
- Positive contribution from at least 70% of funded Tier 1 language pairs

Each experiment must predefine:

- Eligible users
- Treatment and control
- Sample size
- Attribution window
- Confidence threshold
- Concurrent campaigns
- Stopping rule
- Scale or terminate rule

### Required experiments

- Subtitle-only versus dub-plus-subtitle
- Localized versus globally subtitled trailers
- Premise-led versus cast-led creative
- MSA versus dialectal Arabic performance
- Filipino versus English metadata and subtitles
- Creator-led versus paid-media-led discovery
- Immediate versus delayed dubbing

## 13. Risks and contingencies

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Dubbing delay | Subtitle-first fallback; reserved vendors; pre-approved scripts and casting options |
| Poor voice quality | Actual-title auditions, pilot approval, retake reserve, independent QA |
| Vendor failure | Two vendors per core language, business-continuity plans, reserve capacity |
| Cultural backlash | Qualified independent reviewers, documented least-distorting intervention, local moderation |
| Over-localization | Korean meaning-preservation policy and material-change approval |
| Under-localization | Demand-triggered dubbing and localized metadata/creative |
| Historical controversy | Korean-history consultation, fact-checked glossary, market-specific communications |
| Piracy and unofficial terminology | Simultaneous access, rapid official clips, search aliases, monitoring SLA |
| Fandom harassment | Local-language moderation, creator standards, cast-safety escalation |
| Budget fragmentation | Title-language scoring, quarterly reallocation, protected reserve |
| Capacity overload | Admission limits by vendor, QA, cultural review, and marketing throughput |
| AI or synthetic-media misuse | No synthetic voice or likeness without consent, contract, labor, and legal approval |
| Accessibility noncompliance | Territory-specific requirements, certification gate, subtitle-first fallback |
| Rights gaps | Early territory, language, song, clip, and likeness-rights validation |

## 14. Operating model and decision rights

Create a Localization Council covering:

- Content operations
- Localization
- Regional programming
- Marketing
- Product and recommendations
- Data science
- Legal and policy
- Trust and safety
- Customer support
- Local market leadership

Each title has:

- Global accountable owner
- Local market owner
- Language lead
- Cultural-risk owner
- Measurement owner

Decision rights:

- **Language and register:** language lead; global editorial approval for material changes
- **Cultural and historical treatment:** cultural reviewer, global editorial, and local owner
- **Legal and policy edits:** Legal/Policy
- **Rights and creator disputes:** Content/Legal
- **Budget release:** portfolio finance owner
- **Emergency corrections:** localization operations lead
- **Final release authorization:** global content operations owner after certification

Reviewers must be qualified, compensated, conflict-checked, and empowered to escalate. Disagreements must be documented and retained for audit.

### Capacity dashboard

Track quarterly:

- Finished minutes by language
- Vendor utilization
- Reserve capacity
- QA and cultural-review backlog
- Titles entering each gate
- Launch conflicts
- Rejection and rework rates
- Average correction time
- On-time delivery

No title may enter a release window if localization, QA, cultural review, accessibility, marketing, and moderation teams cannot certify it on time.

## Final recommendation

Approve up to **US$12 million on a gated basis**.

The non-negotiable priorities are:

1. Simultaneous, accurate subtitles and discoverability.
2. Premium dubbing for economically justified Latin American Spanish, Brazilian Portuguese, Arabic, Indonesian, Thai, German, French, Italian, and European Spanish title-language pairs.
3. Language-specific Southeast Asian and European execution.
4. Strong Korean honorific, dialect, historical, religious, identity, and humor safeguards.
5. Real vendor capacity, accessibility, QA, piracy response, and contingency.
6. Paid, disclosed, moderated, and privacy-compliant fandom activation.
7. Causal measurement of acquisition, completion, retention, and contribution.

The platform should not maximize the number of dubbed languages. It should maximize **incremental audience and subscriber value per localized dollar**, while ensuring Korean content remains understandable, culturally credible, technically reliable, and timely across markets.
