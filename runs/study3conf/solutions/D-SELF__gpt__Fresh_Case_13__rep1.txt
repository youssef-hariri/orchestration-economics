# Climate-Linked Microinsurance Distribution Strategy for a 2026 Pilot

## 1. Executive decision

Use a **common minimum viable operating platform** with differentiated regional propositions. Do not launch one product or channel model across East Africa, South Asia, and Central America.

| Region | Lead distribution model | Initial product | Secondary channels | Pilot priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| East Africa | Mobile-assisted, locally validated | Simple seasonal parametric cover | Cooperatives, savings groups, community organizations | Test reach, payment reliability, and basis risk |
| South Asia | Trusted-institution-assisted | Hybrid index product with tightly defined review layer | Mobile agents, producer groups, rural finance institutions | Test comprehension, fairness, and renewal |
| Central America | Voluntary embedded distribution | Parametric drought/excess-rain cover linked to finance or inputs | Standalone cooperatives, agents, fintechs, community groups | Test economics while preventing coercion and concentration |

The commercial objective is not the lowest initial acquisition cost. It is:

> **Positive lifetime contribution per retained policy, with credible customer value and controlled catastrophe exposure.**

A low-cost digital sale is unattractive if it produces poor comprehension, failed payouts, complaints, lapses, or reputational damage after a basis-risk event. A higher-cost assisted sale may be preferable if it improves collection, renewal, and claims credibility.

---

## 2. Scope, assumptions, and evidence standards

### 2.1 What is known

The following are general industry considerations, not assumptions about every country:

- Mobile money can reduce collection and payout friction, but mobile access does not guarantee insurance comprehension or independent account access.
- Parametric insurance can reduce conventional loss-adjustment costs and accelerate payment, but it does not eliminate data, conduct, payment, or grievance risk.
- Basis risk—the index payout not matching actual farm loss—is a principal threat to customer trust and renewal.
- Cooperatives and producer organizations can aggregate demand, but their legitimacy, governance, and operating quality vary.
- Religious and community organizations may be effective for education and referral but are not automatically licensed or suitable to sell or adjudicate insurance.
- Bundling with credit, inputs, or agricultural services can reduce acquisition cost but may create coercion, lender-capture, and concentration risks.
- Smallholder climate losses are correlated by geography, crop, season, and peril. Policy count alone is not diversification.
- Regulation of index insurance, electronic consent, commissions, mobile distribution, data use, and bundled products is country-specific.

### 2.2 Working assumptions to validate

The pilot should test rather than assume that:

- Basic-phone access is materially greater than smartphone access.
- Farmers prefer simple seasonal products to broad multi-peril products.
- A transparent first payout improves renewal more than a lower initial price.
- Local institutions improve comprehension and perceived legitimacy enough to justify their added cost.
- Data is sufficient for selected regional perils but not necessarily for farm-level indemnity insurance.
- Seasonal or installment payments are more suitable than monthly payments for farmers with irregular income.

### 2.3 Conditional planning ranges

The following are **budgeting scenarios only**, not market facts or recommended prices.

| Variable | Indicative planning range |
|---|---:|
| Acquisition cost per issued policy | US$0.50–8+, depending on channel and assistance |
| Servicing and payment cost per active policy | US$0.25–3+ |
| Indicative annual premium | US$3–20+, depending on insured amount, peril, subsidy, and reinsurance |
| Clean parametric payout | Approximately 2–14 days where data, identity, and payment rails work |
| Hybrid or reviewed payment | Approximately 21–45 days |
| Voluntary renewal | Must be established by pilot; no universal target is assumed |

All figures must be replaced by country-specific quotations, regulatory costs, partner terms, and observed pilot data.

# 3. Regional recommendations

## 3.1 East Africa: mobile-assisted and locally validated

### Recommended proposition

Use mobile-network agents, USSD, voice, and mobile wallets for reach, enrollment, collection, and payment. Use cooperatives, savings groups, or agricultural aggregators for:

- farmer and crop identification;
- explanation of the index;
- planting-window confirmation;
- customer support;
- complaint escalation.

Local partners should not control technical claims decisions.

### Initial product

A simple seasonal parametric product covering one crop and one or two measurable perils, such as rainfall deficit, excess rainfall, soil moisture, or extreme heat, subject to country-level data validation.

### Required customer journey

1. Farmer receives a local-language explanation, including a payout and a non-payout example.
2. Enrollment occurs through USSD, voice, agent tablet, or assisted digital service.
3. Crop, location, planting window, and beneficiary details are validated.
4. Premium is paid seasonally or in a limited number of installments.
5. Trigger data and the payout calculation are communicated through SMS, voice, radio, agents, and local groups.
6. Payout is sent to a verified, active wallet or alternative account.
7. Complaints are handled independently of the sales channel.

### Main risks and controls

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Agent presents insurance as guaranteed compensation | Certification, scripted explanations, recorded consent, mystery shopping, complaint-linked clawbacks |
| Wallet is inactive or belongs to another person | Pre-season account verification and exception-handling process |
| Farmers cannot read or act on SMS | Voice, agent, radio, and group communication |
| Regional index does not reflect farm-level loss | Smaller validated zones, crop and planting-date calibration, sampled field checks |
| Telecom or wallet concentration | Counterparty and channel exposure limits; alternative payment routes |
| Drought creates correlated losses | District, crop, peril, and season limits; catastrophe reinsurance |

### Pilot priority

First test:

- mobile self-service;
- agent-assisted enrollment;
- cooperative-validated mobile enrollment.

Measure not only conversion, but independent comprehension, active payout-account rates, collection, failed payments, complaints, and renewal.

## 3.2 South Asia: assisted, institution-led, and basis-risk-conscious

### Recommended proposition

Prioritize cooperatives, farmer producer organizations, self-help groups, and appropriately licensed rural finance institutions. Use mobile channels mainly for records, communications, payments, and renewals rather than assuming app-led self-service.

### Initial product

Begin with one major crop, one growing period, and one or two clearly defined monsoon-related perils. Use a hybrid product where the index-to-loss relationship is weak or farmers are likely to expect local loss verification.

### Hybrid structure

1. **Automatic index layer:** rapid payment after a defined regional trigger.
2. **Targeted review layer:** investigates data failure or statistically significant clusters of severe mismatch.
3. **Contractual review benefit:** pays according to documented eligibility and funding rules.

The review process should not require a conventional loss assessment for every policy, or the product may lose its cost and speed advantages.

### Required customer journey

- Pre-season group or cooperative education.
- Individual policy certificate even where enrollment is collective.
- Clear statement of insured crop, location, cover period, trigger, benefit, exclusions, and complaint process.
- Payment through bank, wallet, or permitted assisted cash-out route.
- Independent complaint and basis-risk review.
- Post-season explanation comparing observed climate conditions, index values, and payouts.

### Main risks and controls

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Farmers expect farm-level indemnity | Explain index basis with severe-loss and no-loss scenarios before purchase |
| Group or lender mis-selling | Individual consent, policy certificates, independent callbacks, partner audits |
| Landowner or lender controls benefit | Record cultivator and beneficiary status explicitly |
| Subsidy creates unrealistic price expectations | Test full, partial, and no-subsidy willingness to pay |
| Dense farming creates visible basis risk | Fine-scale data, field sampling, and zone-level product review |
| Local institution influences claims | Separate sales, technical assessment, payment, and grievance functions |
| Women, tenants, or low-literacy farmers are excluded | Voice, visual, local-language, and assisted channels |

### Pilot priority

Compare:

- group-assisted index cover;
- group-assisted hybrid cover;
- digitally supported payment and renewal.

Evaluation should include whether the added hybrid cost improves perceived fairness, collection, and renewal.

## 3.3 Central America: embedded but voluntary and farmer-protective

### Recommended proposition

Use cooperatives, rural banks, credit unions, producer organizations, and agricultural lenders for embedded distribution. Maintain standalone cooperative, agent, or digital options for farmers outside formal finance and value chains.

### Initial product

A crop- and geography-specific parametric product covering drought, excess rain, heat, flood, or cyclone risk where the data and index-to-loss relationship are defensible.

### Required customer journey

1. Farmer receives a separate insurance quotation at loan or input purchase.
2. Premium and insurance benefits are shown separately from finance or input pricing.
3. Farmer provides affirmative consent.
4. Policy certificate is delivered directly to the farmer.
5. Trigger information is available through branch, SMS, messaging, agent, and community channels.
6. Payout is made to the farmer unless a separately disclosed and legally valid creditor interest applies.
7. Standalone purchase remains available where legally and commercially appropriate.

### Main risks and controls

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Insurance is treated as compulsory | Separate consent, disclosure, and receipt; monitor opt-out and complaint rates |
| Lender captures the payout | Farmer-first payout policy; transparent creditor-interest terms |
| One lender, buyer, or cooperative creates accumulation | Limits by partner, municipality, crop, peril, and season |
| Remote or indigenous farmers are excluded | Local-language voice, community, branch, and non-digital options |
| Product protects lender more than farmer | Measure farmer liquidity, benefit, comprehension, and renewal separately |
| Coverage ends when the loan ends | Test standalone renewal and continuation outside the embedded relationship |

### Pilot priority

Compare:

- voluntary embedded lender or cooperative cover;
- standalone cooperative cover;
- agent or digital access for farmers outside formal institutions.

# 4. Fair assessment of distribution channels

| Channel | Comparative strengths | Economic profile | Claims credibility contribution | Main risks | Recommended role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cooperatives and producer organizations | Aggregation, local knowledge, farmer identification, group education | Moderate acquisition cost; potentially lower cost per retained policy | Strong explanation and local escalation, but not technical adjudication | Governance failure, political capture, concentration, leakage | Primary or validation channel depending on region |
| Mobile-network agents | Reach, cash conversion, assisted enrollment, payment access | Potentially lower marginal cost after scale | Moderate; depends heavily on training and customer support | Mis-selling, turnover, weak comprehension, telecom dependence | Primary in selected East African markets; secondary elsewhere |
| Religious/community groups | Social legitimacy, education, hard-to-reach access | Higher supervision and education costs | Strong social communication; limited technical value | Coercion, favoritism, sectarian exclusion, unlicensed activity | Education, referral, aggregation, and grievance escalation |
| Fintech apps | Low marginal cost after integration, automated renewal, transaction data | Potentially very low acquisition cost for active digital users; high integration and churn risk | Limited unless supported by education and service | Digital exclusion, low engagement, app churn, fraud | Selective segment channel, not default rural distribution |
| Rural banks and lenders | Existing records, collection, embedded access | Efficient acquisition and collection | Institutional credibility varies; risk of lender-capture | Forced bundling, concentration, payout redirection | Central America primary; selected South Asian use |
| NGOs and agricultural service providers | Education, resilience services, access to excluded groups | Often higher program and supervision cost | Strong education and community support | Funding dependence, unclear accountability | Selective education and resilience partner |

### Channel decision rule

Evaluate each channel using:

- cost per retained policy;
- contribution after all variable costs;
- comprehension;
- collection and payment reliability;
- complaint and mis-selling rates;
- inclusion;
- basis-risk satisfaction;
- concentration exposure.

No channel should be approved solely because it has the lowest cost per enrollment.

# 5. Business-model options

## Option A: Mobile-assisted parametric

**Best use:** selected East African markets and digitally capable segments elsewhere.

**Advantages**

- Broad reach.
- Lower marginal collection cost after integration.
- Rapid payment potential.
- Basic-phone compatibility if designed correctly.

**Constraints**

- Education and customer support remain necessary.
- Payment-account failure can undermine the claims experience.
- Digital exclusion may be hidden by aggregate enrollment.
- Requires telecom, payment, and agent governance.

**Scale condition**

Positive contribution must remain after integration, agent supervision, payment failures, customer support, complaints, and basis-risk remediation.

---

## Option B: Trusted-intermediary group model

**Best use:** South Asia and low-literacy or low-trust populations.

**Advantages**

- Stronger education and social legitimacy.
- Lower cost per customer when groups are active and well governed.
- Potentially better collection and renewal.

**Constraints**

- Higher training, audit, and supervision cost.
- Intermediaries may misrepresent policy terms.
- Group concentration and governance risk.
- Individual customer rights can be obscured.

**Scale condition**

The cost per retained policy must compare favorably with digital alternatives, not merely the cost per issued policy.

---

## Option C: Embedded credit or input protection

**Best use:** Central America and structured agricultural value chains.

**Advantages**

- Efficient customer acquisition and collection.
- Existing crop, location, and repayment data.
- Potential benefit to both farmer liquidity and agricultural credit portfolios.

**Constraints**

- Forced or misunderstood bundling.
- Lender control of payout.
- High exposure concentration.
- Low voluntary engagement and weak standalone renewal.

**Scale condition**

Affirmative consent, farmer-first benefit, acceptable voluntary renewal, and partner exposure limits must be demonstrated.

---

## Option D: Multi-channel open architecture

**Best use:** after initial product-market evidence.

Use a common policy, data, claims, payments, and reporting layer with different customer interfaces and partner models.

**Advantages**

- Reduces dependence on one distributor.
- Enables fair channel comparison.
- Supports regional adaptation.

**Constraints**

- Greater technology and governance complexity.
- Channel conflict and data-integration costs.
- Risk of building infrastructure before proving demand.

**Recommendation**

Do not build a full regional platform before the pilot. Start with a minimum viable shared platform and add APIs, automated renewals, and advanced analytics only after evidence supports scale.

---

# 6. Product and claims architecture

## 6.1 Initial product design principles

Start with:

- one major crop;
- one growing period;
- one or two measurable perils;
- a simple payout schedule;
- a fixed maximum benefit;
- clear exclusions;
- mobile or account-based payment;
- local-language explanations;
- independent claims and grievance authority.

Do not initially combine drought, flood, pest, disease, price, livestock, input failure, and credit default in one product.

## 6.2 Parametric claims process

1. Independent data provider calculates the index.
2. Data completeness, anomalies, and revisions are checked.
3. A version-controlled trigger algorithm is run.
4. Results are independently reviewed and recorded.
5. Payout files are reconciled with policy and beneficiary records.
6. Customers receive the index value, threshold, payout percentage, payout amount, and payment date.
7. Failed payments are actively followed up.
8. Customers can challenge identity, location, data, or calculation errors.

The policy must specify:

- trigger authority;
- treatment of missing or revised data;
- handling of conflicting data sources;
- data-provider failure;
- algorithm changes;
- payment failure;
- customer identity and location disputes.

## 6.3 Hybrid review process

Use targeted review only for:

- data gaps or suspected data error;
- severe-loss clusters with material index mismatch;
- statistically significant complaints;
- regulator-directed review;
- pre-defined basis-risk escalation.

Use independent enumerators, satellite and vegetation data, geotagged imagery, and sampled field observations. Pay undisputed amounts first.

## 6.4 Basis-risk remedy

For the initial pilot, use one of the following clearly defined structures—not an informal promise:

1. **Contractual top-up:** a capped, priced, funded benefit with documented eligibility.
2. **Separate resilience or mutual fund:** legally distinct from the insurance policy and not represented as guaranteed insurance payment.
3. **No top-up:** transparent index-only cover with active measurement of basis risk.

The recommended default is **no discretionary hardship promise in the first pilot**. If a top-up is needed to achieve credibility, design it as a separately governed and actuarially limited layer before launch.

# 7. Acquisition, affordability, and unit economics

## 7.1 Full contribution model

Evaluate every region-channel-product combination using:

\[
\text{Lifetime contribution}
=
\text{collected premiums}
-\text{claims}
-\text{reinsurance}
-\text{acquisition}
-\text{commissions}
-\text{payment fees}
-\text{servicing}
-\text{fraud controls}
-\text{complaints and remediation}
-\text{capital costs}
\]

Include:

- insured amount and benefit limits;
- expected loss cost;
- uncertainty and catastrophe load;
- reinsurance;
- partner commissions;
- technology integration;
- payment fees and failed payments;
- education and supervision;
- customer service;
- fraud and data controls;
- taxes and regulatory levies;
- capital consumption.

### Required decision outputs

For each channel, calculate:

- contribution per issued policy;
- contribution per retained policy;
- break-even conversion rate;
- break-even renewal rate;
- maximum acquisition cost;
- payback period;
- subsidy sensitivity;
- severe-event capital requirement.

## 7.3 Payment design

Test:

- one seasonal payment;
- two or three installments;
- deduction at crop sale;
- loan or input-linked collection;
- wallet auto-debit.

Monthly payment should not be assumed to be optimal. It may improve affordability but increase transaction, lapse, and recovery costs.

## 7.4 Subsidy policy

Treat subsidies as transition support, not permanent commercial foundations. Test full, partial, and no-subsidy purchase and renewal behavior.

Public or development support should preferably fund:

- data infrastructure;
- farmer education;
- resilience measures;
- reinsurance or catastrophe layers;
- basis-risk reduction.

# 8. Partner governance and incentives

## 8.1 Partner approval scorecard

Approve partners only after assessing:

- active, verified membership or customer base;
- farmer trust and perceived legitimacy;
- governance and political-capture risk;
- complaint and mis-selling history;
- geographic and customer reach;
- payment and data capability;
- regulatory eligibility;
- language and inclusion capacity;
- concentration contribution;
- independence from technical claims decisions.

### Proposed minimum partner evidence

Each partner should demonstrate:

- verified active customer or member records;
- trained and certified personnel;
- ability to explain a payout and non-payout scenario;
- complaint escalation capacity;
- secure data handling;
- acceptable reconciliation and audit performance;
- no material unresolved conduct concerns.

Exact thresholds should be set by the insurer’s compliance and risk functions after country diligence.

## 8.2 Compensation

Use staged compensation:

- valid enrollment payment;
- premium-collection payment;
- persistency or renewal payment;
- separate education and grievance-service fee;
- clawback for invalid enrollment or material mis-selling;
- no penalty for a legitimate claim being paid.

An illustrative structure is 40–60% at valid enrollment, 20–30% after collection, and 20–30% at renewal. These percentages are design options, not universal standards.

## 8.3 Role separation

- Intermediaries may educate, enroll, collect, and support complaints.
- Data providers calculate the index.
- The insurer or designated technical committee controls trigger approval.
- Payment providers execute payouts.
- An independent grievance function handles complaints.
- No partner should control both sales incentives and technical claims outcomes.

# 9. Data and model governance

## 9.1 Data requirements by stage

| Stage | Minimum evidence required |
|---|---|
| Pilot design | Historical climate data, preliminary crop-loss relationship, payment and location coverage assessment |
| Pilot launch | Defined trigger, data-provider agreement, completeness controls, beneficiary and location validation |
| Pilot continuation | Observed field-loss sample, trigger reproducibility, payment performance, complaint and basis-risk analysis |
| Commercial scale | Independent model validation, approved data governance, capital and reinsurance support, stable data performance |

## 9.2 Required controls

Maintain:

- data provenance and ownership records;
- quality, completeness, and latency tests;
- version-controlled algorithms;
- trigger and payout audit trails;
- rules for missing, revised, or conflicting data;
- independent annual validation;
- change approval for data-provider or methodology changes;
- liability and service standards in data-provider contracts.

Farmer-reported observations may support anomaly detection and basis-risk analysis but should not determine aggregate payouts without robust controls.

---

# 10. Portfolio risk, adverse selection, and fraud

## 10.1 Accumulation and reinsurance

Diversify by:

- region and country;
- crop and peril;
- season and planting window;
- geography, soil, and elevation;
- channel and partner;
- lender, buyer, and cooperative;
- index and payment provider.

Use layered risk transfer:

1. insurer retention for ordinary losses;
2. quota-share or surplus reinsurance during early scale;
3. catastrophe excess-of-loss for severe regional events;
4. public, development-finance, or contingent-capital support for systemic events where appropriate.

Before launch, define:

- event windows;
- retention;
- probable maximum loss methodology;
- attachment points;
- aggregate and occurrence limits;
- reinstatements;
- exclusions;
- recovery timing;
- counterparty requirements.

## 10.2 Concentration controls

Set maximum exposure by:

- district or municipality;
- grid cell;
- crop;
- peril;
- season;
- partner;
- index provider;
- payment provider.

Stress-test severe drought, excess rain, flood, cyclone, simultaneous multi-country events, data outage, payment-network failure, reinsurer delay, and severe basis-risk clusters.

## 10.3 Adverse selection controls

For voluntary cover, test and, where lawful, apply:

- enrollment cutoffs before weather information becomes unusually informative;
- late-enrollment restrictions;
- minimum group participation requirements;
- crop and location validation;
- planting-window confirmation;
- limits on post-enrollment changes;
- risk-zone eligibility and pricing;
- monitoring of enrollment timing and prior loss history.

Embedded products reduce adverse selection but can create low engagement and forced enrollment. Both effects must be measured.

## 10.4 Fraud controls

Assign control ownership across the insurer, partner, payment provider, and data vendor. Monitor:

- duplicate identities and policies;
- fictitious or impossible locations;
- false crop declarations;
- agent-created policies;
- payout-account substitution;
- unauthorized policy changes;
- field-assessment collusion;
- duplicate enrollment across channels.

Use proportionate controls: anomaly detection, geolocation checks, random audits, account verification, and targeted field sampling. The control budget must be consistent with the low-premium product economics.

---

# 11. Minimum viable platform

Build only the shared capabilities required for the pilot:

- policy and customer records;
- partner and agent management;
- USSD, voice, SMS, and basic digital interfaces;
- payment integration;
- trigger and payout calculation;
- data audit trail;
- complaints and case management;
- exposure monitoring;
- reinsurance reporting;
- consent and policy-document storage.

Defer broader regional APIs, advanced automated renewals, and extensive data marketplaces until pilot evidence demonstrates demand and unit-economics viability.

The shared platform should be common; customer-facing language, education, payment frequency, partner roles, and grievance design should remain regional.

# 12. Pilot design

## 12.1 Country selection

Select initial countries using a scored screen covering:

- regulatory readiness;
- mobile and payment coverage;
- target farmer concentration;
- partner quality;
- climate-data reliability;
- availability of local loss observations;
- reinsurance appetite;
- political and operational stability;
- inclusion requirements;
- concentration risk.

Do not treat the regional label as sufficient for launch approval.

## 12.2 Channel comparisons

### East Africa

- mobile self-service;
- agent-assisted;
- cooperative-validated mobile enrollment.

### South Asia

- group-assisted index;
- group-assisted hybrid;
- digitally supported payment and renewal.

### Central America

- voluntary embedded lender or cooperative;
- standalone cooperative;
- agent or digital access outside formal institutions.

## 12.3 Experimental design

Before launch, specify:

- unit of randomization or matching: farmer, group, cooperative, lender, or geography;
- sample-size and statistical-power approach;
- subsidy treatment;
- contamination controls where customers can access multiple channels;
- independent evaluator;
- survey schedule;
- control-group protections;
- approach for a season with no trigger.

If no live climate event occurs, use historical back-testing, simulated payout exercises, comprehension testing, and observed collection and renewal evidence. Do not treat a no-event season as proof of claims credibility.

## 12.4 Pilot stages

### Stage 1: design and diligence

Confirm regulatory permissions, partner suitability, data quality, payment capability, product comprehension, pricing, and reinsurance.

### Stage 2: one- to two-season controlled pilot

Launch only a narrow product and measure channel, product, customer, technical, and portfolio outcomes separately.

### Stage 3: selective scale

Scale only the region-channel-product combinations that pass the relevant gates below. Do not scale all channels merely because the platform supports them.

# 13. Executive scale-gate scorecard

Use a concise scorecard for each region and channel.

| Gate | Scale requirement |
|---|---|
| Customer comprehension | Customers can correctly explain trigger, benefit, and principal exclusions |
| Payment reliability | Verified payout accounts, collection, and failed-payment rates are within approved limits |
| Claims credibility | Payout calculation is independently reproducible and understandable |
| Basis risk | Severe-loss non-payment and paid-without-loss outcomes remain within approved tolerances |
| Conduct | No material coercion, mis-selling, unauthorized enrollment, or discriminatory exclusion |
| Economics | Positive contribution after claims, reinsurance, acquisition, servicing, complaints, fraud, and capital costs |
| Persistency | Renewal or retention is acceptable for the specific voluntary, embedded, or assisted product |
| Technical data | Data completeness, latency, revisions, and model performance meet approved standards |
| Portfolio risk | Exposure remains within capital, catastrophe, counterparty, and reinsurance limits |
| Regulatory readiness | Product, intermediary, data, consent, payment, and grievance arrangements are compliant |

Suggested management thresholds may be set after baseline measurement, rather than imposed universally. Any proposed thresholds should be segmented by product type, payout experience, subsidy, country, and channel.

# 15. Go/no-go decision tree

## Go to pilot only if

- The product and intermediary roles are legally permissible.
- A verified payment route exists.
- The trigger has a defensible preliminary relationship with observed loss, or the pilot is explicitly designed to test it.
- Customers can receive and understand the policy explanation.
- An independent complaints function is operational.
- Partner conduct, training, and audit controls are in place.
- Exposure limits and catastrophe scenarios are approved.
- Reinsurance or approved capital support is arranged.
- Actual farmer, crop, location, and beneficiary can be identified with acceptable confidence.
- The pilot budget includes failed payments, complaints, remediation, and basis-risk monitoring.

## Stop or redesign if

- The index-to-loss relationship is materially weak.
- The payment route cannot reliably reach intended beneficiaries.
- The product cannot be explained in local languages and usable formats.
- The intermediary is unlicensed or controls technical claims.
- Embedded insurance is effectively compulsory without compliant consent.
- A top-up or hardship mechanism is unfunded, discretionary, or legally unclear.
- Expected contribution is negative in the base case without committed support.
- Concentration exceeds approved catastrophe limits.
- Reinsurance is unavailable or does not cover modeled accumulation.
- Data quality or latency makes payout timing unreliable.

## Scale only if

- Customer comprehension and payout credibility are demonstrated.
- Contribution is positive after full cost allocation.
- Collection, payment, and renewal are sustainable.
- Basis risk is within approved tolerances.
- No material conduct or inclusion failures are identified.
- Data and trigger governance are independently validated.
- Portfolio exposure remains within capital and reinsurance capacity.

---

# 16. Final recommendation

### East Africa

Proceed first with **mobile-assisted parametric cover**, supported by cooperatives or savings groups. Prioritize basic-phone access, verified wallets, voice communication, rapid transparent payouts, and district-level drought accumulation controls.

### South Asia

Proceed with **assisted institutional distribution** and a narrowly scoped hybrid product where basis risk is likely to be visible. Invest in local-language education, individual policy certificates, cultivator identification, independent grievances, and sampled field validation.

### Central America

Proceed with **voluntary embedded cover** through cooperatives and rural finance institutions, while maintaining standalone options. Protect farmer payout rights, prevent forced bundling, and cap exposure by lender, buyer, municipality, crop, and peril.

## Enterprise position

Build a **minimum viable common platform** for administration, trigger governance, payments, complaints, exposure monitoring, and reinsurance reporting. Do not standardize customer-facing distribution or product design across regions.

The recommended strategy is:

> **Mobile-assisted and locally validated in East Africa; institutionally assisted and basis-risk-conscious in South Asia; embedded but consumer-protected in Central America.**

The pilot should scale only where the insurer can demonstrate the combined presence of:

**credible customer value, understandable triggers, reliable payments, controlled intermediation, positive lifetime economics, sound data governance, and disciplined climate-risk transfer.**
