# Global Labor-Relations Strategy for a Delivery Platform — 2026

## Executive mandate

The platform should adopt a **globally consistent labor-relations framework with jurisdiction-specific implementation**. It should not impose one worldwide employment status, contracting model, or representation structure.

Riders may be employees, dependent contractors, statutory platform workers, genuine independent contractors, fleet-partner workers, or participants in informal collectives. Terms such as “rider” and “platform participant” are operational descriptions only; they do not determine legal status or representative authority.

The global framework should establish minimum standards for:

- freedom of association and lawful collective activity;
- non-retaliation and non-interference;
- safety, transparency, and due process;
- human review of significant adverse decisions;
- privacy and data minimization; and
- timely, meaningful engagement.

Local law, collective agreements, statutory rights, and more protective requirements must prevail. **The company should obtain qualified local counsel’s advice before recognizing a representative, launching a forum, changing pay or algorithms, implementing a classification model, responding to collective action, or making worker-facing legal-status statements. Regulatory developments should be monitored continuously, not only during annual reviews.**

---

# 1. Global principles

## 1.1 Freedom of association

The company should not interfere with a person’s lawful right to:

- form, join, or decline to join a union, association, cooperative, council, or other representative body;
- elect or appoint representatives;
- participate in lawful information, consultation, bargaining, or collective action;
- raise individual or collective concerns; or
- act as a representative.

Union membership or collective affiliation should not be requested unless there is a lawful, necessary, proportionate, and documented purpose.

## 1.2 Non-retaliation and non-interference

No rider or representative should be disadvantaged because of lawful organizing, representation, consultation, bargaining, safety reporting, complaints, or protected collective activity.

Prohibited conduct includes:

- intimidation, threats, coercion, or inducements intended to discourage organization;
- monitoring organizing or private representative communications;
- selective enforcement against organizers;
- discriminatory suspension, deactivation, or reduced access to work;
- management-sponsored or management-selected representative bodies;
- using voluntary forums to fragment or displace formal representation; and
- describing lawful collective activity as fraud, abuse, or security misconduct without documented facts.

Violence, credible threats, fraud, cybersecurity violations, property damage, and other unlawful conduct may be addressed through neutral enforcement. Any adverse action closely following organizing or collective activity should receive enhanced review.

## 1.3 Accurate, status-neutral communications

The company should avoid unsupported global statements such as:

- “all riders are contractors”;
- “contractors cannot organize”;
- “all councils are advisory”; or
- “consultation does not affect management decisions.”

Worker-facing and representative-facing communications concerning classification, representation rights, bargaining authority, codes, benefits, minimum earnings, consultation, or company commitments should receive local legal and communications review.

## 1.4 Local law controls

Each country should maintain a current legal playbook addressing:

- worker categories and classification indicators;
- unions, works councils, employee committees, platform-worker bodies, safety committees, and associations;
- recognition, election, consultation, bargaining, and co-determination rules;
- collective-action protections and restrictions;
- pay, working-time, social-security, tax, insurance, and safety requirements;
- algorithmic-management and monitoring obligations;
- privacy and data-processing requirements;
- competition-law constraints;
- subcontracting and joint-liability rules; and
- labor-authority and dispute-resolution procedures.

Each conclusion should identify its legal source, effective date, covered population, and next review date.

## 1.5 Transparency and due process

Subject to lawful confidentiality and trade-secret protections, the company should provide accessible information about:

- pay calculations, fees, deductions, and incentives;
- dispatch, batching, ranking, and order allocation;
- ratings and performance systems;
- monitoring and location-data use;
- suspension and deactivation grounds;
- appeal and human-review procedures;
- safety, insurance, and accident support; and
- material platform changes.

Information should be timely, comprehensible, multilingual where appropriate, and accessible.

## 1.6 Proportionate operational control

Management may generally retain authority over safety, fraud, cybersecurity, identity integrity, customer protection, service quality, technology, pricing, and commercial relationships.

That authority remains subject to applicable:

- information and consultation duties;
- bargaining or co-determination;
- due-process requirements;
- privacy and monitoring rules;
- safety and anti-discrimination obligations;
- restructuring, notice, and severance requirements; and
- collective agreements.

A matter described as an “operational decision” may still require consultation or bargaining.

# 2. Commitment hierarchy

Each local playbook should classify commitments as:

1. **Legal obligations** — imposed by law, regulation, court order, regulator, or collective agreement.
2. **Global non-negotiables** — mandatory company standards applied worldwide.
3. **Local negotiated commitments** — collective agreements, consultation protocols, settlements, or other binding arrangements.
4. **Public or worker-facing commitments** — statements that may create contractual, regulatory, investor, consumer-protection, or reliance risk.
5. **Discretionary programs or pilots** — time-limited initiatives with defined scope, funding, review, and withdrawal rules.

| Required field | Content |
|---|---|
| Commitment | Exact obligation or promise |
| Source | Law, agreement, policy, statement, or pilot |
| Status | Legal, company-mandatory, contractual, or discretionary |
| Covered population | Legal category, city, country, or region |
| Responsible entity | Local entity and accountable executive |
| Timing | Deadline, frequency, and duration |
| Consequence of breach | Legal, contractual, operational, or reputational exposure |
| Approved wording | Worker-facing and representative-facing language |
| Review date | Date for reassessment |

Calling a commitment “voluntary” does not eliminate potential legal, contractual, regulatory, investor, consumer, or reliance consequences.

# 3. Engagement architecture

## 3.1 Individual voice

Every rider should have access to:

- in-app support;
- earnings and transaction explanations;
- account-status notices;
- human review of significant deactivation decisions;
- safety reporting and emergency assistance;
- accessibility and accommodation channels;
- confidential retaliation reporting;
- appeals and correction procedures; and
- periodic surveys.

These channels do not replace collective representation.

## 3.2 Formal representative institutions

The company should distinguish among:

- unions;
- statutory works councils;
- employee committees;
- platform-worker representative bodies;
- health-and-safety committees;
- dependent-contractor associations;
- sectoral or territorial bargaining bodies;
- European Works Councils, where applicable; and
- voluntary advisory forums.

Each local playbook should specify each body’s:

- constituency;
- selection or election method;
- geographic and worker-category scope;
- information rights;
- consultation rights;
- bargaining authority;
- co-determination or approval rights;
- confidentiality rights; and
- authority to bind represented persons.

A works council may have consultation or co-determination rights without bargaining over wages. A union may not represent every rider. A voluntary forum has no bargaining authority merely because the company meets with it.

### Mandate and authority matrix

| Body | Constituency | Selection method | Information rights | Consultation rights | Bargaining authority | Approval/co-determination | Signing authority | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Verification should be proportionate to the specific engagement. It should not require membership lists, disclosure of union affiliation, or repeated proof of authority. Verification requests should be made once, in writing, with the purpose explained. A mandate dispute should not justify indefinite delay; non-confidential interim dialogue may continue where appropriate.

## 3.3 Worker Voice Forums

A Worker Voice Forum may supplement formal representation where local law permits. Before launch, local counsel should confirm that it will not:

- replace a statutory representative;
- divide or weaken an existing bargaining unit;
- interfere with organizing;
- create an employer-dominated body; or
- misrepresent participants’ legal status.

Typical features include:

- quarterly meetings, with additional meetings for material changes;
- advance agendas and accessible materials;
- neutral participation rules;
- written minutes and action trackers;
- management responses within defined deadlines;
- translation and accessibility support; and
- independent facilitation where representation is contested.

The company should not select representatives, require membership disclosure, or condition dialogue on abandoning union or association activity.

## 3.4 Informal rider collectives

Informal collectives may be unions, associations, cooperatives, strike committees, advocacy groups, unincorporated networks, or groups of independent businesses. Their legal authority cannot be inferred from public visibility or social-media activity.

The company should:

1. acknowledge the collective within one business day where practicable;
2. designate a neutral contact;
3. offer an initial meeting within ten business days;
4. identify the issues and the collective’s mandate;
5. verify only authority necessary for the specific engagement;
6. avoid requiring incorporation, registration, exclusivity, or full membership disclosure;
7. provide relevant information where lawful and appropriate;
8. issue written responses and track commitments; and
9. provide a route to formal consultation if the group later obtains legal recognition.

Forums must not:

- direct riders’ work;
- impose attendance or availability requirements;
- issue performance instructions;
- administer discipline;
- collect union or protest information; or
- require acceptance of the company’s classification position.

Every protocol should state:

> This dialogue does not replace any union, works council, statutory consultation body, bargaining process, collective agreement, or legal remedy.

Forums should be reviewed at least annually to confirm that they supplement rather than fragment or displace formal representation.

# 4. Consultation and bargaining boundaries

Each material change should be classified as one or more of:

1. individual information;
2. collective information;
3. consultation;
4. mandatory bargaining;
5. co-determination or approval;
6. management decision subject to procedural safeguards; or
7. legally restricted conduct.

## 4.1 Potentially negotiable subjects

Subject to local law, worker status, and representative authority, the company may negotiate:

- pay floors, guarantees, incentives, fees, and deductions;
- payment timing and earnings transparency;
- safety programs and equipment;
- insurance and accident coverage;
- training and onboarding;
- scheduling or availability arrangements;
- deactivation notice, review, and appeal;
- data transparency;
- algorithmic-management safeguards;
- representative access and facilities;
- consultation procedures;
- benefits or support programs; and
- transition measures during restructuring.

An agreement with one body should not be assumed to bind all riders or all worker categories.

## 4.2 Management decision authority

Management may generally retain authority over:

- legal and regulatory compliance;
- immediate safety, fraud, cybersecurity, and identity measures;
- product architecture and technology investment;
- customer and merchant relationships;
- commercial pricing;
- market entry, contraction, or exit;
- vendor and fleet-partner selection;
- service standards; and
- corporate resource allocation.

These decisions may nevertheless require information, consultation, bargaining, approval, notice, mitigation, or post-implementation review.

Examples:

- A market exit may be a management decision while restructuring, redundancy, severance, or transition duties apply.
- A safety suspension may occur immediately while reasons, human review, and notification duties remain.
- Algorithmic architecture may remain confidential while worker-impact data, evaluation criteria, monitoring practices, and human oversight require disclosure.
- Pay-model design may remain a management function while its effects on earnings, deductions, or incentives require bargaining or consultation.

## 4.3 Materiality and consultation triggers

A change should be presumptively material if it:

- materially changes expected earnings, fees, deductions, or incentives;
- affects a significant number of riders or a material geographic area;
- changes access to work, ranking, or deactivation criteria;
- introduces or materially expands automated monitoring;
- changes classification or contracting arrangements;
- affects safety, insurance, or social protection;
- changes operating geography or hours; or
- is reasonably likely to trigger statutory consultation, bargaining, or collective action.

Each local playbook should establish numerical or operational thresholds, subject to stricter local law, considering:

- number and percentage of riders affected;
- expected earnings or access-to-work impact;
- duration and reversibility;
- algorithmic or monitoring impact;
- classification implications;
- safety and insurance implications;
- regulatory sensitivity; and
- collective-action risk.

Labor-relations review is required before changes affecting pay, algorithms, ratings, monitoring, deactivation, safety, insurance, delivery zones, operating hours, subcontracting, classification, restructuring, or closure.

## 4.4 Change-management protocol

Before launch, the responsible team must document:

1. affected countries, cities, and worker categories;
2. classification and legal analysis;
3. relevant representative institution;
4. information, consultation, bargaining, or approval requirements;
5. notice and response deadlines;
6. information provided and feedback received;
7. alternatives and mitigation considered;
8. whether implementation must pause;
9. decision-maker and legal approver; and
10. post-launch monitoring and review date.

A qualifying change must not launch after a consultation or bargaining deadline has been missed unless:

- local counsel confirms implementation is lawful;
- the reason for proceeding is documented;
- the designated labor-relations authority approves the exception; and
- affected representatives are notified.

Consultation is complete only when required information has been provided, representatives have had a reasonable opportunity to respond, the feedback has been considered, and a substantive management response has been documented. Non-response does not automatically eliminate consultation duties unless local law permits that result and reasonable engagement efforts have been documented.

## 4.5 Competition-law safeguards

The company should protect lawful worker organizing and collective activity while avoiding unlawful coordination among independent businesses or competing platforms.

Before discussing rates, fees, service supply, territories, customer or merchant prices, coordinated log-offs, or industry-wide restrictions, competition counsel should assess:

- participant legal status;
- whether collective bargaining or action is protected;
- whether the discussion concerns labor conditions or commercial coordination;
- whether competitors or competitively sensitive information are involved; and
- whether a statutory or government-approved framework applies.

A pre-clearance protocol should cover:

- approved participants;
- approved agenda;
- prohibited topics;
- information-handling rules;
- minutes and record retention;
- counsel attendance where appropriate; and
- post-meeting review.

Managers should not broadly tell independent contractors that they cannot organize or discuss rates. The company must not coordinate prices, market allocation, rider allocation, or service restrictions among independent businesses or rival platforms.

# 5. Regional operating models

## 5.1 European Union

The EU requires a country-specific model. EU-level platform-work rules do not create one uniform works-council or bargaining structure.

Each country playbook should verify:

- national transposition and effective dates of applicable EU measures;
- transitional rules;
- covered worker categories;
- national works councils and employee committees;
- union and bargaining structures;
- European Works Council applicability;
- algorithmic-management information and consultation rights;
- GDPR and employee-monitoring requirements; and
- the next legal-review date.

The company should:

- engage the legally appropriate national institution;
- avoid using voluntary forums to bypass statutory bodies;
- provide required information about algorithmic management, monitoring, evaluation, and deactivation;
- maintain an internal Algorithmic Management Register;
- document human oversight, data used, worker impact, appeal routes, and consultation status; and
- coordinate labor, privacy, product, and safety reviews.

The register is an internal governance tool, not a legal safe harbor. Local counsel must confirm requirements concerning automated decisions, expert access, trade secrets, DPIAs, data minimization, and works-council rights.

## 5.2 Latin America

Latin America requires country-level analysis. Each higher-risk country should assess:

- employee, dependent-contractor, platform-worker, and independent-contractor categories;
- classification litigation and enforcement;
- social-security, tax, insurance, and occupational-risk obligations;
- joint-employer and fleet-partner exposure;
- union and association rights;
- bargaining and collective-action rules;
- historical claims, limitation, and prescription;
- migrant-worker and work-authorization issues; and
- settlement and release requirements.

Operational audits should examine actual practices, including:

- pricing and fee control;
- dispatch and order allocation;
- ratings and sanctions;
- availability expectations;
- substitution or delegation;
- geographic and time restrictions;
- equipment and uniforms;
- customer-facing obligations; and
- deactivation.

Higher-risk markets should maintain reclassification scenarios covering compliant contractor, dependent-contractor, employment, hybrid, or transition models, with payroll, benefits, tax, insurance, and social-security readiness.

Internal country labor-relations committees are governance bodies only and should not be presented as statutory bargaining or tripartite institutions.

## 5.3 Southeast Asia

The company should use a country-specific matrix covering:

- employment and contractor law;
- platform-specific rules;
- licensing requirements;
- social-security and accident coverage;
- data protection;
- competition law;
- tripartite structures; and
- industry or self-regulatory codes.

Each code must be classified as:

- legally binding regulation;
- government-endorsed voluntary code;
- contractual undertaking;
- industry-association standard; or
- public company commitment.

The platform should publish accurate worker-facing summaries, define measurable commitments, establish reporting channels, and conduct annual reviews. Participation in industry or government forums must not involve competitively sensitive information or misrepresent the authority of worker participants.

## 5.4 Cities with informal rider collectives

Each material city with an active collective should have:

- a neutral labor-relations contact;
- a prompt acknowledgment and meeting process;
- monthly or quarterly dialogue, depending on risk;
- emergency safety engagement;
- multilingual and accessible communications;
- issue logs and response deadlines; and
- escalation to country and regional leadership for systemic issues.

A classification-risk assessment is required before establishing or materially expanding a forum. City forums must not control work allocation, direct riders’ working methods, select representatives, or displace a legally recognized body.

# 6. Retaliation, data, and collective-action safeguards

## 6.1 Enhanced adverse-action review

Any suspension, deactivation, reduced access, or other adverse action occurring shortly after organizing, representation, safety reporting, complaints, or collective activity should be reviewed for:

- timing;
- automated triggers;
- manager communications;
- comparator treatment;
- consistency of enforcement;
- factual support; and
- retaliation indicators.

Serious allegations should be investigated independently of the implicated operations team where practicable. Interim protective measures should be considered immediately where credible risk exists.

## 6.2 Data controls

The platform should not collect or infer union membership, organizing activity, protest participation, representative status, or collective affiliation unless lawful, necessary, proportionate, and approved.

Controls include:

- no monitoring of private organizing channels;
- no scraping of social-media groups to identify organizers;
- separation of labor-relations data from performance and deactivation systems;
- role-based access and audit logs;
- purpose limitation and retention controls;
- privacy notices and lawful-basis assessments;
- DPIAs where required;
- restrictions on location, biometric, and behavioral data;
- pseudonymized retaliation reporting where possible; and
- human review of adverse decisions potentially influenced by collective activity.

Each local playbook should document data categories, lawful basis, access roles, retention periods, cross-border transfer mechanisms, deletion and anonymization rules, legal holds, and re-identification safeguards.

## 6.3 Collective-action response

The company should:

- respect lawful collective activity;
- avoid discriminatory enforcement;
- protect riders, customers, property, and public safety;
- use neutral service-continuity measures;
- obtain legal review before account action;
- communicate service impacts accurately; and
- distinguish protected activity from independently documented unlawful conduct.

The company should not condition support, appeal, compensation, or internal review on waiver of statutory rights.

# 7. Governance, escalation, and accountability

## 7.1 Governance structure

A Global Labor-Relations Office should own:

- global principles;
- local-playbook standards;
- engagement protocols;
- consultation controls;
- manager training;
- metrics and reporting;
- cross-market learning; and
- escalation.

A Global Labor-Relations Steering Committee should include labor relations, legal, operations, product, safety, privacy, public policy, risk, communications, finance, and regional leadership. It should approve global standards, review high-risk markets, oversee material commitments, resolve cross-market conflicts, and report material matters to the board or relevant committee.

## 7.2 Decision rights

| Decision | Required review |
|---|---|
| Local material pay or fee change | Local counsel and country labor-relations approval |
| Cross-market pay model | Global Labor-Relations Office and executive approval |
| Algorithmic-management change | Privacy, legal, product, and labor-relations review |
| Classification-model change | Local counsel; global approval for multi-country models |
| New representative forum | Local counsel and country labor-relations approval |
| Collective-action response | Local counsel; regional review if material |
| Emergency safety action | Immediate operational action permitted; documented legal and post-action review |
| Public statement on classification or representation | Local legal and communications approval |
| Failure to meet a global standard | Remediation plan; Steering Committee review for material or repeated failures |

Where standards conflict:

1. mandatory local law, court orders, regulators, and collective agreements prevail;
2. local counsel identifies the legal minimum;
3. the Global Labor-Relations Office determines how the global standard can be adapted;
4. the Steering Committee resolves cross-market policy conflicts; and
5. no high-risk launch proceeds during unresolved disagreement unless the designated exception authority documents the legal basis, mitigation, and residual-risk acceptance.

## 7.3 Escalation model

1. **Local resolution:** city operations, support, and labor-relations contact.
2. **Country review:** pay, safety, representation, classification, and policy disputes.
3. **Regional review:** cross-market issues, regulatory inquiries, collective action, serious retaliation, and major disputes.
4. **Global review:** systemic risks, global commitments, major restructurings, and repeated local failures.

Subject to stricter local law:

- retaliation or safety allegations should be acknowledged within one business day;
- initial risk assessment should occur within three business days;
- serious cases should have an investigation plan within five business days;
- unresolved matters should escalate automatically after ten business days; and
- severe, systemic, or regulatory-sensitive cases should be reported to the relevant executive and board or audit committee.

# 8. Implementation roadmap

## First 30 days

- approve global principles and non-retaliation rules;
- appoint global, regional, and country owners;
- freeze inconsistent recognition and forum practices;
- identify unions, councils, associations, collectives, and public bodies;
- screen pending pay, algorithm, deactivation, classification, and restructuring changes;
- create interim escalation channels; and
- begin legal, privacy, competition, classification, and regulatory mapping.

## Days 31–90

- complete worker-status and representation matrices;
- adopt local legal playbooks;
- implement the change-screening form and consultation register;
- train operations, support, product, communications, and security teams;
- establish enhanced review for post-organizing adverse actions;
- adopt commitment, mandate, subject-matter, and decision-rights tables; and
- set current-law refresh dates.

## Months 3–6

- launch forums only after legal validation;
- agree protocols with formal representatives;
- begin city dialogue with informal collectives;
- implement minimum appeal, transparency, and safety standards;
- establish country committees;
- finalize Latin American reclassification contingency plans; and
- complete EU and Southeast Asian jurisdiction-specific reviews.

## Months 6–12 and beyond

- embed labor review into product and operational governance;
- conduct independent audits;
- test worker-participatory pilots;
- publish a carefully scoped annual report;
- establish regional or global dialogue on cross-market issues;
- review whether forums improve voice or fragment representation; and
- refresh local playbooks at least annually and after material legal or regulatory change.

| Unreliable commitments | Commitment classification, defined scope, owner, funding, review date |

# 10. Metrics and assurance

Success should not be measured by the absence of complaints, strikes, or collective action. Low activity may reflect weak channels or underreporting.

Metrics should cover:

### Representation and engagement

- markets with a functioning lawful voice channel;
- formal bodies correctly identified and engaged;
- forums operating under approved protocols;
- participation, accessibility, and language coverage;
- response times and issue closure; and
- rider and representative trust.

### Consultation quality

- qualifying changes screened before launch;
- consultations conducted within required timelines;
- completeness of information provided;
- substantive management responses;
- changes modified or piloted following feedback; and
- missed-consultation incidents and remediation.

### Worker outcomes

- earnings and fee impacts after policy changes;
- safety incidents and near misses;
- appeal access and turnaround;
- deactivation reversal rates by cause;
- unresolved pay and safety issues; and
- substantiated retaliation and remediation rates.

### Legal and business risk

- classification audits completed;
- unresolved high-risk exposures;
- regulatory findings;
- privacy and competition incidents;
- litigation and settlement trends;
- service disruption;
- rider retention; and
- repeat failures by market, process, or manager.

Metric safeguards should include:

- no incentives based solely on low complaints, low collective action, high closure rates, or high appeal-denial rates;
- independent or confidential survey administration;
- auditing of issue-closure classifications;
- segmentation of appeal outcomes by cause and worker category;
- review of complaint underreporting;
- privacy-compliant reporting; and
- periodic internal-audit or independent-assurance testing.

---

## Strategic end state

The platform should achieve **consistency of principles, process quality, and minimum safeguards—not uniformity of worker status or representation**.

Unions, works councils, statutory bodies, associations, and other lawful representatives should exercise their locally defined roles. Informal collectives should be heard without being controlled, mischaracterized, or used to displace formal representation. Consultation and bargaining should occur where required. Management should retain legitimate operational authority, but only within applicable labor, platform-work, privacy, competition, safety, social-protection, and contractual limits.

Implementation should be supported by dated local legal validation, continuous regulatory monitoring, regularly refreshed country playbooks, and documented escalation when local requirements or operating conditions change.
