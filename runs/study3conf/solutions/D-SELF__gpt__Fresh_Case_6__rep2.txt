# Sovereign-Cloud Transition Strategy  
## Preserving Customer Choice, Continuity, Transparency, and Value

## 1. Executive summary

By 2026, evolving data-sovereignty requirements will require the provider to move beyond its single-region architecture. The response must be more than an infrastructure program: it is a redesign of the provider’s product, operating model, commercial terms, governance, and customer-trust approach.

The strategy is to:

1. Provide global, regional, national, and customer-controlled operating options.
2. Define exactly where customer content, metadata, keys, logs, backups, support activity, and AI data are processed.
3. Preserve core workflows, APIs, identity, audit, security, backup, and export capabilities.
4. Prioritize customers with binding legal or contractual deadlines.
5. Give customers a clear choice of deployment profile by workload and data class wherever permitted.
6. Protect customers from avoidable disruption, unexpected cost increases, and unreasonable lock-in.
7. Communicate consistently worldwide while adapting language, evidence, channels, and emphasis to local requirements and trust conditions.
8. Build an enduring governance model for future jurisdictional changes.

### Customer promise

> **We will provide a verifiable and contractually defined control boundary for your data and operations, with transparent limitations, practical migration support, predictable economics, and meaningful portability.**

This promise must be qualified by jurisdiction, workload, sector, operating profile, and applicable law. The provider must not promise immunity from government access, foreign legal process, sanctions, national outages, or geopolitical disruption.

# 2. Strategic principles

## 2.1 Define sovereignty precisely

“Sovereign cloud” must not be used as an unqualified product label. Each environment must have a version-controlled **Sovereignty Control Schedule** covering:

| Control area | Required disclosure |
|---|---|
| Customer content | Storage, processing, caching, indexing, and deletion locations |
| Metadata | Tenant identifiers, usage, billing, telemetry, analytics, and support data |
| Backups | Location, retention, restoration path, and failover jurisdiction |
| Keys | Provider-managed, customer-managed, locally held, or customer-controlled |
| Administration | Personnel location, screening, remote access, and emergency access |
| Support | Ticket location, diagnostic data, screen-sharing, language, and hours |
| Subprocessors | Function, location, ownership, personnel access, and change process |
| Legal entities | Contracting entity, operating entity, parent ownership, and liability |
| Government access | Review, notice, challenge, logging, and disclosure procedures |
| Software control | Release authority, signing, update approval, and dependencies |
| Resilience | In-country, regional, global, or customer-managed recovery |

Use precise service descriptions:

- **Nationally hosted:** primary service data is hosted in the stated country.
- **Region-contained:** defined data and operations remain within an approved region.
- **Nationally controlled:** data plus specified administrative, key, support, and operational controls remain in-country.
- **Customer-controlled:** the customer or approved local operator controls specified infrastructure, keys, or administration.

A regional service must not be represented as satisfying a national requirement without market-specific validation.

## 2.2 Preserve one global product family

Maintain, wherever legally and technically possible:

- Common product identity and user experience.
- Common data models and APIs.
- Common security and privacy baselines.
- Consistent documentation and release governance.
- Unified commercial management.
- A formal process for closing regional feature gaps.

Regional variation should be limited to legal, security, operational, sector, or customer-required differences.

## 2.3 Separate legal necessity from provider choice

Every restriction or variation must be classified as:

1. Legally mandatory.
2. Required by a customer or sector standard.
3. A risk-reduction control.
4. A temporary engineering limitation.
5. A commercial or architectural choice.

This prevents optional decisions from being presented as law and creates accountability for retiring temporary limitations.

## 2.4 Make trust reversible

Trust should be supported by:

- Contractual control schedules.
- Independent assurance.
- Access and key-custody evidence.
- Transparent ownership and subprocessor information.
- Tested migration and recovery.
- Standard exports and documented APIs.
- Deletion evidence.
- Fair termination and transition assistance.

Portability is a trust mechanism, not merely an exit provision.

# 3. Product and architecture model

## 3.1 Deployment profiles

| Profile | Description | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Global service | Global processing where legally and contractually permitted | Lower-regulation customers |
| Regional-contained service | Specified data and operations remain within an approved region | Multinationals and regulated enterprises |
| National-controlled service | Data and defined operational functions remain in one country | Public sector and highly regulated workloads |
| Customer-controlled service | Customer or approved local operator controls selected infrastructure, keys, or administration | Defense and extreme-assurance use cases |

Customers should select a profile by workload, data class, and risk—not necessarily for the entire enterprise.

## 3.2 Control-plane options

### Global control plane

Appropriate where global identity, metadata, administration, support, and release control are acceptable.

### Restricted control plane

Global product governance remains centralized, while identity, privileged operations, keys, audit logs, support, and relevant telemetry are localized or tightly restricted.

### Fully local control plane

Required where local or customer-controlled identity, administration, keys, monitoring, incident response, support, backup, recovery, and release validation are necessary.

A fully local environment should not be labelled as such until it passes an operational-independence test for an approved period, such as 30 or 90 days, including:

- Loss of global connectivity.
- Local staffing unavailability.
- Key-service failure.
- Emergency patching.
- Backup restoration.
- Incident response.
- Hardware replacement.
- Degraded-mode operation.

# 4. Global commitments and regional adaptations

## 4.1 Global commitments

These commitments apply across markets:

- No undisclosed or unauthorized cross-border processing.
- Complete disclosure of content, metadata, logs, backups, support, and AI data flows.
- Least-privilege access and immutable auditability.
- Strong encryption and clear key custody.
- Independent assurance appropriate to the deployment profile.
- Transparent feature and service differences.
- Standard APIs, export, deletion, and portability.
- Fair migration and exit treatment.
- Evidence-based incident response.
- No claims of absolute immunity or zero risk.
- Transparent pricing and service commitments.
- No unreasonable lock-in.
- Clear distinction between committed capabilities and roadmap aspirations.

## 4.2 Regional adaptations

These elements must be validated market by market:

- Required jurisdiction and sector controls.
- Contracting and operating entity.
- Ownership or foreign-investment restrictions.
- Personnel location and screening.
- Approved infrastructure and subprocessors.
- Key-custody model.
- Government-request process.
- Recovery geography.
- Procurement terms and remedies.
- Support language, hours, and escalation norms.
- Tax, employment, invoicing, and public-sector requirements.
- Local communication channels and trusted messengers.

Regional teams may adapt execution and communication but may not weaken global security, privacy, audit, portability, or disclosure baselines without formal approval.

# 5. Customer segmentation

Segment customers by regulatory obligation, workload sensitivity, control requirements, procurement behavior, migration complexity, and trust concerns—not by country alone.

## Segment A: National-security, defense, and critical infrastructure

**Priorities**

- Fully local or restricted operation.
- Screened personnel and controlled support.
- Local or customer-held keys.
- Supply-chain assurance.
- Disconnected or degraded operation.
- Independent and, where required, restricted-access assurance.

**Primary concerns**

- Foreign personnel or government access.
- Software-update control.
- Supply-chain exposure.
- National resilience.
- Whether the environment is genuinely sovereign.

**Engagement**

- Formal procurement briefings.
- Controlled technical workshops.
- Executive and regulator engagement.
- Qualified local security advisers.

## Segment B: Highly regulated private enterprises

Examples include financial services, healthcare, telecommunications, utilities, and payment providers.

**Priorities**

- Auditability.
- Data-flow evidence.
- Recovery testing.
- API and workflow continuity.
- Predictable pricing.
- Contractual remedies.

**Engagement**

- CISO, risk, legal, procurement, and architecture workshops.
- Regulator-ready evidence packs.
- Migration rehearsals and acceptance reviews.

## Segment C: Multinational enterprises

**Priorities**

- Workload-level placement.
- Federated governance.
- Common APIs and releases.
- Unified commercial management.
- Clear global-administration boundaries.

**Engagement**

- Global account teams.
- Enterprise architecture councils.
- Multi-country pilots.
- Central procurement and regional legal reviews.

## Segment D: Public-sector agencies

**Priorities**

- Contracting accountability.
- Procurement eligibility.
- Local support.
- Ownership transparency.
- Government-request procedures.
- Genuine local capability.

**Engagement**

- Formal tenders.
- Public-sector forums.
- Local advisers and integrators.
- Policy and procurement briefings.

## Segment E: Commercial and smaller customers

**Priorities**

- Whether migration is actually required.
- Simple eligibility guidance.
- Low disruption.
- Transparent pricing.
- Limited compliance burden.

**Offer**

- Automated eligibility assessment.
- Preconfigured control profiles.
- Self-service validation.
- Fixed migration packages.
- Partner-assisted migration.
- Plain-language contracts and support.

## Segment F: Customers skeptical of both governments and foreign vendors

**Priorities**

- Customer-controlled keys.
- Access transparency.
- Independent oversight.
- Data minimization.
- Portability.
- Honest limits on provider guarantees.

Market profiles are hypotheses, not national stereotypes. Each profile must record research sources, sample composition, language and sector coverage, confidence level, assumptions, and disconfirming evidence.

# 6. Phased roadmap

## Phase 0: Mobilize and discover  
**First 60–90 days**

### Actions

- Appoint an executive sponsor and program leader.
- Establish integrated governance, decision rights, and escalation paths.
- Build the regulatory deadline and applicability register.
- Inventory content, metadata, logs, backups, AI data, support data, keys, identity, personnel access, and subprocessors.
- Freeze new dependencies that conflict with target controls.
- Identify customers with binding legal or contractual deadlines.
- Baseline service quality, feature coverage, trust, churn, cost, and exit capability.
- Draft the Sovereignty Control Schedule, claims register, and existing-contract policy.
- Begin controlled briefings with priority customers and relevant regulators.

### Deliverables

- Market and sector heat map.
- Data-flow and subprocessor register.
- Customer obligation matrix.
- Target architecture.
- Initial feature-gap and cost model.
- Pilot shortlist.
- Critical-path schedule.
- Crisis and escalation framework.

## Phase 1: Foundations and representative pilots

Select pilots based on:

- Legal urgency.
- Workload sensitivity.
- Technical representativeness.
- Different trust conditions.
- Customer willingness to participate.
- Reusable platform value.

Build and test:

- Sovereign landing zones.
- Local or customer-controlled keys.
- Region-aware identity.
- Local monitoring and support.
- Migration, validation, export, and recovery tooling.
- Backup and restore.
- Access, incident, and degraded-operation runbooks.
- Contract and procurement templates.

Do not declare general availability until launch gates are met.

## Phase 2: Priority environment launches

Sequence according to:

1. Binding legal deadlines.
2. Data sensitivity and public-interest impact.
3. Regulatory commitments.
4. Customer concentration and platform reuse.
5. Sustainable economics.
6. Operational readiness.

A regional-first approach may improve efficiency but must not be used as a substitute for national requirements.

## Phase 3: Customer migration waves

Each migration includes:

1. Eligibility and control-boundary assessment.
2. Data and application dependency discovery.
3. Contract and subprocessor confirmation.
4. Workload-specific migration design.
5. Non-production rehearsal.
6. Replication and checksum validation.
7. Identity, integration, API, and permission testing.
8. Backup and restore testing.
9. Cutover and rollback or forward-recovery plan.
10. Customer acceptance.
11. Dual-running and hypercare.
12. Post-migration evidence and review.

Align migrations with customer audit, fiscal, change-control, and lower-risk operating periods.

## Phase 4: Optimize and sustain

- Close priority feature gaps.
- Reduce code forks and release divergence.
- Improve capacity utilization and margin.
- Review pricing against actual costs.
- Test export, deletion, provider replacement, and degraded operation.
- Retire legacy paths only after contractual, regulatory, and customer-transition requirements are satisfied.
- Reassess laws, subprocessors, geopolitical risks, and customer needs annually.

# 7. Migration operating model

## 7.1 Customer journey

| Stage | Provider responsibility | Customer decision |
|---|---|---|
| Eligibility | Assess obligation and affected workloads | Confirm scope and business requirements |
| Discovery | Map data, integrations, users, and dependencies | Provide architecture and data-owner information |
| Design | Recommend control profile, recovery model, and migration path | Approve target design and commercial terms |
| Rehearsal | Run validation, integration, and recovery tests | Confirm readiness and change window |
| Cutover | Execute migration, monitor, and communicate | Authorize cutover and provide business validation |
| Hypercare | Resolve issues and monitor service | Confirm operational acceptance |
| Completion | Provide evidence and retire source data when permitted | Sign acceptance and approve optimization |

## 7.2 Workload-specific runbooks

Create separate runbooks for:

- Stateless services.
- Relational databases.
- Object stores.
- Event streams.
- Analytics pipelines.
- AI workloads.
- Identity systems.
- Continuous-write workloads.
- Systems with external side effects.
- Data under legal hold or retention obligations.

Each runbook must define:

- Replication mechanism.
- Write-freeze or read-only period.
- Cutover method.
- Maximum data divergence.
- Reconciliation method.
- Idempotency and duplicate-event controls.
- External side-effect treatment.
- Rollback point.
- Forward-recovery process.
- Acceptance criteria.

Rollback must not be promised universally. For some workloads, forward recovery is safer.

## 7.3 Dual-running controls

Define:

- Which copy is authoritative.
- Synchronization and reconciliation rules.
- Retention and legal holds.
- Customer access to each copy.
- Cost responsibility.
- Source-environment retirement date.
- Deletion method and evidence.
- Jurisdiction of every copy during transition.

## 7.4 Non-readiness or refusal

Use a graduated process:

1. Early warning.
2. Readiness remediation.
3. Executive escalation.
4. Temporary documented exception where legally permissible.
5. Restricted service or feature status.
6. Regulator engagement where appropriate.
7. Contractual remedy, export, termination, and transition support.

Publish an environment-specific matrix using:

- **Full parity**
- **Functionally equivalent**
- **Available with restrictions**
- **Planned but uncommitted**
- **Unavailable for legal or technical reasons**

Cover:

- Core workflows.
- APIs and integrations.
- Identity and administration.
- Audit and compliance.
- Backup, restore, and export.
- Security.
- Reporting and analytics.
- AI.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Release schedules.
- Workarounds and customer impact.

## Priority order

1. Core workflows.
2. Identity and administration.
3. Audit and compliance.
4. APIs and integrations.
5. Backup, restore, and export.
6. Security and resilience.
7. Reporting and analytics.
8. AI.
9. Convenience features.

Only approved product, legal, finance, and customer-success owners may classify a capability as contractually committed. Sales teams may not represent operational targets as guarantees.

## AI sovereignty policy

For each AI capability, define:

- Inference location.
- Prompt, output, embedding, and index location.
- Retention.
- Training use.
- Model ownership and hosting.
- Update authority.
- Key handling.
- Export capability.
- Local or non-AI alternatives.
- Pricing impact if unavailable.

Maintain a product-complexity budget covering code forks, release divergence, bespoke configurations, customer-specific variants, and aging temporary limitations.

# 9. Trust-building commitments

## 9.1 Transparency

Provide customers with:

- Current control schedules.
- Data-flow and subprocessor registers.
- Ownership and operating-entity information.
- Personnel and support-access rules.
- Government-request procedures.
- Feature and API matrices.
- Recovery profiles.
- Pricing assumptions.
- Migration plans.
- Incident and deletion evidence.

## 9.2 Choice

Where legally permitted, allow customers to choose:

- Global, regional, national, or customer-controlled operation.
- Workload-level placement.
- Key-management model.
- Recovery geography.
- Local support options.
- AI-enabled or alternative workflows.

Customers may not always choose whether migration is required, but they should have a meaningful choice among compliant profiles.

## 9.3 Continuity

Commit to preserving:

- Core workflows.
- APIs and integrations.
- Identity and access management.
- Audit and compliance functions.
- Security controls.
- Backup, restore, and export.
- Defined service levels.
- Customer access during transition.

## 9.4 Fair value

Adopt standard protections:

- No or capped setup fees for mandated migrations.
- Included migration tooling.
- Defined migration-service hours.
- A defined dual-running period.
- Price protection through migration and the first renewal.
- Standard export without punitive fees.
- Credits for provider-caused migration failure.
- Remedies for missed critical parity commitments.
- No forced long-term renewal as a condition of compliance support.
- Fair transition assistance.

Publish an included-services schedule distinguishing provider-funded migration from chargeable customer application redesign, data cleansing, network changes, or custom integration work.

## 9.5 Portability and exit

Support export of, where applicable:

- Content.
- Metadata.
- Permissions and roles.
- Configurations and schemas.
- Audit history.
- Retention information.
- Attachments and large objects.
- Event streams.
- Integrations.
- AI prompts, outputs, embeddings, and evaluation data.
- Key dependencies.

Test restoration into an independent environment and report export completeness, duration, errors, and restoration success.

# 10. Pricing and contracting

## 10.1 Pricing structure

Offer transparent tiers:

- Standard global service.
- Regional-contained service.
- National-controlled service.
- Dedicated or customer-controlled service.
- Optional local support, customer-managed keys, or disconnected operation.

Separate:

- Protected recurring charges.
- Variable usage charges.
- Pass-through costs.
- Taxes and foreign exchange.
- Optional controls.
- Customer-requested capacity.
- Regulatory-change repricing.

Explain cost drivers, including local staffing, dedicated capacity, assurance, key custody, recovery, and reduced scale economies.

## 10.2 Existing-contract policy

Classify customers as:

1. Current service remains compliant.
2. Mandatory migration required.
3. Optional sovereignty requested.
4. Contract restricts relocation.
5. Contract lacks relevant change terms.

For each category define:

- Notice.
- Consent.
- Repricing.
- SLA treatment.
- Migration funding.
- Termination and remedies.
- Legacy-retirement process.

Where the provider cannot deliver a compliant service under the existing contract, offer an approved combination of amendment, equivalent service, temporary extension, price protection, migration credit, termination without punitive fees, export, and transition assistance—subject to applicable law and negotiated terms.

# 11. Stakeholder and communications plan

## 11.1 Message architecture

Every communication must answer:

1. Why is the transition occurring?
2. Is migration mandatory for this customer or workload?
3. What remains unchanged?
4. What may differ?
5. What actions and dates apply?
6. What will it cost?
7. What support and remedies apply?
8. What remains uncertain?

Use confidence labels:

- Confirmed requirement.
- High-confidence interpretation.
- Pending regulator guidance.
- Planned but uncommitted feature.
- Pricing assumption.
- Unavailable capability.

A confidence label is not a legal guarantee.

## 11.2 Audience plan

| Audience | Emphasis | Primary materials and channels |
|---|---|---|
| Executive leaders | Continuity, regulatory resilience, cost, strategic options | Executive briefings, risk summaries, decision papers |
| Procurement and legal | Contract, price, audit, liability, exit, subprocessors | Sovereignty schedule, tender library, contract clinics |
| Security and architecture | Data flows, identity, keys, APIs, recovery, migration | Architecture workshops, test evidence, runbooks |
| Regulators | Control effectiveness, access boundaries, resilience, auditability | Technical briefings, assurance submissions, formal consultations |
| Customer support | Approved language, escalation, limitations, incident handling | Training, scripts, knowledge base, escalation matrix |
| Investors and board | Investment, margin, retention, risk, milestones | Quarterly dashboard and decision reviews |
| Independent experts | Evidence, governance, privacy, security, accountability | Consultations, technical documentation, controlled reviews |

## 11.3 Message emphasis by customer context

- **Public sector:** accountability, control, local operations, ownership transparency.
- **Regulated enterprises:** audit evidence, resilience, API continuity, contractual protection.
- **Multinationals:** workload placement, common product, federated governance.
- **Commercial customers:** choice, simplicity, price, and disruption avoidance.
- **Low-trust audiences:** independent evidence, customer-controlled keys, portability, and access transparency.

Avoid:

- Nationalistic slogans.
- “Local equals safe.”
- “Government-proof.”
- “Zero access.”
- Unqualified “sovereign” claims.
- Implied regulatory endorsement.
- Presenting optional controls as legal requirements.
- Blaming customers for costs caused by the provider’s original architecture.

## 11.4 Single source of truth

Create a role-based, version-controlled customer portal with:

- Executive summary.
- Procurement and legal materials.
- Technical control schedules.
- Feature and API matrices.
- Processing-location and subprocessor information.
- Migration guidance.
- Pricing principles.
- Incident and service information.
- Effective dates and change history.
- Public and customer-confidential versions.

Material changes must have defined notice periods, customer alerts, acknowledgement tracking, and contractual linkage where relevant.

## 11.5 Announcement cadence

### Before announcement

- Brief regulators where appropriate.
- Brief priority customers only on permitted operational-readiness matters.
- Provide standard information simultaneously to affected customers.
- Review public-sector briefings for equal-treatment requirements.
- Train sales, support, partners, legal, and executives.
- Prohibit selective disclosure of nonpublic pricing, roadmap, regulatory, or competitor information.

### Announcement

Publish:

- Executive statement.
- Global principles.
- Market roadmap.
- Customer-impact assessment.
- FAQ.
- Known limitations.
- Security and privacy documentation.
- Contact routes for regulated customers.

### During migration

Provide:

- Account-specific migration plans.
- Readiness reviews.
- Technical workshops.
- Cutover notices.
- Status dashboards.
- Issue and decision logs.
- Executive escalation for blocked accounts.

### After migration

Provide:

- Acceptance confirmation.
- Control and compliance evidence.
- Service review.
- Post-migration survey.
- Updated parity status.
- Lessons learned and next steps.

All material communications require local legal, technical, cultural, accessibility, and translation review. Test for negative interpretations, including surveillance enablement, foreign-influence concerns, political alignment, and regulatory profiteering.

# 12. Security, operations, and resilience

## 12.1 Security and access controls

Implement as appropriate:

- Encryption in transit and at rest.
- Local HSMs or customer-controlled keys.
- Tenant- and jurisdiction-specific key separation.
- Just-in-time privileged access.
- Dual authorization.
- Immutable audit logs.
- Local monitoring.
- Egress monitoring and data-loss prevention.
- Secure deletion.
- Independent vulnerability testing.
- Confidential computing for high-assurance workloads.

## 12.2 Identity boundary

Document:

- Customer identity provider.
- Provider workforce identity.
- Privileged identity.
- Break-glass credentials.
- Certificates and secrets.
- Federation metadata.
- Session logs.
- Device and administrator authentication.
- Identity recovery when a provider is unavailable.

## 12.3 Support localization

Define by environment:

- Support tiers permitted to access data.
- Metadata-only diagnostic procedures.
- Customer approval for production access.
- Personnel location and screening.
- Emergency access.
- Language coverage.
- Time-zone and holiday coverage.
- On-call staffing.
- Local incident command.
- Access logging and review.
- Backup staffing.

Measure response time, first-contact resolution, escalation accuracy, terminology errors, customer satisfaction, and unauthorized-access exceptions.

## 12.4 Software, hardware, and facility controls

Cover:

- Software provenance.
- Dependency inventories.
- Signed releases and build attestations.
- Signing-key custody.
- Local update validation.
- Emergency patching and rollback.
- Hardware and firmware provenance.
- Spare parts and replacement inventory.
- Facility access and maintenance personnel.
- Media handling and secure disposal.
- Power, environmental, and connectivity resilience.
- Alternate suppliers.

## 12.5 Recovery profiles

| Profile | Sovereignty | Resilience |
|---|---:|---:|
| In-country only | Highest | Lower against national disruption |
| Approved regional recovery | High | Higher |
| Global recovery | Lower | Highest |
| Customer-managed backup | Variable | Customer-dependent |

Define degraded modes such as:

- Read-only operation.
- Local export.
- Customer-managed restoration.
- Limited authentication.
- Delayed analytics.
- Disabled AI.
- Manual support.

Contracts must identify RTO, RPO, maximum data loss, recovery jurisdictions, national-outage consequences, customer responsibilities, and remedies.

# 15. Metrics and decision gates

Every metric must have an owner, source, baseline, target, warning threshold, stop condition, and causal interpretation.

## Core metrics

| Area | Measures |
|---|---|
| Customer | Renewal, churn, at-risk revenue, migration completion, delays, satisfaction, blocked migrations |
| Migration | Duration, downtime, divergence, validation exceptions, recovery, incidents |
| Product | Mission-critical parity, API compatibility, release divergence, gap closure |
| Operations | Availability, latency, RTO/RPO, restore success, capacity, support performance |
| Security | Cross-border exceptions, privileged-access reviews, key events, incident response |
| Compliance | Findings, audit exceptions, data-location discrepancies, due-diligence response |
| Trust | Confidence, risk approval, legal redlines, pilot acceptance, expansion, competitor trials |
| Economics | Cost per workload, utilization, margin, migration cost, subsidy, price exposure |
| Exit | Export completeness, deletion evidence, restoration success, replacement time |
| Workforce | Staffing fill rate, screening completion, training, coverage resilience |
| Sustainability | Energy, utilization, hardware lifecycle, and resource efficiency by environment |

## Launch and migration stop triggers

Pause launch or migration for:

- An unresolved critical control gap.
- An unauthorized or unexplained data flow.
- An untested required recovery path.
- An unsupported critical workflow.
- Failed key-recovery testing.
- No approved support or incident model.
- A missed legal deadline without contingency.
- Material contractual or regulatory uncertainty.
- Cost or staffing conditions that make sustained service quality unlikely.

## Trust-recovery measures

After a material incident, track:

- Time to complete corrective actions.
- Time to restore customer confidence.
- Repeat evidence requests.
- Customer acceptance of remediation.
- Renewal and expansion behavior.
- Independent assurance completion.

# 16. First 90 days

## Days 0–30

- Establish governance, RACI, decision SLAs, and non-waivable controls.
- Approve control dimensions and deployment profiles.
- Build the legal deadline and applicability register.
- Inventory content, metadata, logs, backups, AI, support, personnel, subprocessors, keys, and identity flows.
- Freeze new noncompliant dependencies.
- Baseline trust, churn, cost, feature, service, exit, and sustainability metrics.
- Draft the control schedule, claims register, existing-contract policy, and customer-impact assessment.

## Days 31–60

- Approve global, restricted, and fully local control-plane designs.
- Build market, budget, staffing, capacity, and migration-throughput scenarios.
- Define launch, pause, retirement, exception, and complexity thresholds.
- Select pilots representing different regulatory, trust, and workload conditions.
- Define standard migration services, dual-running, credits, price protection, and remedies.
- Create workload-specific migration runbooks.
- Build the tender response library and role-based customer portal.
- Test communications with local legal, procurement, security, technical, executive, support, accessibility, and cultural reviewers.

## Days 61–90

- Conduct pilots and at least one continuous-write migration rehearsal.
- Test residency validation, egress blocking, key failure and recovery, export, deletion, restore, support access, and degraded operation.
- Validate incident-notification and jurisdictional-conflict workflows.
- Publish the roadmap, limitations, control schedules, procurement materials, and pricing principles.
- Train sales, support, partners, and executives on approved claims.
- Begin independent assurance.
- Establish customer advisory mechanisms where appropriate.
- Start monthly executive reporting against approved targets and stop conditions.

# 17. Final decision framework for senior leaders

Approve an environment for general availability only when leadership can confirm:

### Customer

- Affected customers and workloads are identified.
- Customers know whether migration is mandatory.
- Migration owners, dates, costs, and remedies are documented.
- Small and complex customers have an appropriate support path.

### Technical

- Data flows, metadata, support, AI, keys, identity, and backups are mapped.
- The stated control profile has passed its operational-independence test.
- Core workflows, APIs, identity, audit, security, backup, restore, and export are available.
- Migration and recovery have been tested under realistic conditions.

### Legal and procurement

- Claims are validated for the target market.
- Contracts define control boundaries, access, subprocessors, recovery, pricing, migration, and exit.
- Procurement and accreditation timelines are complete or have approved contingencies.
- Legal holds, conflicting legal demands, and termination are addressed.

### Commercial

- Pricing and price protection are approved.
- Provider-funded and customer-funded migration work is defined.
- Liability, insurance, credits, and remedies are aligned.
- The environment has sustainable economics or an approved strategic subsidy.

### Trust and communications

- Customer-facing information is accurate, accessible, and version-controlled.
- Local adaptation has been reviewed without stereotypes or political overreach.
- Known limitations and uncertainties are disclosed.
- Incident and escalation communications are ready.

## Closing position

The transition should be measured not by the number of national clouds launched, but by whether customers can make informed decisions and continue operating with confidence.

The provider’s durable trust position is:

> **We can show what is controlled, by whom, where, under which contract, with what evidence, at what cost, and what remedy applies if the commitment fails.**

That position preserves customer choice, continuity, transparency, and value while acknowledging that sovereignty is a set of verifiable controls—not a guarantee of absolute isolation from law, markets, infrastructure, or geopolitics.
