# Global AI Productivity Toolkit  
## Trusted Adoption and Change-Management Strategy

### Executive decision

Approve the approved AI-productivity toolkit as a **mandatory governed capability** for in-scope knowledge workers.

This means employees must:

- Complete role-appropriate training and practical assessment.
- Follow global and local AI, privacy, security, records, and professional standards.
- Use the approved toolkit in designated workflows that have passed legal, risk, privacy, security, accessibility, and operational approval.
- Apply required human review and sign-off.
- Report material incidents, harmful outputs, and sensitive-data exposure.

It does **not** mean employees must use AI for every task or generate visible activity.

Employees may—and sometimes must—not use AI where:

- The workflow is prohibited, unsuitable, or not yet approved.
- The relevant data cannot lawfully or safely be processed.
- The toolkit is unavailable, unreliable, inaccessible, or materially unsuitable.
- Professional judgment requires non-use.
- A local legal, regulatory, collective-agreement, accessibility, or employee-representative exception applies.

> **Success means open, appropriate, compliant use that improves insurance outcomes without compromising privacy, professional judgment, customer outcomes, psychological safety, professional dignity, or sustainable employment capability.**

---

## 1. Strategic objectives

By the end of 2026, the Group will seek to:

1. Reduce covert or unapproved AI use and associated data exposure.
2. Embed the approved toolkit in validated workflows such as drafting, summarization, research, coding, translation, analysis, and knowledge retrieval.
3. Preserve claims, underwriting, customer, conduct, compliance, and professional standards.
4. Build trust in privacy, fairness, measurement, and workforce-impact safeguards.
5. Provide credible reskilling, mobility, redeployment, and career support.
6. Establish a repeatable operating model for future AI capabilities.
7. Demonstrate sustainable value through improved quality, service, capacity, control effectiveness, or cycle time.

---

## 2. Non-negotiable principles

1. **Enablement before enforcement**  
   Mandatory workflow use begins only when the tool, training, controls, support, and exception process are ready.

2. **Outcomes over activity**  
   Prompt counts, logins, time in the tool, and raw usage rankings are not performance measures.

3. **Human accountability**  
   AI assists; accountable professionals decide, verify, explain, and sign off.

4. **Appropriate non-use is compliant behavior**  
   Employees must not be pressured to use AI where it is unsafe, unlawful, inaccessible, unnecessary, or professionally inappropriate.

5. **Global guardrails, local execution**  
   Local teams may adapt language, examples, sequencing, and consultation, but not minimum safeguards.

6. **Protected transparency**  
   Low-risk historical covert use will have a safe migration route, subject to legal and regulatory limits.

7. **Honest workforce communication**  
   The Group will not promise that jobs will be unaffected. It will commit to transparency, consultation, reskilling, mobility, redeployment where feasible, and fair process.

8. **Psychological safety is a control**  
   Employees must be able to report errors, risks, coercion, and job concerns without retaliation.

9. **Stop, remediate, and learn**  
   Rollout deadlines never override customer, legal, regulatory, safety, privacy, or employee-protection concerns.

## 3. Scope and operating model

The Group will publish a formal scope standard covering:

- Permanent employees.
- Contractors, contingent workers, secondees, interns, and temporary staff.
- Remote and hybrid workers.
- Subsidiaries, acquired entities, joint ventures, and shared-service centers.
- Outsourced operations where contractual controls are required.
- Employees with limited access, accessibility needs, or collective-agreement coverage.

Where the global model cannot legally be imposed, the relevant local council must document an equivalent or stronger control and escalate it to the Global AI Governance Committee.

Local law, regulatory requirements, works-council agreements, collective bargaining arrangements, and stronger local protections take precedence over global targets.

No country, function, team, or demographic group will be publicly ranked using raw adoption rates.

## 5. Workflow, regulatory, and model-risk governance

### Jurisdiction-by-use-case register

Before rollout, Legal, Regulatory Affairs, Privacy, HR, and Risk will maintain a register identifying, by jurisdiction and workflow:

- Insurance-supervision expectations.
- Consumer protection and fair-treatment obligations.
- Automated decision and adverse-action requirements.
- AI-specific high-risk-system rules.
- Data-protection impact assessments.
- Employment monitoring and algorithmic-management restrictions.
- Outsourcing and cloud-provider requirements.
- Explainability and recordkeeping obligations.
- Model-validation requirements.
- Copyright and intellectual-property restrictions.
- Legal privilege and professional secrecy.
- Cross-border transfer requirements.
- Accessibility and discrimination law.
- Regulatory notification and incident-reporting thresholds.

The register will specify approvers, evidence, retention requirements, review dates, and escalation routes where local requirements delay or prevent deployment.

### Workflow risk categories

| Category | Illustrative uses | Minimum controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-risk assistance** | Internal drafting, summaries, non-sensitive translation | Approved environment, basic training, user verification |
| **Medium-risk assistance** | Research, coding, analysis, customer correspondence | Role training, source checking, documented human review, quality monitoring |
| **High-risk assistance** | Claims triage, underwriting support, fraud indicators, medical or financial analysis, regulated advice | Formal risk assessment, validation, specialist accreditation, enhanced monitoring, documented override |
| **Restricted** | Uses with heightened customer, privacy, employment, or regulatory implications | Exceptional approval, local legal and risk sign-off, enhanced controls |
| **Prohibited** | Fully automated consequential decisions or processing that cannot meet applicable standards | Not permitted |

Every workflow approval must document:

- Purpose and expected benefit.
- Data categories, processing location, hosting, and transfers.
- Applicable legal, regulatory, and professional obligations.
- Model, vendor, and integration dependencies.
- Human-review requirements.
- Quality, fairness, customer, and control measures.
- Outage and fallback arrangements.
- Training and operating procedures.
- Pause, redesign, retirement, and reapproval triggers.
- Approval expiry and review date.

### AI and model lifecycle controls

Maintain a central inventory of tools, models, vendors, versions, workflows, owners, and approvals. Controls must cover:

- Pre-use validation.
- Bias, accessibility, and disparate-impact testing.
- Performance and drift monitoring.
- Vendor and model-change notification.
- Prompt, retrieval, and integration change control.
- Versioning and audit trails.
- Revalidation after material change.
- Rollback and fallback procedures.
- Incident linkage to model and version.
- Retirement and records retention.

Risk standards will define monitoring frequency, escalation levels, performance and drift thresholds, rollback authority, and mandatory reapproval triggers.

### Meaningful human review

Human review must be proportionate to risk and must not be a rubber stamp. Requirements will specify:

- Reviewer competence and accreditation.
- Authority to reject or override output.
- Verification of facts, calculations, sources, and citations.
- Documentation of material judgments and overrides.
- Escalation for uncertainty, bias, or customer impact.
- Sampling and independent quality audits.
- Workload capacity sufficient for genuine review.

## 6. Adoption diagnosis and differentiated responses

Low usage must not be interpreted as a single condition. It may indicate appropriate non-use, covert use, lack of access, unsuitable workflows, privacy concerns, professional identity, or employment anxiety.

Each country and function will be assessed across two dimensions:

- **Behavior:** open adoption, selective use, covert use, non-use, or active resistance.
- **Motivation:** perceived value, capability gap, privacy concern, professional identity, employment anxiety, regulatory caution, communication barrier, or access constraint.

Motivation must not be inferred from dashboard activity alone.

| Adoption pattern | Response |
|---|---|
| **Open adopters and champions** | Provide sandbox and advanced access. Recognize reusable workflows, peer support, quality improvement, and risk spotting—not activity volume. Require champions to model verification and appropriate non-use. |
| **Pragmatic or selective users** | Provide fast, integrated, role-specific workflows, local examples, and guided practice. Demonstrate reduced rework, cycle time, or administrative burden. |
| **Covert users** | Offer confidential migration and remediation for low-risk historical use. Identify capability, speed, language, access, integration, and policy gaps driving shadow use, then address the root cause. |
| **Privacy-sensitive or regulated teams** | Lead with lawful data use, hosting, retention, auditability, redaction, and explicit “do not use” cases. Use synthetic or approved data. Treat appropriate refusal as good control behavior. |
| **Professional-identity groups** | Have respected practitioners define suitable tasks, quality standards, review points, and prohibited uses. Preserve expert sign-off and recognize quality protection. |
| **Employees fearing job loss** | Provide the Workforce Impact Compact, protected learning time, reskilling, mobility, redeployment where feasible, and workload monitoring. |
| **Active resisters** | Distinguish good-faith disagreement, whistleblowing, safety concerns, and collective activity from misconduct. Use evidence, listening, and co-design rather than shame or pressure. |
| **Low-readiness or excluded groups** | Resolve language, accessibility, connectivity, licensing, device, reliability, and workload barriers before treating low adoption as resistance. |

An initial increase in shadow-use disclosures, questions, or near-miss reporting may indicate improved trust and visibility. It must be interpreted alongside severity, technical evidence, and survey results.

## 7. Protected migration from covert use

Each rollout population will receive a **90-day protected remediation window**, subject to local legal, regulatory, labor, and employee-relations review. The period may be adjusted locally where consultation or investigation requirements demand it, without weakening minimum protections.

Employees may disclose low-risk historical use to receive:

- Migration to the approved toolkit.
- Data-remediation support.
- Policy clarification.
- Training and workflow assistance.
- Confidential coaching.

Protection does not remove duties relating to:

- Ongoing exposure.
- Customer, health, financial, privileged, or regulated data.
- Deliberate concealment or malicious conduct.
- Material security, legal, regulatory, or customer harm.
- Mandatory reporting or whistleblowing obligations.

The remediation protocol will define:

- Historical-use cut-off.
- Low-risk criteria.
- Independent risk-assessment authority.
- Disclosure access, retention, and deletion.
- When confidentiality ends.
- Challenge and appeal rights.
- Separation from routine performance systems.
- Routing to formal breach, security, legal, or regulatory processes where required.

The channel should be independent of line management wherever feasible. It is protected remediation, not unconditional amnesty.

## 8. Exception and appropriate non-use process

Every designated workflow will have a simple, binding exception process.

### Permitted grounds

- Legal, regulatory, privacy, or professional restriction.
- Sensitive data that cannot safely be processed.
- Tool outage, unreliability, inaccessibility, or material unsuitability.
- Customer or conduct risk.
- Lack of required training or approval.
- Local collective, accessibility, or employee-representative requirements.
- Good-faith professional judgment.

### Operating rules

- Low-risk exceptions may be self-documented through a standard form.
- Manager approval is not required for legal, safety-related, or protected professional-judgment exceptions.
- Pending a decision, employees may use an approved fallback process without penalty.
- Emergency non-use during an outage is protected and recorded later if required.
- Recurring exceptions are reviewed by the workflow owner and may trigger workflow redesign.
- Employees may appeal rejected exceptions through HR, Risk, Privacy, or an independent channel.
- Good-faith use of the exception process cannot reduce a performance rating.

The protocol will specify decision owners, evidence requirements, service levels, duration, renewal rules, and appeal timelines.

## 9. Leadership, managers, and communications

### Leadership commitments

Executives must communicate that:

- The capability is mandatory only where designated and suitable.
- Professional judgment and appropriate non-use are protected.
- Customer, regulatory, quality, and conduct obligations override adoption targets.
- Usage data is not a crude individual productivity ranking.
- Jobs and skills may change, and the Group will provide transparent support.
- Reporting mistakes, risks, or coercion is protected.

Leaders should demonstrate both appropriate AI use and appropriate non-use.

### Manager requirements

Managers must:

- Discuss relevant workflows, not demand arbitrary usage percentages.
- Provide protected training and experimentation time.
- Ask what barriers prevent safe adoption.
- Reinforce data, review, and escalation rules.
- Use quality, customer outcomes, workload, and controls alongside adoption information.
- Avoid screenshots, prompt inspection, individual rankings, and unlike-role comparisons.
- Apply exception rules consistently.
- Recognize sound judgment, risk identification, and quality improvement.
- Escalate privacy, technical, workforce, and employee-relations concerns.

When usage is low, managers must first check:

1. Is the workflow designated and suitable?
2. Does the employee have access, training, language, and accessibility support?
3. Is an exception or outage involved?
4. Is the tool reliable and fit for purpose?
5. Is there evidence of quality, control, or customer impact?
6. Has coaching and support been provided?

Only after these checks, and with HR review, may repeated non-compliance with a valid designated workflow be addressed through normal fair-performance processes. Activity volume alone is never sufficient.

Coercive metric use, retaliation, falsification, unauthorized data linkage, or metric manipulation are conduct matters subject to investigation and consequences.

### Communications

Core message:

> The toolkit is mandatory for designated suitable work, not every task. It supports professional judgment rather than replacing it. We will protect data, explain what is measured, provide support and reskilling, and respond to concerns without retaliation. Employees remain accountable for decisions and customer outcomes.

Communications must explain:

1. Why the Group is acting.
2. What is mandatory and what is not.
3. How privacy and lawful data use are protected.
4. How roles and skills may change.
5. What reskilling, mobility, and support are available.
6. How incidents, coercion, and retaliation can be reported.
7. What has changed as a result of employee feedback.

Use local languages, town halls, manager briefings, role-specific demonstrations, peer stories, FAQs, anonymous Q&A, works-council briefings, and small-group listening.

Local adaptation should reflect cultural context without stereotyping:

- Anonymous questions where employees may not challenge leaders openly.
- Respected professional voices in autonomy-oriented expert cultures.
- Controls and evidence in privacy-sensitive environments.
- Private remediation in face-sensitive settings.
- Accessible and low-bandwidth formats where required.

## 10. Workforce, professional pride, and psychological safety

### Workforce Impact Compact

Before broad productivity reporting, publish:

- Expected task and role changes.
- Skills likely to increase in value.
- Reskilling budget and protected learning hours.
- Internal mobility and redeployment processes.
- Treatment of contractors and contingent workers.
- How released capacity will initially be used.
- Workload, overtime, burnout, and service-quality safeguards.
- The role and limits of AI data in workforce decisions.
- Consultation, notice, review, and appeal processes.
- Equality and discrimination monitoring.

Individual usage activity must not be a standalone criterion for promotion, discipline, performance rating, or redundancy. Aggregated workflow evidence may inform workforce planning only through established governance, legal review, employee consultation, minimum aggregation thresholds, and safeguards against re-identification or indirect profiling.

Initial capacity benefits should prioritize customer service, quality improvement, backlog reduction, training, and control improvement rather than automatic headcount reduction.

Professional experts should co-design suitable and unsuitable tasks, review standards, records, and development pathways. Skepticism must not be treated as incompetence.

## 11. Privacy and dashboard governance

### Usage-data charter

Publish before rollout. It must specify:

- Data collected and source.
- Whether prompts, outputs, metadata, or content are retained.
- Provider and administrator access.
- Model-training exclusions.
- Hosting, residency, and cross-border transfers.
- Retention, deletion, and legal-hold rules.
- Permitted and prohibited uses.
- Employee notice, correction, objection, and appeal rights.
- Audit arrangements.

Technology must maintain a data-flow map, vendor-access map, retention schedule, and re-identification assessment.

### Dashboard design

Dashboards must separate:

1. **Readiness:** access, reliability, training, support, language, and accessibility.
2. **Responsible adoption:** relevant approved use, shadow-use migration, human review, and appropriate non-use.
3. **Experience:** trust, psychological safety, job security, workload, and manager support.
4. **Business outcomes:** cycle time, quality, rework, customer outcomes, and capacity released.
5. **Risk:** incidents, near misses, data exposure, bias, audit findings, and resolution time.

Every metric requires:

- Purpose and owner.
- Definition and denominator.
- Data source and quality limits.
- Access rules.
- Retention period.
- Interpretation guidance.
- Prohibited uses.
- Review and retirement date.

Default reporting is aggregate, team, or workflow level. Apply:

- Minimum cell sizes and small-cell suppression.
- Pseudonymization.
- Longitudinal re-identification controls.
- Access recertification.
- Audit-log review.
- Restrictions on linking dashboard data with HR or productivity data.
- Extract-retention limits.

Managers may not:

- Set prompt, login, or time-in-tool targets.
- Request screenshots.
- Routinely inspect prompts or content.
- Compare unlike roles, workflows, or countries.
- Treat low usage alone as poor performance.
- Require use in prohibited or unsuitable work.

Any employment decision informed by AI-related information requires human review, documented context, employee notice, access to relevant evidence, and appeal. Appeals should pause adverse action where legally feasible.

## 12. Enablement and support

### Core curriculum

All employees complete practical training covering:

- AI capabilities and limitations.
- Approved, restricted, and prohibited uses.
- Data classification, privacy, confidentiality, and security.
- Prompting, verification, hallucination, bias, copyright, and recordkeeping.
- Human review, sign-off, and escalation.
- Usage-data and performance-dashboard rules.
- Incident and near-miss reporting.
- When not to use AI.

### Role-based pathways

Additional pathways will cover:

- Claims and customer operations.
- Underwriting and actuarial.
- Legal, compliance, and risk.
- Finance and corporate functions.
- Technology and data.
- Managers.
- Executives and governance leaders.

Higher-risk users require scenario-based assessment or accreditation before accessing relevant capabilities.

Training will be available in relevant languages, accessible formats, and low-bandwidth options. Practice should use synthetic or approved data. Completion alone is insufficient; practical competence must be assessed.

### Support model

- **Tier 0:** searchable policy, approved prompts, examples, workflow templates, and diagnostics.
- **Tier 1:** local help desk, champions, office hours, and manager support.
- **Tier 2:** privacy, legal, risk, security, accessibility, and specialist workflow support.
- **Tier 3:** global product, engineering, model-risk, and incident teams.

Before each rollout gate, confirm funding and capacity for:

- Help-desk coverage and response times.
- Champion coverage.
- Translation and accessibility.
- Training facilitators and protected learning time.
- Incident response.
- Local legal, privacy, risk, and employee-relations support.
- Licensing, integration, device, connectivity, reliability, and product-fitness requirements.

Critical incidents should be acknowledged within one business day, or faster where law or existing procedures require.

## 13. Product fitness standards

The approved toolkit must be demonstrably fit for designated workflows. Minimum standards should cover:

- Reliability and availability.
- Response time.
- Functionality and accuracy.
- Integration with relevant systems.
- Language support.
- Accessibility and assistive-technology compatibility.
- Data handling and residency.
- User experience and workflow effort.
- Support and recovery arrangements.

A workflow mandate must be suspended when the toolkit fails the applicable fitness standard or creates incentives for unsafe workarounds.

## 14. Implementation phases

### Phase 1: Establish control — first 30 days

- Appoint sponsors, program director, workflow owners, and local councils.
- Freeze individual usage-based employment actions pending dashboard approval.
- Baseline trust, shadow use, workload, job concerns, and access barriers.
- Inventory tools, models, vendors, use cases, and data flows.
- Draft the usage-data charter, Workforce Impact Compact, exception protocol, and remediation process.
- Complete jurisdictional legal, regulatory, privacy, labor, and works-council mapping.
- Select diverse pilot workflows.
- Train managers on psychological safety and non-coercive adoption.
- Approve the funded resource and dependency plan.

**Gate 1:** scope, lawful basis, data flows, employee rights, governance, resources, support, and metrics are defined.

### Phase 2: Co-design and pilot — days 31–90

- Complete workflow risk assessments and regulatory reviews.
- Pilot varied low- and medium-risk workflows.
- Include regulated teams, professional groups, low-readiness locations, and shadow-use populations.
- Test training, support, dashboards, exceptions, remediation, and human review.
- Establish pre-rollout business, quality, customer, workload, and risk baselines.

**Gate 2:** benefit is demonstrated, quality is stable or improved, support is adequate, risk is acceptable, and trust has not materially deteriorated.

### Phase 3: Controlled scale — months 4–6

- Roll out approved workflows by risk tier and readiness.
- Require practical assessment before higher-risk capability access.
- Expand local-language support, champions, and office hours.
- Review adoption gaps in context.
- Pause or redesign workflows failing quality, trust, workload, product-fitness, or control thresholds.

**Gate 3:** workflow approval, trained users, support capacity, local consultation, and compliant dashboard are in place.

### Phase 4: Governed mandate — months 7–12

- Require approved-tool use in designated suitable workflows.
- Apply contextual, human-reviewed performance processes.
- Track shadow-use migration, quality, customer outcomes, workload, workforce effects, and incidents.
- Publish progress, limitations, decisions, and corrective actions.
- Complete independent dashboard, privacy, and employment-data reviews.

### Phase 5: Sustain and optimize — beyond 12 months

- Revalidate models, vendors, use cases, and controls.
- Refresh training and policies.
- Retire unsafe or low-value workflows.
- Review customer, regulatory, workforce, and professional effects.
- Reapprove benefits before further expansion.

## 15. Measurement and benefits realization

Targets must be baseline-dependent, portfolio-level, and subject to quality, risk, workload, local-law, and product-fitness guardrails. They are not individual quotas.

### Capability and access

- Training and practical-assessment completion.
- Approved workflow coverage.
- Tool reliability, accessibility, and language availability.
- Support response and resolution times.
- Time to resolve access barriers.

### Responsible adoption

- Repeat use in validated workflows.
- Approved-tool use compared with estimated shadow use, with uncertainty disclosed.
- Correct data handling.
- Human-review compliance.
- Appropriate non-use.
- Voluntary migration and remediation activity.

### Employee outcomes

- Trust in data governance.
- Psychological safety to report concerns.
- Professional respect.
- Job-security and fairness sentiment.
- Manager behavior.
- Workload, overtime, burnout, attrition, and internal mobility.

### Business and customer outcomes

- Cycle time and capacity released.
- First-time-right quality and rework.
- Claims, underwriting, service, and documentation outcomes.
- Customer satisfaction and complaints.
- Control adherence and decision quality.
- Sustainable benefit after launch support and incentives taper.

### Risk outcomes

- Privacy and security incidents.
- Sensitive-data submissions.
- Harmful or unsupported outputs.
- Bias, discrimination, and accessibility findings.
- Human-review failures.
- Near-miss severity and resolution time.
- Regulatory and audit findings.

### Benefits-realization standard

For each workflow:

- Establish a pre-rollout baseline.
- Use a comparable control group or phased comparison where feasible.
- Adjust for seasonality, staffing, workload, and case mix.
- Measure quality, customer outcomes, workload, and capacity alongside speed.
- Document assumptions and attribution limits.
- Require minimum sample sizes and confidence thresholds.
- Validate benefits independently through Finance, Strategy, Transformation, or Internal Audit.
- Reassess after launch support and incentives taper.
- Report failed or inconclusive business cases rather than scaling by default.

A workflow scales only when it demonstrates measurable improvement in a relevant business outcome without material deterioration in quality, customer, employee, or control outcomes.

## 16. Listening, escalation, and appeals

### Listening loops

- **Weekly:** access, usability, training, and support issues.
- **Monthly:** privacy, security, legal, compliance, and model-risk review.
- **Quarterly:** employee pulse surveys and listening groups.
- **Quarterly:** workflow value, quality, customer, and control review.
- **Semiannually:** workforce, skills, mobility, attrition, workload, and equality review.

Each loop requires a named owner, service-level commitment, escalation threshold, and “you said, we did” communication.

### Reporting channels

Provide separate routes for:

- Technical problems.
- Harmful or inaccurate outputs.
- Privacy and security incidents.
- Policy questions.
- Coercive monitoring or metric manipulation.
- Retaliation.
- Job-impact concerns.
- Accessibility and cultural barriers.

Good-faith reporting, lawful disagreement, whistleblowing, and collective rights must be protected.

### Employee rights and appeals

Employees must have:

- Clear notice of monitoring and data use.
- The ability to correct inaccurate usage records.
- A route to challenge workflow designation or exception decisions.
- Access to relevant evidence.
- Human review before adverse employment action.
- An independent appeal route.
- Defined response times.
- Local-language and accessible appeal channels.
- Protection against retaliation.

Appeals should pause adverse action where legally feasible. Recurring appeals must be analyzed for systemic problems in policy, workflow design, management behavior, or dashboard use.

## 17. Principal risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Covert use remains hidden | Protected migration, anonymous listening, rapid approved access, product improvements |
| Disclosure becomes a disciplinary trap | Clear protection boundaries, independent assessment, restricted access, no automatic punishment for low-risk historical use |
| Dashboard becomes surveillance | Data charter, aggregation, access controls, audit, appeal, prohibited uses |
| Prompt inflation or performative adoption | No activity quotas; outcome-based measures; anomaly review |
| Manager coercion | Manager training, reporting channels, audits, conduct consequences |
| Professional pride is damaged | Expert co-design, human sign-off, recognition of judgment and risk prevention |
| Headcount fear suppresses adoption | Workforce compact, reskilling, mobility, workload monitoring, honest communication |
| Cultural variation is stereotyped | Local evidence, local councils, adaptable delivery, common minimum rights |
| Quality declines as speed rises | Quality gates, sampling, customer measures, workload monitoring, pause criteria |
| Sensitive data is mishandled | Classification, redaction, approved environments, DLP, clear non-use rules |
| Access inequality distorts adoption | Support thresholds, language and accessibility testing, protected learning time |
| Incident reporting falls | Non-retaliation, anonymous routes, separation from adoption scores |
| Benefits become immediate headcount targets | Workforce governance, consultation, capacity safeguards |
| Small-group dashboards enable re-identification | Minimum cell sizes, suppression, aggregation, access review |
| Model or vendor changes invalidate controls | Versioning, change control, revalidation, vendor monitoring |
| Local law prevents global deployment | Equivalent-control process, documented deviation, escalation to the Global Committee |
| Tool is materially inferior for the workflow | Product-fitness standards, fallback process, suspension of mandate |
| Human review becomes nominal | Risk-tiered review standards, accreditation, sampling, audit, workload capacity |

## 18. Stop, escalate, and reactivation criteria

Pause or redesign a workflow when:

- Customer, regulatory, legal, conduct, or safety harm occurs.
- Sensitive data is exposed.
- Quality or human-review compliance deteriorates.
- Bias or accessibility problems emerge.
- Usage rises while trust, workload, burnout, or incidents worsen.
- Managers coerce or manipulate metrics.
- Employees suppress reporting or professional judgment.
- Adoption gaps reflect unresolved infrastructure, language, accessibility, or tool limitations.
- The tool becomes materially unreliable or inferior.
- A model, vendor, prompt, retrieval layer, or integration changes without revalidation.

A paused workflow may return to mandatory status only when:

- The root cause is documented.
- Corrective actions are implemented.
- Required legal, risk, privacy, security, and accessibility approvals are renewed.
- Human-review capacity is confirmed.
- Affected employees and managers are informed.
- The workflow passes the relevant scale gate.

## 19. Executive success test

The program should not be judged by maximum visible activity.

It succeeds when:

- Approved use increases while shadow use declines.
- Employees can explain when AI should and should not be used.
- Professional experts participate in governance.
- Quality, customer outcomes, and controls remain stable or improve.
- Trust and psychological safety improve.
- Workload does not intensify unsustainably.
- Managers stop using arbitrary activity targets.
- Reskilling and mobility are credible and used.
- Benefits are independently validated and replicated responsibly.
- Local adaptation strengthens adoption without weakening global safeguards.

> **The defining test is whether the Group achieves trusted, lawful, professionally accountable, culturally adaptable, and sustainable AI adoption that improves insurance outcomes while preserving privacy, dignity, psychological safety, human judgment, and credible employment safeguards.**
