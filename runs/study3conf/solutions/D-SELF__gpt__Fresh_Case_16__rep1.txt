# Executive summary

Launch a **configurable household assistant**, not a translated Western personal assistant. Keep one global interaction model:

> **Detect → understand → check context and permissions → confirm if risky → act → show what happened.**

Localize five independent layers:

1. **Language:** honorifics, politeness, dialects, code-switching, names, scripts, and pronunciation.
2. **Identity:** optional profiles, preferred names, accessibility, language, and response preferences.
3. **Permissions:** who may control, hear, change, or purchase.
4. **Household policy:** rooms, guests, children, elders, caregivers, workers, announcements, and retention.
5. **Compliance:** consent, biometrics, retention, data residency, transfers, and payments.

The product should compete on **predictability, household usefulness, and visible privacy control**, not on sounding maximally human.

---

# 1. Interaction style

## Global default

Use a **neutral, respectful service register**:

- Concise and competent
- Polite without excessive deference
- Neither childish nor overly familiar
- Not gender-coded by default
- No unsolicited humor, teasing, or imitation
- Not framed as a servant, elder, religious authority, or human companion

The assistant should understand direct commands, polite requests, questions, indirect hints, elliptical household speech, and mixed-language commands.

It should not execute every indirect comment. For example:

> “It’s getting warm in here.”

should produce:

> “Would you like me to lower the temperature?”

rather than changing the thermostat automatically.

Users should be able to select:

- Concise polite, warm polite, formal, or locally appropriate casual style
- Name, title, or no form of address
- Language and code-switching behavior
- Voice, response length, and volume
- Spoken, visual, or silent responses
- Whether personal information may be spoken in shared rooms

Do not infer authority, age, gender, nationality, religion, or relationship from voice, accent, language, or politeness.

## Risk-based confirmation

**Normally no confirmation:** lights, timers, music, weather, and routine appliance adjustments.

**Multimodal confirmation required:** door unlocking, security changes, payments, external messages, location sharing, cameras, recordings, health or financial information, and household-wide privacy changes.

Voice recognition may personalize the experience but must not be the sole authentication factor for high-risk actions.

# 2. Household roles and permissions

Treat the household as the shared operating context while preserving individual privacy.

## Supported roles

Provide configurable, non-stigmatizing roles for:

- Multiple adult administrators
- Household members
- Children
- Elders
- Guests
- Caregivers
- Domestic workers
- Drivers or other service providers
- Temporary users

No person should become the default administrator because they purchased the device, completed setup, are older, male, or have a recognized voice.

## Granular permissions

Permissions should be limited by **person, room, device, function, and time**.

| Area | Example controls |
|---|---|
| Appliances | Rooms and devices a person may control |
| Security | Locks, alarms, cameras, intercom |
| Information | Calendars, reminders, health, finance, account data |
| Commerce | Shopping, delivery, payments |
| Management | Profiles, routines, privacy settings |
| Communication | Announcements, calls, external messages |
| Data | Voice profile, history, recordings, personalization |

A caregiver or worker might control appliances and deliveries without accessing cameras, recordings, purchases, or private calendars.

Required controls:

- Multiple administrators
- Temporary access with expiry
- One-step profile suspension
- Voice-profile and personal-history deletion
- Permission-change audit history
- Private responses for personal information
- Shared lists, calendars, routines, and announcements
- A notice when a request affects another person’s data or settings

If identity is uncertain:

> “I’m not sure who is speaking. Please use the app or confirm on the device.”

The assistant must not silently grant access to the nearest or highest-privilege user.

## Conflict handling

For conflicting household settings, use this order:

1. Physical safety and legal requirements
2. Device security
3. Individual privacy
4. Household preferences
5. Convenience and personalization

Privacy-protective restrictions should prevail for recordings, cameras, personal data, locks, payments, and external messages. The app should show who changed a rule, when, and what it affects.

# 3. Privacy signaling

Privacy must be visible on the device, in the app, and during setup.

## Distinguish six states

Clearly communicate whether:

1. The microphone is enabled for local wake-word detection
2. A wake-word candidate was detected
3. Command audio is actively captured
4. Audio is transmitted off-device
5. Audio or transcripts are retained
6. Data is accessed for human review or model improvement

A single ambiguous light is insufficient. Provide:

- Hardware-backed microphone mute with an obvious physical state
- Activation tone or clear visual feedback
- Local wake-word detection by default
- Local processing of basic device commands where feasible
- No raw-audio retention by default unless affirmatively enabled
- Camera-free hardware or a physical camera shutter
- Quiet and “no-audio-response” modes
- Room privacy zones and schedules
- Private notifications through a phone, display, or earbuds

## Consent and data controls

Obtain separate, understandable consent for:

- Voice-profile creation or update
- Recording retention
- Personalization
- Human review
- Advertising
- Model training
- Camera activation
- Sharing information with other household members

Ordinary conversation must never automatically enroll someone in voice recognition. Store protected voice templates rather than reusable raw recordings, and provide pause, deletion, re-enrollment, and non-biometric fallback.

Provide practical shared-home modes:

- No-profile guest mode
- No-retention mode
- Household recording-off mode
- Private-response mode
- Temporary worker/caregiver mode
- Room- and time-based privacy suspension

Document retention and deletion for raw audio, transcripts, voice templates, personalization data, diagnostics, backups, third-party copies, and training data. State clearly what is deleted immediately, what is removed on a backup schedule, and any legal exceptions.

Essential privacy controls—including mute, deletion, and private responses—must not be paywalled.

# 4. Feature prioritization

## Tier 1: Launch-critical trust and utility

Prioritize:

- Hardware mute and clear microphone-state indicators
- Local wake-word processing
- Basic local device control where feasible
- Privacy dashboard and deletion
- Household roles, guest and child modes
- Room-, time-, and device-level permissions
- Silent app and physical controls
- Risk-based multimodal confirmation
- High-quality local speech recognition
- Climate, appliance, timer, reminder, list, calendar, and weather functions
- Offline or degraded-mode support for basic routines
- Accessibility for elders, people with disabilities, and second-language users

## Tier 2: Local differentiation

Add after trust is established:

- Honorific and speech-register handling
- Code-switching
- Speaker-specific preferences
- Family coordination
- Quiet-room and private-notification features
- Energy and climate optimization
- Local media, traffic, weather, and public-service integrations
- Caregiver and worker permissions
- Intercom and announcements with privacy-aware routing
- Optional, source-labeled religious-calendar features in relevant Gulf markets

## Tier 3: Defer until independently audited

Delay:

- Voice-assisted payments and commerce
- Advanced locks, cameras, and access control
- Sensitive health inference
- Broad advertising personalization
- Open-ended companionship
- Unbounded religious advice
- Hidden monitoring of workers, children, or guests

# 5. Country adaptations

These are product hypotheses to validate through local research, not assumptions about every resident.

## Japan

### Interaction

- Default to natural *desu/masu* service speech.
- Offer formal speech but avoid excessive *keigo*, which can sound comic or servile.
- Understand polite requests and indirect comments.
- Support user-selected surnames, given names, family titles, or no address.
- Test sentence-final particles, regional variation, generations, and disability-related speech needs.
- Provide Japanese name, address, homophone, and script disambiguation.

### Household and privacy

- Prioritize quiet operation for apartments and shared rooms.
- Offer elder-friendly volume, display, and controls without assuming elders are passive users.
- Support single-person and multigenerational homes.
- Provide bedroom, bathroom, and guest-room privacy schedules.
- Keep personal reminders and information off shared-room announcements by default.
- Use authoritative, source-labeled disaster information; do not claim delivery unless verified.

### Launch focus

Japanese appliance, climate, reminder, calendar, accessibility, and privacy functions. Gate intercom and disaster features separately.

## South Korea

### Interaction

- Support everyday polite *haeyo-che*, formal *hapsho-che*, and neutral display forms.
- Permit casual *banmal* only when explicitly selected.
- Handle Korean-English switching, fast colloquial speech, technology terms, and regional accents.
- Do not infer speech level from age, gender, or voice.
- Avoid automatic relationship terms such as *oppa*, *unni*, *hyung*, or *halmeoni*.

### Household and privacy

- Prioritize apartment-friendly volume, nighttime quiet mode, and false-wake-word handling.
- Support family and school coordination and elder assistance.
- Separate child safety alerts from routine searches, conversations, location, or inferred emotions.
- Treat building intercom and access control as separate security programs.

### Launch focus

Korean appliance control, timers, schedules, quiet operation, family coordination, and private responses.

## Thailand

### Interaction

- Support Standard Thai with clearly stated coverage limits.
- Handle Thai-English switching, tonal variation, names, nicknames, and visual correction for homophones.
- Use *ครับ*, *ค่ะ*, and *คะ* contextually rather than as a mandatory gender setting.
- Permit neutral speech without requiring users to declare gender.
- Test regional varieties, including Isan, Northern, and Southern speech where demand supports them.
- Do not treat English as a more advanced mode.

### Household and privacy

- Support multigenerational, single-person, renter, migrant, and multilingual households.
- Prioritize cooking timers, climate and appliance control, elder and child modes.
- Provide room- and time-limited caregiver and worker permissions.
- Give workers notices in their chosen language and delete profiles when employment ends.
- Provide clear state recovery after power or connectivity interruptions.

### Launch focus

Standard Thai and Thai-English support where quality thresholds are met, core appliance and climate control, timers, lists, guest mode, and privacy controls.

## Gulf markets

The Gulf should be managed as separate country programs at minimum:

- Saudi Arabia
- United Arab Emirates
- Qatar
- Kuwait
- Bahrain
- Oman

### Required country decisions

Before launch in each country, specify:

- Supported Arabic dialects and Modern Standard Arabic
- English and evidence-based expatriate-language support
- Arabic-English switching scope
- Recognition and generation thresholds
- Data-hosting and cross-border-transfer rules
- Emergency, weather, media, delivery, and public-service sources
- Prayer-time calculation methods and local conventions
- Payment and identity requirements
- Customer-support languages
- Features excluded from launch

Do not market “Arabic” as one performance category or select expatriate languages solely based on worker demographics.

### Interaction and household use

- Support relevant local dialects, Modern Standard Arabic, English, and validated additional languages.
- Provide right-to-left interfaces, Arabic name handling, and local pronunciation.
- Do not infer religion, nationality, kinship, gender, or authority from language or accent.
- Prioritize climate control, energy management, air quality, room privacy, silent phone/earbud interaction, guest mode, and private notifications.
- Provide functional permissions for families, caregivers, workers, and drivers without hidden monitoring.
- Include citizen, expatriate, mixed-nationality, single-person, apartment, and non-domestic-worker households in research.

### Religious features

Offer prayer-time reminders, Hijri/Gregorian calendars, and Ramadan schedules only as optional, source-labeled features. Expose calculation methods and location sources. Distinguish calendar calculation from religious interpretation, and do not present the assistant as a religious authority.

# 6. Rollout sequencing

## Phase 1: Foundations and feasibility — Q1–Q2 2026

Complete:

- Hardware mute and indicator design
- Privacy and deletion architecture
- Shared permission model
- Country-specific legal and data-flow reviews
- Language and dialect benchmarks
- Evidence register for cultural and demand assumptions
- Minimum viable launch definition
- Support, repair, connectivity, and pricing plan

**Gate:** no country proceeds without passing privacy comprehension, permission enforcement, language quality, and compliance reviews.

## Phase 2: Controlled pilots — Q3 2026

Pilot representative homes, including:

- Single-person and multigenerational households
- Apartments and villas
- Multilingual homes
- Elders, children, guests, workers, and caregivers
- Noisy environments and connectivity interruptions

Use independently recruited workers and caregivers, separate consent, appropriate compensation, and protection from employer or household retaliation.

## Phase 3: Staged country launches — late 2026 onward

Do not launch all markets simultaneously. Launch only the country packages that pass their gates. A practical approach is:

1. Launch the strongest validated language and privacy package first.
2. Add further countries after independent audit of the shared platform.
3. Release basic utility before intercom, building, emergency, religious, caregiving, and commerce integrations.
4. Expand dialects and expatriate languages based on observed demand and measured performance.

## Phase 4: Advanced capabilities — 2027 onward

Introduce payments, commerce, advanced security, cameras, and third-party automation only after high-risk authentication, deletion, incident response, and independent privacy audits are demonstrated.

# 7. Key risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Uneven recognition across accents, ages, or disabilities | Publish segmented performance; provide safe fallback and multimodal control |
| Incorrect honorifics or speech levels | Native-speaker review of successes, refusals, and errors |
| Voice spoofing or misidentification | Never use voice as sole high-risk authentication |
| Household power imbalances | Independent worker research, transparent permissions, profile deletion |
| Guest or visitor privacy exposure | No-profile guest and no-retention modes |
| Conflicts between household administrators | Privacy-protective conflict hierarchy and audit history |
| Overpromised deletion | Data inventory, backup schedule, technical verification, clear exceptions |
| False emergency or religious information | Authoritative sources, uncertainty language, content governance, delivery verification |
| Gulf overgeneralization | Six-country launch matrix and separate language/compliance decisions |
| Privacy features delayed by hardware or cost | Make mute, deletion, and private response core requirements on every device tier |
| Connectivity or power outages | Offline basic routines and clear failure feedback |
| Product perceived as surveillance | Opt-in personalization, visible processing status, no hidden monitoring |
| Overly broad cultural defaults | Evidence register, segment-based research, reversible settings |

# 8. Success metrics

Metrics must define the denominator, sample, segments, confidence interval, baseline, and severity. Use stop-ship rules for critical incidents.

## Trust and privacy

- At least 90% of representative pilot users correctly identify microphone, capture, transmission, and retention states.
- At least 90% complete recent-activity deletion without assistance.
- Zero critical unauthorized lock, payment, camera, recording, or privacy-setting actions in controlled pilots.
- Track false activations per household-day and per wake-word opportunity.
- No material disparity in privacy comprehension across tested groups.

## Interaction quality

- Less than 5% unintended execution of ambiguous indirect requests in scenario testing.
- At least 85% correct human-rated handling of local speech registers and honorific scenarios.
- 20% reduction in repeated corrections for local names and mixed-language commands.
- Recognition and safe-failure results reported separately by language, dialect, age, disability, and second-language status.

## Household adoption

- At least 70% of active households configure a permission or privacy rule.
- At least 60% use two or more localized utilities.
- Six-month household retention meets or exceeds the company’s comparable-device benchmark.
- At least 80% of worker and caregiver pilot participants understand their permissions and data treatment.

## High-risk expansion

Before payments, cameras, or access-control expansion:

- Zero critical incidents in monitored pilots.
- At least 95% successful step-up authentication for high-risk actions.
- Unauthorized-action rate below a defined threshold with a documented denominator and severity model.
- Independent privacy, security, accessibility, linguistic, and cultural audits completed.

---

# Recommended positioning

> **A respectful, private household assistant that works the way each family speaks and lives.**

Avoid positioning the product as an obedient servant, a replacement for family or caregivers, a surveillance device, a religious authority, or a system requiring one “correct” way to speak.

The strategic advantage is **predictable assistance, explicit household authority, culturally competent interaction, and privacy users can see and control**.
