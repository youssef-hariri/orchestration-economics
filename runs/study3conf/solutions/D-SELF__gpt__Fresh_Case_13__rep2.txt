# Climate Microinsurance Distribution and Business-Model Strategy

## 1. Executive summary

The insurer should adopt a **regionally adapted hybrid model** rather than a single global distribution channel.

- **Trusted local institutions**—cooperatives, producer organizations, MFIs, banks, and selected community groups—should build legitimacy, explain coverage, and support assisted enrollment.
- **Digital and financial rails**—MNO agents, mobile money, USSD, banks, and fintechs—should collect premiums, confirm policies, service customers, and make individual claim payments.
- **The insurer must retain control** of product design, underwriting, trigger validation, claims decisions, complaints, data governance, customer protection, and portfolio risk.
- **Reinsurers, public facilities, or donors** should absorb defined layers of correlated catastrophe risk and early-stage model uncertainty.

> **Recommended position: trusted locally, transacted digitally, governed centrally, priced transparently, and scaled only on evidence.**

Regional recommendations are hypotheses, not universal facts. Final decisions must be made at **country, district, crop, peril, and customer-segment level**.

| Region | Primary pathway | Secondary pathway | Initial product posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| East Africa | Cooperative/farmer-group acquisition plus mobile-money servicing | Community referral plus MNO fulfillment | Simple drought or rainfall-deficit cover; hybrid review where data are weak |
| South Asia | MFI, bank, cooperative, or farmer-group embedded model | Community or faith-group education with licensed fulfillment | Area-yield or hybrid cover where credible; rainfall products elsewhere |
| Central America | Producer/value-chain association plus rural finance | Community-assisted digital distribution | Crop- and peril-specific drought, excess-rain, flood, or cyclone cover |

The pilot should be judged primarily on **comprehension, claims credibility, basis-risk experience, renewal, full unit economics, consumer protection, and stressed portfolio resilience**, not enrollment volume alone.

---

# 2. Strategic principles and hard gates

## Principles

1. **Separate trust from transactions.** The most trusted organization need not collect premiums or distribute claims.
2. **Make the insurer visible.** Customers should know who underwrites the policy, determines triggers, pays claims, and handles complaints.
3. **Automate only where data support the promise.**
4. **Explain non-payment before purchase.** Customers must understand severe loss with payout, visible loss without payout, and mild loss without payout.
5. **Treat basis risk as a customer, retention, and portfolio risk**, not merely an actuarial issue.
6. **Price the full economics**, including claims, capital, reinsurance, distribution, payment fees, servicing, fraud, taxes, and subsidy administration.
7. **Use subsidies to improve access, not conceal weak economics.**
8. **Control concentration from the first policy issued.**
9. **Pre-register pilot thresholds before enrollment.**
10. **Maintain fallback routes** for payment, data, customer support, and partner failure.

## Hard gates

Do not begin live enrollment unless:

- product, insurer, intermediary, group-policy, digital-consent, and payment permissions are confirmed;
- index or event data can be independently validated;
- regulated premium and claim-payment routes are proven;
- partner governance, reconciliation, and conduct controls are adequate;
- capital, reinsurance, and liquidity cover defined stress events;
- the product can be explained accurately in relevant languages and accessible formats.

A weighted market score cannot override a failed hard gate.

# 3. Customer and trust segmentation

Channel choice should be based on customer characteristics, not region alone.

| Segment | Characteristics | Preferred route |
|---|---|---|
| Organized smallholder | Cooperative or producer-group member; identifiable crop and location | Cooperative plus assisted digital |
| Financially active farmer | MFI, bank, input-credit, or procurement relationship | Embedded finance |
| Basic-phone/mobile-money user | Digital transactions but limited app use | MNO agent, USSD, voice |
| Digitally active commercial farmer | Smartphone and platform use | Fintech or value-chain platform |
| Low-trust or excluded household | Low insurance familiarity, low literacy, remote | Community referral plus licensed agent |
| Mobile or migrant household | Changing phone, location, beneficiary, or income | Rural finance or agent channel with strong record updates |

Measure three separate forms of trust:

1. **Intermediary trust:** confidence in the cooperative, agent, group, bank, or app.
2. **Insurer trust:** belief that the insurer will honor the policy.
3. **Index/process trust:** acceptance that the data and trigger fairly represent the insured risk.

Low index trust cannot be solved solely by using a more trusted distributor. It may require revised triggers, narrower geography, hybrid review, or a different product.

# 4. Product architecture

## 4.1 Product tiers

### Tier 1: Pure parametric cover

Pays when a predefined rainfall, temperature, wind, vegetation, or other index crosses a threshold.

**Best fit:** locations with stable, auditable, spatially representative data.

**Advantages:** low claims-administration cost, rapid settlement, limited fabricated individual-loss claims, and scalability.

**Risks:** spatial and functional basis risk, data-provider dependence, and visible loss without payout.

### Tier 2: Parametric cover with defined exception review

Uses automatic trigger calculation, with review for predefined conditions such as data outage, station failure, incorrect location, or documented geographic mismatch.

**Best fit:** markets where data are useful but not strong enough for fully automated settlement.

Exceptions must define eligible grounds, evidence, decision authority, service standards, audit trails, and remedies. They must not become discretionary negotiation over every claim.

### Tier 3: Area-yield or area-loss cover

Pays when measured yield or loss in a defined area falls below a threshold.

**Best fit:** organized production zones with credible crop-cutting, yield, or remote-sensing data.

**Trade-off:** potentially better alignment with agricultural outcomes, but higher operating cost, slower settlement, and continuing area-to-farm basis risk.

### Tier 4: Embedded resilience bundle

Combines insurance with credit, inputs, storage, extension, irrigation, or other services.

Insurance price, benefit, exclusions, and claims route must be separately disclosed. Resilience services must not obscure inadequate insurance coverage.

## 4.2 Initial product discipline

Start with one or two clearly defined perils per market:

- rainfall deficit or drought;
- excess rainfall or flood;
- cyclone or wind;
- heat stress where crop-response data are credible.

Avoid generalized “crop loss” language. Defer pest, disease, and comprehensive cover until surveillance and verification systems are robust.

If full coverage is unaffordable, first test:

1. lower limits;
2. catastrophe-only cover;
3. phase-specific protection;
4. basic and enhanced tiers;
5. higher attachment points or deductibles where appropriate;
6. targeted subsidy.

Distribution efficiency cannot make an inherently high-risk peril affordable by itself.

# 5. Intermediary-specific options

## 5.1 Cooperatives and producer groups

### Best use

Organized farming areas with credible membership, procurement, input, credit, or extension records.

### Appropriate roles

- Education and product demonstrations.
- Member, crop, and location verification.
- Assisted enrollment.
- Claims explanation and complaint escalation.
- Provision of production and crop-calendar data.

They should not ordinarily hold or redistribute claims funds.

### Advantages

- Local legitimacy.
- Lower education cost per farmer through group meetings.
- Better control of adverse selection.
- Agricultural and production information.
- Potential for seasonal or harvest-linked collection.

### Risks

- Governance failure or political capture.
- Farmer expectation that the cooperative guarantees claims.
- Premium diversion or unauthorized deductions.
- Crop, geography, and partner concentration.
- Weak liquidity or reconciliation.

### Required controls

- Governance, financial, and reputation due diligence.
- Audit and data-access rights.
- Individual policy records and direct claim payment.
- Commission tied to activated policies, persistency, comprehension, and service quality.
- Clawbacks for mis-selling, diversion, or poor reconciliation.
- Exposure limits by cooperative, crop, district, peril, and weather cell.

### Acquisition economics

Initial CAC is likely medium. Group enrollment may reduce marginal cost, but training, data cleaning, field support, audit, and partner-management costs must be included. Calculate break-even policy volume for each cooperative.

---

## 5.2 Mobile-network agents

### Best use

Basic-phone and mobile-money users in areas with reliable network coverage, agent density, cash-out capacity, and simple products.

### Appropriate roles

- Assisted digital enrollment.
- Premium collection.
- Policy confirmation.
- Renewal reminders.
- Claim-status checks.
- Cash-out and basic servicing.

Agents should not be expected to resolve complex basis-risk disputes without insurer support.

### Advantages

- Broad reach.
- Efficient small-value transactions at scale.
- USSD, SMS, and voice compatibility.
- Fast digital notification and settlement.

### Risks

- Poor product explanation.
- Agent liquidity shortages during mass claims.
- Network, wallet, or API failure.
- SIM and identity problems.
- MNO concentration.
- High lapse from frequent payment requirements.

### Required controls

- App-independent USSD, SMS, voice, and agent service.
- Explicit separation between premiums, loans, savings, and transaction fees.
- Commission tied to activated policies, persistency, comprehension, and conduct—not registrations.
- Multiple payment providers where feasible.
- Mass-payout liquidity plan.
- Exposure limits by MNO footprint, agent network, crop, peril, and geography.

### Acquisition economics

CAC may be low at scale but initial integration, training, marketing, failed transactions, customer support, fraud, and liquidity costs may be substantial. Compare seasonal payment with installments; frequent payments may improve affordability but increase lapse and fee leakage.

---

## 5.3 Religious and community groups

### Best use

Education, referral, inclusion, and first contact in markets where social institutions are more trusted than formal insurers.

### Appropriate roles

- Product education.
- Referral to a licensed seller.
- Meeting access.
- Local-language and culturally relevant communication.
- Feedback and complaint escalation.

They should not ordinarily adjudicate claims or hold claim funds. Direct selling is appropriate only where legally authorized and supervised.

### Advantages

- Strong community legitimacy.
- Potential reach to women, remote households, and low-literacy customers.
- Useful for explaining non-obvious index mechanics.

### Risks

- Licensing restrictions.
- Social pressure or implied obligation.
- Religious or political exclusion.
- Overpromising charitable compensation.
- Confidentiality concerns.
- Reputational contagion after a disputed claim.

### Required controls

- Distinguish religious institutions, savings groups, women’s groups, producer groups, and NGOs during due diligence.
- Use fixed education or referral fees, not claim-volume incentives.
- Prohibit coercion, discrimination, and religious framing that misrepresents insurance.
- Provide confidential complaints outside the group hierarchy.
- Use independent licensed fulfillment and direct payment.

### Acquisition economics

Initial CAC is likely medium and scaling may be slower than digital. Retention may be strong where trust is genuine, but reputational downside is material.

---

## 5.4 Fintech applications and platforms

### Best use

Digitally active farmers using smartphones, digital credit, input platforms, marketplaces, remittance services, or farm-management applications.

### Appropriate roles

- Contextual offers.
- Digital enrollment and payment.
- Renewal and servicing.
- Claim-status access.
- Consent-based data integration.

### Advantages

- Low marginal servicing cost at scale.
- Data integration potential.
- Convenient renewal and embedded offers.
- Personalized communication.

### Risks

- Weak first-sale trust among remote or low-literacy customers.
- Digital exclusion.
- Data misuse or inadequate consent.
- Algorithmic bias.
- Platform failure or lock-in.
- High initial acquisition and integration cost.

### Required controls

- Explicit consent for data use and recurring payment.
- Durable, portable policy records.
- SMS, voice, hotline, and agent alternatives.
- Audit rights over algorithms and customer communications.
- Data-use limits, cybersecurity standards, and exit provisions.
- Limits by platform, crop, geography, peril, data source, and customer cohort.

### Acquisition economics

Initial CAC may be high; marginal servicing cost may fall at scale. The strongest economics may come from renewal and embedded offers to existing active users rather than mass-market app acquisition.

---

## 5.5 Hybrid trusted-face/digital-rail model

This should be the default pilot architecture:

1. A trusted local organization explains the product.
2. Licensed staff or agents complete assisted enrollment.
3. MNOs, banks, or fintechs collect premiums and provide servicing.
4. The insurer validates triggers, manages claims, and handles complaints.
5. Claims are paid directly to customers.
6. Reinsurance or public facilities cover defined catastrophe layers.

Use this model when trust, data quality, or basis-risk acceptance is uncertain. Do not use it automatically where partner governance is weak or field costs cannot be justified by persistency and customer value.

# 6. Cross-regional comparison

| Dimension | East Africa | South Asia | Central America |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary trust challenge | Converting mobile reach into insurance trust | Overcoming institutional complexity and prior claim skepticism | Reaching fragmented rural households while managing catastrophe concentration |
| Likely credible acquisition face | Cooperatives, farmer groups, savings groups, local aggregators | MFIs, banks, cooperatives, farmer groups; community organizations for education | Producer associations, cooperatives, rural banks, MFIs |
| Best operational rail | Mobile money, USSD, MNO agents | Banks, MFIs, mobile and assisted digital | Banks, mobile payments, rural agents |
| Likely payment preference to test | Seasonal, harvest-linked, or mobile installments | Seasonal or loan-linked with explicit opt-in | Harvest deduction, rural finance, or bank/mobile payment |
| Main claims concern | Fast mobile payout and understandable trigger | Transparent assessment, grievance, and exception review | Reliable payment after drought, storm, or flood |
| Main data concern | Sparse or unrepresentative stations | Quality of area-yield and local loss data | Localized topography, flood, and hurricane exposure |
| Main portfolio risk | Cooperative, MNO, crop, and district concentration | District, crop, lender, and public-program correlation | Hurricane, drought, basin, and value-chain aggregation |
| Preferred starting model | Cooperative-plus-mobile | Group and embedded finance | Value-chain plus rural finance |

These comparisons are directional. Country-level evidence must override regional assumptions.

# 7. Recommended business-model pathways by region

## 7.1 East Africa: cooperative-plus-mobile

### Recommended design

- Cooperative or farmer group: education, enrollment, crop information, and claims explanation.
- MNO agent or field officer: assisted digital registration and payment support.
- Mobile money or bank: direct settlement.
- Insurer: clearly identified claims provider.
- Reinsurer: quota share during early scale plus catastrophe protection.

### Product

Start with drought or rainfall-deficit cover where data are credible. Use hybrid review in sparse-station or heterogeneous areas. Add excess-rain or flood cover only after localized validation.

### Key risks

- Variable cooperative governance.
- MNO or agent liquidity shortage during claims.
- Confusion with savings, loans, or relief.
- Sparse or unrepresentative data.
- SIM, beneficiary, and payment-account changes.

### Controls

- Partner due diligence and audit.
- Direct individual payment.
- Multiple payment routes where feasible.
- Voice, SMS, paper, and agent support.
- Limits by cooperative, crop, district, peril, and weather cell.
- Mass-payout liquidity plan.
- Post-event basis-risk surveys.

### Contingency

If cooperatives underperform, shift education to savings groups, extension networks, input dealers, or community organizations while retaining mobile rails where reliable. If mobile service is weak, add bank or supervised agent payment and reduce payment frequency.

---

## 7.2 South Asia: group and embedded finance

### Recommended design

- MFI, bank, cooperative, or farmer organization offers the product.
- Customers actively opt in.
- Premium appears separately from loan principal, interest, input cost, and other fees.
- Insurer provides claims, appeals, and grievance handling.
- Faith and community groups primarily educate and refer unless licensed to sell.

### Product

Use area-yield or hybrid cover where crop-cutting, yield, or remote-sensing data are credible. Use rainfall products where weather data are stronger. Keep insurance benefits separate from debt relief.

### Key risks

- Perceived compulsory enrollment.
- Historical skepticism about crop-insurance assessment.
- Regulatory and public-program complexity.
- District and crop correlation.
- Substitution by disaster relief.

### Controls

- Explicit consent and separate receipts.
- Independent trigger governance.
- Local-language explanations and appeals.
- Complaints outside the lender.
- Limits by district, crop, lender, and season.
- Aggregate stop-loss or catastrophe protection.
- Staff monitoring for coercive sales and claim diversion.

### Contingency

If public-program alignment is slow, pilot through licensed MFIs or farmer organizations in a narrow geography. If yield data are weak, use lower-limit rainfall cover or hybrid protection. If complaints indicate coercion, replace default enrollment with active group or agent-assisted opt-in.

---

## 7.3 Central America: value-chain and rural finance

### Recommended design

- Producer or value-chain association: education and agricultural context.
- Rural bank or MFI: licensed sale and collection.
- Mobile or bank rails: payment and servicing.
- Insurer: trigger, claims, complaints, and portfolio management.
- Reinsurer: catastrophe protection before material exposure accumulates.

### Product

Design around specific value chains and perils. Use satellite or weather triggers where validated and hybrid review or local sampling for highly localized flood or topographic exposure.

### Key risks

- Hurricane and drought aggregation.
- Fragmented farms.
- Migration and changing payment accounts.
- Small-partner governance.
- Disaster-relief expectations.

### Controls

- Catastrophe excess-of-loss from inception.
- Limits by value chain, basin, cooperative, lender, peril, and weather cell.
- Spanish and relevant Indigenous-language communication.
- Regular account and beneficiary updates.
- Alternative payment routes after storms.
- Independent weather and satellite validation.

### Contingency

If producer organizations lack data capacity, use rural banks or MFIs for records and retain producer groups as education partners. If connectivity is weak, use field, radio, voice, and bank channels. If catastrophe concentration is too high, begin with smaller zones or less-correlated crops and perils.

# 8. Acquisition cost and affordability mechanisms

## 8.1 Full acquisition cost

\[
CAC =
\frac{
\text{commissions}
+\text{marketing}
+\text{field enrollment}
+\text{KYC}
+\text{technology}
+\text{training}
+\text{first-year servicing}
}{
\text{activated paid policies}
}
\]

Do not count registrations that never pay, immediately lapse, or cannot be contacted.

Calculate separately:

- first-year CAC;
- renewal CAC;
- assisted versus self-service CAC;
- subsidized versus unsubsidized CAC;
- break-even policy volume;
- minimum viable volume for each partner.

## 8.2 Contribution margin

\[
\text{Contribution margin}
=
\text{earned premium}
-
\text{expected claims}
-
\text{reinsurance}
-
\text{commissions}
-
\text{payment fees}
-
\text{variable servicing}
-
\text{fraud and leakage}
-
\text{subsidy administration}
\]

The model must distinguish:

- written versus earned premium;
- gross versus ceded loss;
- fixed versus variable costs;
- ordinary claims versus basis-risk remediation;
- subsidy receivables and timing risk;
- capital and liquidity costs;
- first-year versus renewal economics.

Stress the model for:

- lapse;
- claim frequency and severity;
- CAC;
- payment fees;
- severe seasons;
- reinsurance price and recovery delays;
- subsidy withdrawal or late payment;
- data-provider failure;
- customer remediation.

### Scale economics rule

No product-channel combination should scale unless it shows a credible path to positive contribution margin under base and stress scenarios without relying on uncommitted subsidies or optimistic reinsurance recoveries.

## 8.3 Affordability mechanisms

If full technical coverage is unaffordable, test:

1. lower limits;
2. catastrophe-only coverage;
3. phase-specific protection;
4. basic and enhanced tiers;
5. seasonal or harvest-aligned payment;
6. higher attachment points or deductibles where suitable;
7. targeted or declining subsidies;
8. resilience bundles with separately disclosed services.

Measure:

- premium as a share of seasonal farm income;
- affordability-related lapse;
- uptake by income and farm-size band;
- premium financed through borrowing;
- subsidy incidence and leakage;
- renewal after subsidy reduction;
- customer understanding of subsidized and unsubsidized prices.

## 8.4 Partner incentives

Compensation should reward:

- activated paid policies;
- persistency and renewal;
- customer comprehension;
- accurate identity and location data;
- timely claims communication;
- complaint quality and resolution.

Do not reward claim volume or claim approval. Prohibit undisclosed compulsory insurance, automatic claim diversion to debt repayment without lawful consent, retaliation against complainants, and sales targets that override comprehension.

# 9. Claim credibility and basis-risk mechanisms

## 9.1 Claims workflow

1. Independent data provider produces the index.
2. The insurer validates data quality and trigger calculation.
3. Policy, location, beneficiary, and exposure records are checked.
4. Standard claims are paid automatically.
5. Defined exceptions are reviewed under documented rules.
6. Customers receive trigger and payment-status notifications.
7. Failed payments are reattempted and escalated.
8. Post-event surveys measure actual loss, fairness, and comprehension.

## 9.2 Claims timestamps

Report:

1. Event or index observation.
2. Data confirmation and trigger determination.
3. Claim authorization and payment initiation.
4. Funds available to the customer.

Report median, 90th, and 95th percentile for each stage. Successful funds availability—not merely payment initiation—should be the principal customer outcome.

Planning ranges, subject to local validation:

| Product | Planning settlement range |
|---|---|
| Pure parametric | 3–10 business days after confirmed trigger |
| Parametric with exception review | 10–20 business days |
| Area-yield/local verification | 30–60 days, depending on data availability |

Each product must define:

- clock start and stop;
- responsible party at each stage;
- treatment of data-provider, network, payment-provider, and account failures;
- customer notification requirements;
- escalation procedures;
- remedies for insurer-controlled delays.

## 9.3 Basis-risk categories

- **Spatial basis risk:** index location or grid does not represent the farm.
- **Functional basis risk:** measured peril does not explain the loss.
- **Process error:** incorrect location, data, policy, beneficiary, or payment record.

## 9.4 Basis-risk metrics

Track separately:

1. Reported loss without payout.
2. Verified loss without payout.
3. Verified basis-risk rate above a predefined material-loss threshold.
4. Payout without material loss.
5. Process-error rate.

Before launch, pre-register:

- material-loss severity threshold;
- maximum reported and verified loss-without-payout rates;
- maximum payout-without-material-loss rate;
- maximum process-error rate;
- sampling and verification method;
- confidence intervals;
- remediation requirements;
- pause, redesign, and exit triggers.

These tolerances should differ by peril, crop, geography, and coverage tier.

## 9.5 Appeals and remediation

Customers may appeal:

- identity or location errors;
- data or calculation errors;
- payment failures;
- policy-record errors;
- defined exception conditions.

Appeals should specify eligible evidence, response times, independent escalation, and remedies. True index non-triggers should be explained; confirmed process or data errors should be corrected through payment correction, fee reversal, service credit, or expedited review as appropriate.

An appeal process must not silently convert parametric insurance into discretionary indemnity insurance.

# 10. Data, actuarial credibility, and regulatory considerations

## 10.1 Data and actuarial requirements

Each product approval must document:

- historical data length, missingness, and quality;
- station uptime and satellite continuity;
- spatial and temporal resolution;
- relationship between index and observed agricultural losses;
- treatment of outliers and structural breaks;
- weighting of historical observations, remote sensing, agronomic models, catastrophe models, and expert judgment;
- parameter, model, climate, and basis-risk uncertainty;
- confidence intervals or scenario ranges;
- uncertainty margins;
- pricing methodology;
- pricing refresh frequency;
- repricing, redesign, and suspension criteria.

Historical back-testing must be supplemented by climate-adjusted scenarios covering:

- changing rainfall and temperature patterns;
- shifting planting dates;
- crop and irrigation changes;
- farmer adaptation;
- heat and flood trends;
- satellite and data-provider methodology changes.

If a credible loss distribution cannot be estimated, narrow the geography, lower limits, use hybrid review, secure external capital, or defer launch. More precise pricing does not resolve inadequate data.

## 10.2 Regulatory sequence

1. **Pre-design feasibility:** assess product, intermediary, digital, payment, data, group-policy, and reinsurance rules.
2. **Formal filing:** obtain approval before solicitation, enrollment, or premium collection.
3. **Operational clearance:** confirm agent scripts, disclosures, complaint procedures, data processing, and payment arrangements.
4. **Post-launch monitoring:** report claims, complaints, cancellations, basis-risk issues, and material product changes as required.

Confirm:

- whether parametric insurance is recognized;
- who may sell, refer, collect, or administer policies;
- rules for digital consent and electronic documents;
- embedded and credit-linked insurance requirements;
- recurring-payment and cancellation rules;
- data localization, portability, deletion, and cross-border transfer;
- use of third-party data in trigger determination;
- claims and appeals requirements;
- reinsurance and foreign-counterparty rules;
- community and religious-group permissions;
- accessibility and vulnerable-customer standards.

# 11. Portfolio-risk controls

## 11.1 Risk-transfer layers

| Risk layer | Preferred treatment |
|---|---|
| Frequent ordinary losses | Retention or quota share |
| Medium aggregate losses | Quota share, stop-loss, or aggregate cover |
| Regional catastrophe | Catastrophe excess-of-loss or public facility |
| Early model uncertainty | Conservative limits, blended capital, external validation |
| Recovery timing risk | Liquidity facility or retained claims reserve |

## 11.2 Concentration limits

Set limits by:

- country, district, watershed, and weather cell;
- crop, value chain, peril, and season;
- cooperative, MFI, MNO, fintech, and data provider;
- payment provider and reinsurer;
- subsidy cohort.

Group enrollment and embedded distribution should be recognized as concentration drivers, not automatically as diversification.

## 11.3 Reinsurance controls

Assess:

- counterparty quality and collateral;
- exclusions and reinstatement premiums;
- aggregate limits;
- attachment and exhaustion;
- geographic and peril definitions;
- policy-to-reinsurance trigger mismatch;
- recovery timing;
- cross-border legal and payment restrictions;
- liquidity needs before recovery.

## 11.4 Capital and stress testing

Each pilot approval should specify:

- maximum net retained loss;
- modeled solvency impact;
- minimum liquidity before reinsurance recovery;
- catastrophe stress metrics;
- maximum exposure by partner, peril, geography, crop, and data source;
- board-approved risk appetite.

Stress-test:

1. Multi-country drought.
2. Major cyclone or hurricane.
3. Localized flood with high basis risk.
4. Excess rain during a network outage.
5. Data-provider failure during a trigger window.
6. Cooperative or agent premium diversion.
7. Mass payout and agent cash shortage.
8. Reinsurer delay or non-recovery.
9. Subsidy withdrawal or delayed reimbursement.
10. Higher reinsurance prices or reduced capacity.

# 12. Pilot sequencing and implementation

## Phase 0: Country and product selection

Use an indicative scorecard:

| Criterion | Indicative weight |
|---|---:|
| Regulatory feasibility | 20–25% |
| Data and index credibility | 20–25% |
| Trusted intermediary quality | 15–20% |
| Payment and claims infrastructure | 10–15% |
| Customer need and affordability | 10–15% |
| Portfolio diversification value | 10–15% |

Hard gates override the score.

## Phase 1: Validation

Complete:

- country-level trust mapping;
- customer willingness-to-pay and payment-timing research;
- three-scenario comprehension testing;
- partner and vendor due diligence;
- data and model audit;
- historical and climate-adjusted back-testing;
- basis-risk simulation;
- formal regulatory filing and clearance;
- reinsurance and liquidity arrangements;
- customer-remediation design;
- partner, vendor, and data-provider exit plans;
- pre-registration of KPIs and thresholds;
- sample-size and exposure plan.

## Phase 2: Controlled live pilot

Where feasible, compare:

- trust-led versus digital or embedded acquisition;
- assisted versus self-service enrollment;
- seasonal versus installment payment;
- subsidized versus unsubsidized cohorts;
- pure parametric versus hybrid cover.

Use randomization where ethical and operationally feasible. Otherwise use matched cohorts, phased rollout, or difference-in-differences designs controlling for geography, crop, season, customer type, and partner.

Separate evidence objectives:

| Evidence type | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| Commercial proof | Conversion, CAC, renewal, contribution margin |
| Operational proof | Payment, claims, complaints, partner performance |
| Customer-value proof | Comprehension, trust, basis-risk acceptance |
| Actuarial proof | Loss distribution and capital adequacy across seasons and modeled events |

Set sample sizes separately for behavioral and operational outcomes, and set policy exposure requirements separately for technical learning. Do not require a major catastrophe to validate the product; use historical and modeled events for tail-risk assessment.

## Phase 3: Selective scale-up

Add geography, crops, partners, automation, coverage limits, and subsidy reduction incrementally. Do not change all dimensions simultaneously.

## Phase 4: Regional scale

At scale:

- diversify partners and payment providers;
- recalibrate pricing using credible experience;
- retain offline support;
- publish claims and complaint outcomes;
- review climate, basis-risk, capital, reinsurance, and concentration assumptions each season.

# 13. KPIs, ownership, and scale gates

## KPI ownership

| Area | Accountable owner |
|---|---|
| CAC, conversion, renewal | Commercial/product |
| Comprehension and trust | Product/customer protection |
| Claims timing and complaints | Claims/operations |
| Basis risk and trigger integrity | Actuarial/data governance |
| Fraud and partner conduct | Compliance/risk |
| Capital, liquidity, reinsurance | Enterprise risk |
| Inclusion and accessibility | Customer protection |

## Core KPIs

### Commercial

- CAC per activated and renewed policy.
- Conversion from lead to enrollment and payment.
- Lapse and renewal by channel, subsidy status, and payment method.
- Contribution margin after claims, reinsurance, fees, servicing, fraud, and subsidy administration.
- Minimum viable policy volume and break-even volume.

### Customer value

- Comprehension of payout and non-payout scenarios.
- Correct identification of insurer and claims route.
- Trust in intermediary, insurer, and index separately.
- Claim timing at each stage.
- Payment failure and reattempt rate.
- Complaint rate and resolution time.
- Reported and verified loss without payout.
- Payout without material loss.
- Renewal after payout versus after non-trigger loss.
- Inclusion by gender, farm size, remoteness, phone type, and assisted-service use.

### Technical and operational

- Actual versus expected loss ratio.
- Trigger frequency versus modeled frequency.
- Basis-risk measures by crop, peril, and geography.
- Data outages, revisions, and trigger corrections.
- Fraud and duplicate-enrollment rate.
- Premium-reconciliation exceptions.
- Agent liquidity during claims.
- Reinsurance recovery time.
- Exposure against concentration limits.
- Stress loss and liquidity requirement.

## Pre-agreed scale gates

Thresholds must be approved before enrollment by product, actuarial, risk, compliance, operations, and customer-protection owners. They should be product-, country-, peril-, crop-, and segment-specific and should change only through formal governance.

| Dimension | Expand | Remediate/retest | Pause or exit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comprehension | Customers generally explain trigger and non-trigger scenarios | Confusion concentrated in a segment | Customers believe all crop loss is covered |
| Claims | Most claims meet service standard; tail delays controlled | Repeated delays or payment failures | Widespread delay, opacity, or missing payments |
| Basis risk | Within approved tolerance | Concentrated or uncertain mismatch | Persistent severe mismatch |
| Renewal | Demand persists beyond subsidy or compulsion | Strong only in selected cohorts | Collapses after subsidy or non-trigger season |
| CAC | Within approved full-cost range | High but improving | Unsustainable at credible volume |
| Contribution margin | Positive or credible path under base and stress cases | Dependent on uncertain assumptions | Negative without committed support |
| Partner conduct | Reliable disclosure, records, and reconciliation | Corrective action required | Mis-selling, diversion, or governance failure |
| Data | Independent, stable, auditable | Manageable with hybrid controls | Cannot support product promise |
| Capital/liquidity | Within risk appetite | Buffer remediation required | Stress losses or liquidity exceed appetite |

When metrics conflict, apply this order:

1. Legal, regulatory, and customer protection.
2. Customer comprehension and claim credibility.
3. Solvency, liquidity, and catastrophe exposure.
4. Sustainable unit economics.
5. Growth and enrollment.

# 14. Major risks and contingencies

| Risk | Response |
|---|---|
| Weak data or high basis risk | Narrow geography, lower limits, blend data, add defined review, or defer launch |
| Cooperative or community partner failure | Freeze enrollment, audit, pay customers directly, replace or downgrade partner |
| MNO or agent failure | Add payment providers, reduce payment frequency, introduce field/bank support |
| Fintech economics or access failure | Restrict to active users, add assisted enrollment, use platform for renewal |
| Claims credibility deterioration | Pause scale, publish trigger evidence, conduct independent sampling, redesign product |
| Subsidy withdrawal | Reduce scope or limits, target support, test basic catastrophe cover |
| Data-provider failure | Use alternate data source, preserve version history, apply documented contingency rules |
| Reinsurer delay or non-recovery | Maintain liquidity facility, retained reserve, and counterparty limits |
| Mass payout and network outage | Pre-position liquidity, use multiple payment channels, provide voice and field support |
| Embedded-sales coercion | Require explicit opt-in, separate disclosure, independent complaints, and staff monitoring |
| Partner or vendor exit | Activate customer notification, data transfer, claims continuity, payment rerouting, and policy portability plan |

# 15. Key assumptions and definitions

The strategy should maintain a controlled glossary:

- **Activated paid policy:** a policy for which premium has been successfully received and coverage is in force.
- **Automated trigger:** automated calculation of whether an index crossed the specified threshold.
- **Automated settlement:** automated approval and payment processing after trigger validation; it does not guarantee funds are immediately available.
- **Confirmed trigger:** the recorded point at which data quality and trigger calculation have been validated.
- **Material loss:** a product-specific loss severity threshold approved before launch.
- **Verified basis risk:** independently sampled material loss without the corresponding payout.
- **Affordability:** assessed through premium relative to seasonal income, payment behavior, borrowing, lapse, willingness to pay, and subsidy dependence—not premium size alone.
- **Credible data:** data with sufficient completeness, spatial and temporal resolution, independence, stability, auditability, and demonstrated relationship to agricultural outcomes for the relevant product tier.
- **Subsidy dependence:** the degree to which uptake, renewal, or positive contribution margin disappears when subsidy support is reduced or removed.
- **Available-to-customer payment:** the point at which funds can actually be accessed by the insured, not merely when payment is initiated.

## Final recommendation

Approve a **limited, multi-channel pilot under the hybrid trusted-face/digital-rail architecture**, subject to:

- country-level hard gates;
- independent data validation;
- formal regulatory clearance before solicitation;
- pre-agreed financial, customer, basis-risk, capital, and liquidity thresholds;
- reinsurance and payment capacity before enrollment;
- defined partner, vendor, customer-remediation, and exit plans.

The insurer should begin with:

- **East Africa:** cooperative or farmer-group trust layer, MNO-assisted enrollment and mobile-money settlement.
- **South Asia:** MFI, bank, cooperative, or farmer-group distribution with explicit opt-in, separate premium disclosure, strong grievance handling, and area-based or hybrid products where justified.
- **Central America:** producer and value-chain distribution supported by rural finance, crop- and peril-specific products, direct payment, and catastrophe protection from inception.

The pilot’s success condition is not maximum reach. It is demonstrable customer understanding, credible and timely claims, sustainable renewal, viable full-cost economics, effective consumer protection, and a portfolio that remains within capital, liquidity, and concentration limits during correlated climate events.
