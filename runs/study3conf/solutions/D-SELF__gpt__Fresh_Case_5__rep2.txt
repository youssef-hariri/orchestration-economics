# 2026 Market-Entry and Product-Adaptation Strategy  
## Gulf States, Malaysia and Indonesia

## 1. Executive recommendation

Enter these markets as a **partner-led, locally governed financial-wellness platform**, not as a single autonomous AI-investment app.

The initial proposition should help customers:

1. understand cash flow;
2. build liquid emergency savings;
3. set personal and household goals;
4. receive explainable, user-controlled recommendations;
5. access only locally authorized conventional or Shariah-approved products.

The European startup should initially own the technology, user experience, education and financial-wellness functionality. Licensed local entities should perform regulated activities—including investment advice, portfolio management, execution, custody, payments, e-money and regulated distribution—where required.

“AI,” “technology provider” and contractual disclaimers will not by themselves avoid regulation if the startup gives personalized recommendations, routes orders, manages portfolios, holds funds or solicits customers.

### Board recommendation

Approve:

- **Malaysia** as the first full pilot;
- **Indonesia** as a separate low-ticket, flexible-saving model;
- **UAE or Bahrain**, but not both unless readiness is exceptional, as the only possible Gulf pilot in 2026;
- **Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Oman and Kuwait** as later markets;
- savings and financial wellness before broad investment functionality;
- optional, product-level Shariah pathways;
- explainable, consent-based and human-supervised AI;
- licensed-partner-first entry;
- country-specific regulatory, Shariah, data, operating and economic gates.

Because the current date is **30 August 2026**, the original full-year roadmap is no longer applicable. Remaining 2026 activity should focus on readiness, gap closure and only tightly controlled pilots. Broader expansion should normally be planned for 2027.

This is strategic guidance, not legal, tax or religious advice.

# 2. Confirmed facts, assumptions and dependencies

## Confirmed strategic facts

- Financial-services permissions are country-specific; licenses should not be assumed to passport between markets.
- Payments, e-money, investment advice, portfolio management, execution, custody and fund distribution may be separately regulated.
- Shariah status is product- and structure-specific, not a generic property of an app or brand.
- Digital-payment adoption does not automatically establish trust in automated investment advice.
- Family participation may be commercially important but must not override individual ownership, privacy or consent.
- Customer assets should be held and executed through appropriately licensed institutions.
- Savings discipline, liquidity and household goals are the appropriate initial customer problem—not promised investment outperformance.

## Working assumptions

- The startup has no local financial-services license, balance sheet or established regional distribution network.
- It will use licensed custodians, banks, brokers, investment managers and payment providers.
- Initial functionality will comprise financial wellness, goal saving and limited recommendations, not unrestricted discretionary management.
- Initial investments will be simple, liquid, diversified and locally authorized.
- Shariah products will be optional; users will not be required to disclose religious identity.
- Controlled pilots will precede any independent licensing or mass-market expansion.

## Must be validated before customer onboarding

Obtain written local-counsel and partner confirmation covering:

- regulatory classification of every feature;
- contracting entity and customer terms;
- advice, portfolio-management, execution and custody responsibilities;
- payment, e-money, aggregation and stored-value permissions;
- partner license scope;
- product approval and distribution permissions;
- financial-promotion and influencer rules;
- Shariah approval scope and permitted terminology;
- data-controller, processor, hosting and cross-border-transfer responsibilities;
- family, delegated-authority and minor-account rules;
- tax, foreign-exchange, remittance, residency-change and inheritance treatment;
- complaints, redress, incident and wind-down responsibilities.

A partner must be licensed for the **specific activity and customer type**, not merely licensed as a bank, payment institution or asset manager.

# 5. Product strategy

## Modular architecture

1. **Financial wellness**
   - budgeting;
   - transaction categorization;
   - cash-flow analysis;
   - education;
   - goal planning.

2. **Savings**
   - scheduled contributions;
   - emergency-fund goals;
   - flexible and irregular contributions;
   - pause/resume;
   - goal spaces.

3. **Advice**
   - suitability;
   - affordability;
   - liquidity;
   - loss-capacity assessment;
   - risk profiling;
   - approved-product recommendations.

4. **Investment**
   - locally authorized funds and instruments;
   - narrow, liquid and diversified product shelves;
   - separate conventional and Shariah-approved pathways.

5. **Family**
   - personal and shared goals;
   - contribution-only links;
   - granular view, suggestion and approval permissions.

6. **Governance**
   - consent and recommendation logs;
   - product eligibility rules;
   - Shariah classifications;
   - human-review queues;
   - model monitoring;
   - audit and incident controls.

## Liquidity-first sequence

1. Cash-flow visibility.
2. Emergency reserve.
3. Short-term goal saving.
4. Medium-term investment.
5. Long-term investment.

Do not recommend market investments where essential expenses, affordability, liquidity or loss capacity have not been assessed.

## Defer from the first release

- automatic rebalancing;
- broad product marketplaces;
- complex structured products;
- gold and fractional assets;
- children’s accounts;
- zakat calculation or payments;
- full joint-account functionality;
- aggressive gamification;
- leveraged or speculative products.

# 6. Cash, savings and investment classification

The interface must distinguish clearly between:

- cash;
- deposits;
- safeguarded payment funds;
- wallets;
- investment accounts;
- money-market funds;
- Islamic investment accounts.

For every balance type, disclose:

- legal provider;
- legal and economic structure;
- who owns or safeguards the money;
- whether principal is guaranteed and by whom;
- whether returns are contractual, discretionary, profit-sharing or absent;
- withdrawal and settlement timing;
- fees;
- credit and market risk;
- applicable deposit or investor protection;
- treatment after residency change or closure.

“Cash-only” is not automatically Shariah-compliant, and “liquid” does not mean risk-free.

Do not sweep idle funds into an interest-bearing arrangement for customers selecting a Shariah pathway unless the arrangement has been reviewed by local counsel, the licensed partner and qualified Shariah advisers.

Emergency funds should generally remain in highly liquid, low-volatility arrangements. A Shariah-approved investment is not automatically suitable for emergency savings if its value can fall or settlement is delayed.

# 7. Islamic-finance framework

## Product pathways

Where legally permitted, offer:

1. **Cash or savings only**
2. **Shariah-approved investments**
3. **Conventional investments**

Users select product preferences; they do not need to disclose religious identity.

Do not market the entire app as “halal” or “Shariah-compliant.” Claims must apply to a specified product, structure, methodology, customer journey and approval scope.

## Required review

Each Islamic product must be assessed for:

- **riba**, including interest-bearing cash, financing, rewards and leverage;
- **gharar**, including unclear ownership, pricing, settlement or redemption;
- **maysir**, including gambling-like mechanics and speculative prompts;
- prohibited business activities;
- interest income and debt ratios;
- leverage;
- derivatives and short selling;
- securities lending;
- ownership and possession;
- liquidity and trading rules;
- currency exposure;
- fees, agency and revenue-sharing structures;
- purification;
- customer disclosures and marketing claims.

Avoid return leaderboards, high-frequency prompts, binary outcomes, investment competitions, random product selection and pressure-based contribution mechanics.

## Product files

Each Shariah product should document:

- legal and contractual structure;
- investment mandate;
- asset list;
- screening methodology and thresholds;
- review frequency;
- valuation, liquidity and redemption rules;
- fees, rebates and conflicts;
- purification methodology, if applicable;
- responsible adviser or board;
- approval and expiry dates;
- approved marketing wording;
- non-compliance and replacement procedures.

Potential categories such as screened equity funds, sukuk funds, Islamic money-market products, gold or fractional assets require individual review. “Sukuk-like,” “ethical” and “interest-free” are not sufficient descriptions.

## Shariah governance

### Central policy committee

Sets group standards for:

- prohibited activities;
- screening;
- purification;
- product development;
- marketing;
- conflicts;
- AI recommendations;
- methodology changes;
- incident escalation.

### Local or partner governance

Use the licensed partner’s Shariah board, qualified local advisers or another formally recognized arrangement. Review must cover the product, contracts, customer disclosures, recommendation logic, execution model and marketing.

### Independent assurance

At least annually:

- test portfolio holdings;
- review screening and purification;
- audit claims;
- test recommendations against approved products;
- review changes and exceptions;
- analyze complaints and mis-selling.

The governance charter should specify qualifications, independence, appointment and removal, remuneration, conflicts, quorum, records, approval expiry, disagreement escalation and annual attestation.

AAOIFI and IFSB materials may be useful references but do not replace local regulatory, partner or scholarly approval.

## Purification and zakat

Purification is not a universal remedy for an otherwise non-compliant investment. If relevant, document:

- who calculates it;
- methodology and period;
- investor allocation;
- approved charitable recipient;
- evidence of transfer;
- customer reporting;
- treatment when an asset fails the screen.

Zakat calculators and automated payments should remain out of scope unless separately validated by qualified scholars, tax advisers, payment providers and relevant charitable authorities.

# 8. AI and automated-finance controls

## Graduated automation

### Stage 1: Assisted financial wellness

AI may:

- categorize transactions;
- identify savings patterns;
- suggest goals;
- explain scenarios;
- provide reminders and education.

### Stage 2: Recommendations with confirmation

Before a personalized investment recommendation, complete:

- identity verification;
- suitability;
- affordability;
- emergency-fund and liquidity assessment;
- loss-capacity assessment;
- risk profiling;
- product eligibility;
- conflict review;
- disclosures;
- explicit customer confirmation.

### Stage 3: Limited pre-authorized automation

Only after regulatory and partner approval:

- recurring investments;
- contribution rules;
- limited rebalancing within user-defined boundaries.

All material automated actions must be visible, reversible and subject to user-set limits.

## Explanation requirements

Each recommendation must show:

- goal and time horizon;
- contribution amount;
- liquidity requirement;
- risk level;
- assumptions;
- potential downside;
- fees and FX costs;
- why the product was selected;
- alternatives;
- Shariah methodology and approval scope where relevant;
- how to reject, change or pause the recommendation.

Distinguish observed data, user assumptions, model projections and unknowns. Return “insufficient information” or escalate where data is unreliable.

## Human control

Provide:

- local-language human support;
- recommendation rejection and modification;
- contribution and investment pause;
- manual data correction;
- human review for high-risk or unusual cases;
- rules-based fallback;
- model kill switch;
- complaint and appeal channels.

Do not optimize for recommendation acceptance alone. High acceptance may reflect poor comprehension or coercive defaults.

## AI governance and data

Maintain:

- model inventory;
- version and change history;
- input/output logs;
- recommendation rationale;
- product-eligibility records;
- consent records;
- bias and language testing;
- rollback procedures;
- incident reporting;
- human-review records.

Define:

- data-controller and processor roles;
- retention and deletion periods;
- customer access and correction rights;
- model-training permissions;
- cross-border transfers;
- third-party AI restrictions;
- breach-notification responsibilities.

Do not use identifiable transaction data to train general-purpose models by default. Do not infer religious identity, gender roles, marital consent, family authority, vulnerability or financial sophistication from names, language, location, device type or family structure unless lawful, necessary and transparent.

# 9. Family permissions and vulnerable customers

## Family permission model

Separate permissions for:

- viewing;
- contributing;
- suggesting;
- approving;
- withdrawing;
- full account access.

Required controls:

- separate authentication;
- explicit consent;
- account-owner and beneficial-owner designation;
- permission-change notifications;
- immediate revocation;
- audit logs;
- step-up authentication for material withdrawals;
- dispute procedures;
- privacy-preserving personal goals;
- safeguards against coercive financial control.

Also define treatment of:

- gifts and third-party contributions;
- death and incapacity;
- inheritance and beneficiaries;
- minor accounts and guardianship;
- relationship breakdown;
- migration after partner failure;
- safe exit where coercion or abuse is suspected.

Do not require spousal or parental approval merely because of a family relationship.

## Vulnerable customers

Create a formal policy covering:

- low financial literacy;
- unstable income;
- debt distress;
- disability;
- older age;
- language barriers;
- digital exclusion;
- fraud and scams;
- bereavement;
- coercive household situations.

Provide low-literacy explanations, audio and screen-reader support, low-bandwidth access, low minimums, contribution pauses, human assistance, scam warnings and cooling-off measures where legally available.

Marketing must not exploit religion, shame, fear, gender stereotypes or family pressure.

# 10. Country playbooks

## Malaysia — first full pilot

**Priority segments**

- young salaried professionals;
- emerging affluent users;
- education and housing savers;
- users seeking Islamic products;
- employers;
- Islamic banks and institutions.

**Initial proposition**

- emergency-fund goals;
- scheduled savings;
- one or two named, approved investment products;
- Malay and English;
- detailed factsheets;
- employer and Islamic-finance distribution.

**Required validation**

Confirm the roles of the Securities Commission Malaysia, Bank Negara Malaysia, licensed partners and applicable Shariah structures. Confirm advice, digital investment management, payments, aggregation, custody, data and marketing requirements.

Do not launch until the initial product shelf and partner responsibility matrix are complete.

## Indonesia — distinct low-ticket model

**Priority segments**

- young mobile users;
- gig workers;
- micro-entrepreneurs;
- families;
- first-time investors;
- platform and community users.

**Initial proposition**

- Bahasa Indonesia-first onboarding;
- very low minimums;
- variable contributions;
- local bank and licensed wallet integrations;
- contribution links;
- narrow, liquid investment shelf;
- strong fraud, withdrawal and payment-failure controls.

**Required validation**

Confirm the OJK and Bank Indonesia perimeter, payment permissions, agent rules, DSN-MUI and partner Shariah arrangements, data and cloud requirements, tax and FX treatment, and influencer or religious-organization marketing.

Agents should initially be limited to referral and approved onboarding assistance. They must not independently recommend products, collect funds unless authorized, promise returns or make halal claims.

Set hard gates for payment cost, support cost, fraud losses, partner revenue share, 90-day retention and contribution margin.

## UAE — possible single Gulf pilot

Focus on:

- expatriates;
- employers;
- affluent professionals;
- selected younger nationals.

Required features:

- Arabic and English;
- multi-currency goal display;
- remittance-linked saving;
- clear residency-change and closure procedures;
- conservative approved products.

Select one route—onshore, DIFC or ADGM—and map the relevant SCA, Central Bank, DFSA or FSRA requirements. These regimes are not interchangeable.

## Bahrain — partnership and capability base

Use Bahrain for Islamic-finance relationships, controlled testing, institutional partnerships and fintech capability-building.

Validate Central Bank of Bahrain requirements, sandbox conditions, data and outsourcing rules, Shariah governance and implications of serving customers outside Bahrain.

Bahrain does not provide a regional passport.

## Saudi Arabia — later expansion

Require:

- Arabic-first product and support;
- major local partner;
- local complaints capability;
- confirmed CMA and Saudi Central Bank perimeter;
- data and cybersecurity compliance;
- approved products and marketing;
- voluntary family collaboration without forced family control.

## Qatar, Oman and Kuwait

Enter one at a time only where there is:

- an appropriately licensed partner;
- a defined segment;
- an approved product shelf;
- Arabic and English support;
- viable economics;
- tested custody, withdrawals and complaints;
- a documented regulatory route.

Relevant starting points include Qatar Central Bank, QFMA and QFCRA; Central Bank of Oman and the Financial Services Authority; and Central Bank of Kuwait and the Capital Markets Authority, depending on the model.

# 12. Consumer protection, fees and resilience

## Fees and disclosures

Before commitment, display total cost in local currency, including:

- platform fee;
- fund-management fee;
- custody;
- brokerage or execution;
- payment charges;
- FX spread;
- redemption or transfer charges;
- fixed minimum charges;
- rebates and commissions.

Avoid performance fees at launch, hidden spreads, punitive inactivity fees and fixed charges that make micro-contributions uneconomic.

Provide a standardized pre-investment decision record containing:

- product;
- amount;
- term;
- liquidity;
- downside;
- fees;
- provider and custodian;
- Shariah status and approval scope where relevant;
- conflicts or remuneration;
- customer confirmation;
- cancellation or cooling-off rights where available.

## Complaints and redress

Each market requires local-language complaints handling with:

- a named responsible entity;
- free intake channels;
- acknowledgement and resolution deadlines;
- escalation to the partner and regulator where applicable;
- correction and redress;
- categorization of AI, fee, suitability, withdrawal, Shariah, privacy and fraud complaints.

Also define treatment of failed payments, negative balances, dormant accounts, unclaimed assets, death, incapacity, partner failure and account closure.

## Cybersecurity and resilience

Minimum controls include:

- strong authentication;
- device and transaction monitoring;
- step-up verification for new withdrawal destinations;
- encryption;
- privileged-access controls;
- secure software development;
- penetration testing;
- vendor-security reviews;
- immutable audit logs;
- daily reconciliation;
- disaster recovery;
- incident notification;
- model rollback and emergency shutdown.

Maintain tested plans for partner, custodian, payment, cloud or AI-vendor failure; European parent failure; customer migration; data breach; residency change; and wind-down.

Obtain appropriate professional-indemnity, cyber and product-liability insurance after local review.

# 14. KPIs and scale gates

Set numerical thresholds before each pilot for:

### Trust and compliance

- identity, suitability and consent completion;
- customer understanding of fees, risk and custody;
- complaints per 1,000 funded users;
- resolution time;
- failed withdrawals;
- reconciliation exceptions;
- critical compliance findings;
- AI and Shariah issues closed on schedule.

### Product

- registration-to-funded-account conversion;
- first contribution within seven days;
- 30-, 90- and 180-day contribution retention;
- contribution frequency;
- emergency-fund completion;
- goal completion;
- family-feature adoption;
- recommendation rejection and modification;
- human-escalation satisfaction.

### Commercial

- acquisition cost by channel;
- average contribution and balance;
- assets administered or invested;
- revenue per funded user;
- payment, custody and support cost;
- partner revenue share;
- cost to serve;
- contribution margin;
- payback period;
- churn and dormancy.

### Responsible AI and inclusion

- recommendation comprehension;
- unsuitable-recommendation rate;
- human-review rate;
- model incident rate;
- language-related performance differences;
- cost, liquidity and product disparities;
- adoption among women and underserved users;
- users contributing below €25 equivalent monthly;
- accessibility completion.

Define before launch:

- funded user;
- repeat contribution;
- material complaint;
- unsuitable recommendation;
- critical incident;
- statistically meaningful disparity;
- gate owner;
- escalation deadline;
- authority able to pause onboarding.

Scale only if:

- no critical regulatory issue remains;
- custody and withdrawals function reliably;
- fraud and payment performance are acceptable;
- customers understand fees, risk and custody;
- repeat contributions persist for three to six months;
- Shariah governance and claims are validated;
- at least two distribution channels work;
- economics show a credible path to sustainability;
- no material unexplained disparity exists;
- partner-failure and wind-down procedures have been tested.

Downloads and recommendation acceptance are not primary scale criteria.

## Final recommendation

Proceed with a **Malaysia-first, Indonesia-distinct, Gulf-selective** strategy:

- savings and financial wellness before broad investment functionality;
- licensed local partners for regulated activities;
- product-level Islamic-finance governance rather than generic religious branding;
- qualified local scholars or boards and transparent approval scope;
- voluntary family collaboration without forced joint control;
- gradual, explainable and human-supervised automation;
- local language, payments, support and disclosures;
- at most one Gulf pilot in 2026;
- expansion based on suitability, comprehension, withdrawal reliability, retention, trust and economics.

The defensible proposition is:

> **A locally governed savings platform that uses transparent automation to help households build liquidity and access approved investment products, with optional Shariah pathways, human oversight and full user control.**
