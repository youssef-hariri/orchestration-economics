# Gulf Grocery JV Expansion Strategy  
## Saudi Arabia, UAE and Qatar | Decision basis: September 2026

## 1. Executive recommendation

Adopt a **staged, country-specific joint-venture strategy**:

1. **UAE:** first operating pilot and potential regional capability base.
2. **Saudi Arabia:** parallel preparation followed by a one-city pilot; principal long-term scale market.
3. **Qatar:** focused option, initially limited to diligence and—subject to approval—one compact store plus digital ordering.

The company should **not yet approve** a full three-country rollout, fixed ownership percentages, large-format stores, a UAE regional hub, or broad Qatar expansion.

### Approve now

- Competitive partner selection in all three countries.
- Country-specific legal, regulatory, tax, workforce and competition diligence.
- Customer, competitor, pricing and site research.
- Pilot unit economics and downside scenarios.
- JV term-sheet negotiations.
- UAE and Saudi platform preparation within board-approved capital caps.

### Conditional authorization

| Country | Conditional authorization | Requires separate approval |
|---|---|---|
| UAE | Two to four pilot stores plus digital ordering in one selected emirate | Flagship, second emirate, regional hub |
| Saudi Arabia | One-city pilot after city, partner, licensing, funding and workforce approval | Second city, large format, national rollout |
| Qatar | One compact store plus digital ordering | Second store, flagship, broad rollout |

The strategic thesis is **UAE learning, Saudi scale and Qatar focus**. A 24–36-month country-platform break-even objective may be used for planning, but is not a commitment until store economics, labor, localization, supply-chain and delivery assumptions are validated.

---

## 2. Strategic architecture

Create:

- **Three country operating JVs**, each with its own legal entity, licenses, management team, P&L and compliance ownership.
- A **regional coordination function**, initially based in the UAE only if independently justified, covering:
  - food safety and quality;
  - sourcing standards and private label;
  - technology architecture;
  - data governance;
  - learning and talent development;
  - regional supplier development.

The UAE should not automatically become the importer, distributor, data-hosting location or services center for the other markets. Each function requires a separate business case covering licensing, customs, tax, transfer pricing, data, substance, service levels and contingency arrangements.

### Portfolio objective

The first 24 months should be treated as an **option-building and operating-model validation phase**. The program should maximize validated learning within an explicit capital-at-risk limit. Cluster expansion requires:

- credible four-wall profitability;
- repeat customer demand;
- reliable supply and availability;
- compliant workforce localization;
- viable omnichannel economics;
- demonstrable partner delivery.

---

## 3. Country roles and sequencing

## 3.1 UAE: learning market and potential capability base

### Role

Use the UAE to test:

- Arabic/English store and digital execution.
- Fresh, bakery, deli, prepared-food and food-to-go propositions.
- Three-tier private label.
- Known-value-item pricing.
- Loyalty and personalization.
- Store-based fulfillment and delivery.
- Gulf retail talent and operating systems.

The UAE is a strong candidate for initial learning, but this remains a commercial hypothesis to be tested against customer adoption, competition, rent and delivery economics.

### Entry plan

- Concentrate two to four pilots in one selected emirate and comparable catchments.
- Launch digital ordering in the same catchments.
- Consider a flagship only if independently justified.
- Consider a second emirate only after the first pilot produces reliable learning.

Select the first emirate using:

- legally usable site pipeline;
- occupancy cost;
- licensing complexity and timing;
- customer mix;
- competitor intensity;
- parking and loading;
- delivery economics;
- partner capability;
- expected learning value.

### Formats

- Express: approximately 300–800 square meters.
- Neighborhood supermarket: approximately 1,500–3,000 square meters.
- Optional flagship: approximately 4,000–7,000 square meters.

These are format hypotheses, not approved commitments.

### Proposition

- European-quality fresh food and private label.
- Strong UAE, Arab and regional ranges alongside international products.
- Bakery, deli, prepared meals and food-to-go.
- Competitive staples; not an exclusively premium position.
- Arabic and English signage, digital content and service.
- Additional language support based on catchment data.

---

## 3.2 Saudi Arabia: principal scale platform

### Role

Saudi Arabia should become the primary growth and profit platform, with:

- a dedicated Saudi managing director and P&L;
- local sourcing and distribution capability;
- city-based expansion;
- Arabic-first execution;
- a workforce model built around current localization requirements.

Riyadh, Jeddah and the Eastern Province should be evaluated as distinct markets.

### City-selection framework

Select the first city using a scored comparison of:

- legally usable site pipeline;
- rent, construction and fit-out costs;
- competitive price index;
- catchment density and shopping missions;
- parking and loading;
- delivery economics;
- labor availability and localization cost;
- fresh-supply access;
- partner execution capability;
- climate and Ramadan implications.

Riyadh may be the initial candidate, but should not be predetermined.

### Entry plan

- Begin with a one-city pilot.
- Develop a second-city plan in parallel, without funding it until first-city gates are passed.
- Establish suppliers, management and localization capability before opening.
- Use stores as both shopping destinations and fulfillment assets.

### Formats

- Compact supermarket: approximately 800–1,500 square meters.
- Neighborhood supermarket: approximately 2,500–5,000 square meters.
- Larger destination format only after demonstrated four-wall economics.

### Proposition

- Family baskets, larger packs and bulk missions.
- Competitive known-value items.
- Saudi and regional products integrated into core planograms.
- Fresh food, bakery, dairy, poultry, dates, beverages and household essentials.
- Arabic-first signage, app content and service.
- Scheduled delivery, repeat ordering, loyalty and click-and-collect where viable.
- Family-oriented layouts and culturally appropriate service, without assuming historic gender-segregation requirements.

---

## 3.3 Qatar: focused Doha option

### Role

Qatar should be treated as an **option-preservation investment** and controlled proof point. Digital, residential, hospitality and institutional partnerships can extend reach beyond the initial store network.

### Entry plan

Base case:

- one compact store;
- digital ordering and delivery from launch;
- Doha and adjacent catchments only;
- residential, hospitality and institutional partnerships;
- second store only after first-store productivity, supply reliability and delivery economics are demonstrated.

### Formats

- Convenience: approximately 300–800 square meters.
- Compact premium supermarket: approximately 1,000–2,500 square meters.

### Proposition

- Fresh quality and selected premium imports.
- Competitive staples and core private label.
- Meal solutions, bakery, dates, gifting and hospitality ranges.
- Local fresh and bakery partnerships.
- Strategic safety stock and diversified import routes.

Qatar should not be assumed to require local ownership or a universal private-sector Qatarization quota. Both issues require confirmation for the exact activity and entity.

# 4. Partner-selection strategy

Select the operating partner—not merely the family group—through a competitive process with at least three credible candidates per country.

## Core scorecard

| Criterion | Core weight |
|---|---:|
| Verified sites and lease execution | 20% |
| Funding capacity and willingness to fund losses | 15% |
| Retail operating record | 15% |
| Regulatory and stakeholder execution | 15% |
| Compliance, reputation and governance | 15% |
| Supply chain, cold chain and local sourcing | 10% |
| Localization and talent development | 5% |
| Technology, data and reporting compatibility | 5% |

Adjust by country:

- **Saudi Arabia:** increase operating, workforce and supply-chain capability.
- **UAE:** increase digital, retail-talent and emirate-level execution.
- **Qatar:** increase site control, import capability and supplier diversification.

Each score must state:

- evidence required;
- verification source;
- internal reviewer;
- minimum passing score;
- disqualifying issues;
- evidence expiry date.

### Evidence required

- Completed store-opening and operating history.
- Audited financial statements and liquidity analysis.
- Binding capital and loss-funding commitments.
- Verified site pipeline, title or lease authority and permitted use.
- Prior localization and labor-welfare performance.
- Landlord, supplier, contractor and employee references.
- Beneficial ownership, family governance and succession information.
- All grocery, convenience, food-service, marketplace, delivery, logistics and landlord interests.
- Cybersecurity, data-protection and anti-bribery policies.

### Partner no-go conditions

Reject or suspend a candidate where there is:

- undisclosed competing activity;
- unexplained related-party pricing;
- refusal of audit, inspection or data-access rights;
- inability to evidence funding;
- pressure to use nominee, side-letter or informal agency arrangements;
- material bribery, sanctions or regulatory concerns;
- unresolved succession or ownership disputes;
- inability to substantiate proposed sites.

### Partner economics

Compensate the partner for verified delivery:

- site-development fees only after opening and agreed productivity milestones;
- arm’s-length logistics, recruitment and local-service fees;
- earn-outs linked to opening dates, availability, shrinkage, localization and four-wall EBITDA;
- suspension or clawback for delay, regulatory failure or undisclosed conflicts.

Aggregate and benchmark the full package, including equity, rent, site fees, logistics, management fees, procurement margins and service fees, to prevent double compensation.

# 5. Due diligence and validation requirements

## Partner and site diligence

Before definitive agreements:

- complete beneficial-ownership, sanctions, litigation, insolvency and adverse-media reviews;
- assess funding capacity, succession and family governance;
- review competing businesses and related-party interests;
- verify site ownership or lease authority;
- confirm permitted retail, delivery and signage use;
- independently benchmark rent, traffic, sales and construction costs;
- assess parking, loading, cold-chain and utility capacity;
- confirm partner service capability and references.

The retailer must retain the right to source sites independently. Partner-originated sites do not receive automatic approval.

## Legal and regulatory validation

Obtain written advice from licensed local counsel and, where appropriate, relevant authorities for the precise entity, activity and premises.

### UAE

Validate separately for:

- mainland and free-zone structures;
- Dubai, Abu Dhabi and other emirates;
- store retail;
- importation;
- warehousing;
- e-commerce;
- delivery;
- food registration;
- Emiratisation;
- data and marketing.

A free-zone structure should not be assumed to provide unrestricted mainland retail rights.

### Saudi Arabia

Validate:

- foreign investment and retail licensing;
- e-commerce, warehouse and import activities;
- importer-of-record status;
- municipal and premises approvals;
- Nitaqat and occupation-specific requirements;
- wages, work permits and third-party labor;
- food registration, halal and labeling;
- data, marketing and competition requirements.

### Qatar

Validate:

- ownership and activity approvals;
- retail, import, warehouse, e-commerce and delivery licensing;
- importer and distributor requirements;
- food registration and halal;
- applicability of Qatarization;
- data and consumer-protection requirements;
- competition and exclusivity;
- enforceability of exit and IP provisions.

### Change-sensitive issues

Refresh before each fiscal year, each Ramadan and each material expansion:

- ownership and licensing rules;
- occupation-specific localization;
- working hours, overtime and breaks;
- product registration and labeling;
- halal requirements;
- customs and duties;
- data hosting and transfers;
- loyalty, marketing and promotions;
- competition and merger-control requirements;
- VAT, withholding, transfer pricing and repatriation;
- arbitration, put/call, step-in, non-compete and brand-termination enforceability.

Each item must have an internal owner, adviser or authority source, written deliverable, validation date and consequence if unresolved.

# 6. Ownership, governance and control

## Ownership principles

Do not use a universal 51% target. Determine the structure by:

- country;
- exact activity;
- legal entity;
- store, import, warehouse, e-commerce and delivery requirements;
- premises and emirate or municipality;
- licensing practice.

The structure must distinguish:

- economic ownership;
- voting rights;
- board appointments;
- executive appointments;
- budget and capital control;
- bank-account authority;
- premises and licensing control;
- importer-of-record status;
- procurement control;
- data control;
- IP control;
- exit rights.

### Fallback structure

For each country, assess:

1. foreign-controlled operating company;
2. majority foreign-owned JV;
3. protected-minority JV;
4. local operating company with management and brand agreements;
5. service-provider or distribution model;
6. no-go if effective operating control cannot be protected.

Avoid nominee arrangements.

## Governance model

Each JV should have:

- shareholder-appointed directors;
- an independent chair without an automatic casting vote on fundamental matters;
- Audit and Risk Committee;
- Investment and Expansion Committee;
- Remuneration and Talent Committee;
- Food Safety and Quality Committee or equivalent executive forum.

### Retailer consent

- Brand use, licensing and sub-licensing.
- Core assortment and private-label specifications.
- Food-safety systems, withdrawals and recalls.
- Material technology, loyalty or cybersecurity changes.
- Customer-data access, transfer or monetization.
- Appointment or removal of key executives.
- Franchising or use of the concept outside the territory.
- Material changes to pricing architecture or market positioning.
- Store closures or format changes outside the approved plan.
- Use of retailer IP or know-how after termination.

### Partner consent

Limit to:

- unusual government commitments materially binding the partner;
- material commitments outside the approved plan affecting partner liability or reputation;
- significant unbudgeted land or construction obligations;
- actions materially affecting the partner’s licenses or regulatory position.

Partner consent should not extend to ordinary hiring, supplier selection, individual prices, quality, technology or store operations.

### Joint approval

- Annual budget and business plan.
- Capital calls and capital structure.
- Material debt, guarantees and security.
- New countries, cities or major formats.
- Acquisitions and disposals.
- Major litigation settlements.
- Related-party transactions.
- Change of control, liquidation or sale.

### Executive authority

The agreements must specify:

- who appoints and removes the CEO;
- whether the partner can block key appointments;
- who controls ordinary hiring;
- who controls ordinary prices and promotions;
- who approves the budget;
- who controls bank accounts and payments;
- who signs leases and supplier contracts;
- who can authorize an emergency recall;
- who communicates with regulators during a crisis.

The retailer should control or approve the appointment of the CEO, COO, CFO, commercial lead, technology lead and quality leader, subject to agreed competence criteria.

Management should operate within the approved budget without shareholder interference.

## Deadlock and funding default

Deadlock process:

1. Management resolution within five business days.
2. CEO and chair escalation.
3. Shareholder-principal meeting.
4. Independent expert determination for technical, accounting or valuation matters.
5. Mediation or arbitration where appropriate.
6. Buy-sell, put, call or third-party sale process after a defined period.

Essential operations must continue during deadlock, including payroll, regulatory filings, supplier payments, food safety, customer service, cybersecurity response and recalls.

Funding provisions should include:

- minimum initial funding;
- capital-call timing and form;
- equity versus shareholder debt;
- default interest;
- dilution;
- suspension of defaulting shareholder rights;
- non-defaulting shareholder funding rights;
- step-in and buyout rights;
- treatment of unfunded commitments in valuation.

# 7. Minority, management, data and IP protection

## Management and minority rights

Provide:

- objective executive appointment and removal standards;
- notice and cure periods;
- delegated management authority;
- shareholder non-interference;
- whistleblower and non-retaliation protection;
- timely financial and operational information;
- inspection and audit rights;
- protection against unauthorized dilution;
- pre-emption rights, subject to local law;
- tag-along rights and fair sale-process treatment.

## IP and know-how

The retailer should retain rights over:

- trademarks and brand assets;
- operating manuals and training materials;
- software configurations;
- private-label specifications;
- supplier and product databases;
- planograms and store layouts;
- customer insights and analytics models;
- operating-model improvements;
- locally developed Gulf adaptations.

The agreements must define whether the retailer may reuse localized adaptations in other Gulf markets and after termination.

## Data governance

Define:

- whether the JV is controller, processor or joint controller;
- retailer access rights;
- prohibited partner and affiliate uses;
- retention and deletion;
- hosting and cross-border transfers;
- security standards;
- breach notification;
- data portability;
- rights to aggregated or anonymized insights.

The partner must not use identifiable customer data, supplier pricing, category strategy or competitor intelligence for unrelated affiliates.

# 8. Format and proposition adaptation

## Common proposition

- European-quality fresh food and food safety.
- Strong halal governance.
- Arabic and English execution.
- Local and regional products integrated into core ranges.
- Family-size and sharing packs where justified.
- Value, core and premium private label.
- Known-value-item pricing on visible staples.
- Fresh, bakery, deli and prepared-food visibility.
- Meal bundles, gifting and hospitality ranges.
- Digital loyalty and scheduled delivery.
- Culturally appropriate service without relying on outdated assumptions.

## Country formats

| Market | Initial format hypotheses |
|---|---|
| UAE | 300–800 sqm express; 1,500–3,000 sqm neighborhood; optional 4,000–7,000 sqm flagship |
| Saudi Arabia | 800–1,500 sqm compact; 2,500–5,000 sqm neighborhood; larger format later |
| Qatar | 300–800 sqm convenience; 1,000–2,500 sqm compact premium |

Each format requires:

- minimum catchment density;
- sales-density target;
- maximum rent-to-sales ratio;
- parking and loading requirements;
- delivery radius and order-density threshold;
- labor and localization model;
- capex ceiling;
- four-wall EBITDA and payback hurdle;
- kill or resize criteria.

The UAE flagship and larger Saudi format remain outside the base case.

# 9. Ramadan and Eid operating model

Ramadan should be managed as a separate commercial, labor and supply-chain cycle. Dates, trading hours, overtime, breaks and public-holiday arrangements must be reconfirmed annually from current official guidance.

| Timing | Required actions |
|---|---|
| T–12 to T–9 months | Demand forecast, supplier capacity, import commitments and private-label planning |
| T–8 to T–6 months | Assortment, packaging, approvals, promotions, labor and delivery plans |
| T–5 to T–3 months | Purchase orders, safety stock, recruitment, training and store layouts |
| T–8 to T–4 weeks | Progressive inventory build, delivery reservations and operational testing |
| Ramadan | Daily monitoring of availability, waste, labor, picking, checkout and delivery |
| Final Ramadan days | Gifting, sweets, premium food and hospitality ranges; control overstock |
| Eid | Adjust staffing and trading hours; support gifting and family occasions |
| T+1 to T+4 weeks | Markdown seasonal inventory, normalize open-to-buy and review working capital |

Forecast by:

- country;
- city;
- format;
- category;
- customer mission;
- week and daypart;
- store versus digital channel.

Use low, base and high scenarios. Link purchasing to shelf life, supplier lead time, sell-through, waste ceilings, working-capital limits and post-Eid markdown capacity.

### Opening-window policy

1. **Preferred:** sufficient normal-season trading before Ramadan.
2. **Fallback:** opening after Ramadan.
3. **Exceptional:** opening within four to six weeks before Ramadan, requiring investment-committee approval and a documented risk case.

### Country emphasis

- **Saudi Arabia:** larger family baskets, bulk packs, fresh and prepared food, evening traffic and city-specific inventory.
- **UAE:** mixed-nationality demand, premium imports, ready meals, gifting and digital convenience.
- **Qatar:** concentrated Doha planning, imported-product availability, gifting and hospitality demand.

Restricted or culturally sensitive products require country-specific legal classification, supplier documentation, merchandising controls and approval before purchase orders are placed.

# 10. Supply chain and omnichannel

## Supply chain

- Country-specific importer-of-record and product-registration ownership.
- Direct-import versus UAE-routed analysis by category.
- Local and regional fresh sourcing.
- Dual suppliers for critical categories.
- Temperature-controlled infrastructure.
- Country safety stock.
- Traceability and recall capability.
- Alternative routes for border, port or shipping disruption.

### UAE

Use established logistics infrastructure and develop local fresh sourcing. Test the regional sourcing and services model before creating a hub.

### Saudi Arabia

Build local capability in fresh produce, dairy, poultry, bakery, dates, beverages, water and household products. Evaluate a Saudi distribution center or dedicated 3PL as store density develops.

### Qatar

Use a concentrated Doha model, direct import where advantageous, strategic safety stock and multiple sources or routes for vulnerable categories.

## Omnichannel

Launch with:

- Arabic/English website and app or compliant interim solution;
- store-based fulfillment where viable;
- scheduled delivery;
- click-and-collect where appropriate;
- inventory visibility;
- substitution controls;
- loyalty and personalized promotions;
- refunds and complaint workflows;
- recall traceability;
- payment security and cybersecurity controls.

The proprietary app should not unnecessarily delay store opening. A simplified or third-party solution may be used temporarily subject to data, cybersecurity and customer-service controls.

# 11. Workforce-localization compliance

Localization must be managed by legal entity, establishment, occupation, wage, contract type, nationality and third-party labor model.

Each register should show:

- applicable program and authority;
- covered occupations;
- required headcount or percentage;
- wage and registration conditions;
- treatment of temporary, outsourced and contractor labor;
- reporting deadlines;
- penalties and incentives;
- evidence of substantive employment.

Do not rely on nominal appointments, artificial wage arrangements or non-substantive employment.

## Saudi Arabia — Saudization

Obtain current confirmation from Saudi employment counsel and the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development regarding:

- Nitaqat classification by establishment;
- occupation-specific localization;
- wage and social-insurance treatment;
- work-permit implications;
- outsourced and third-party labor;
- current retail, sales, procurement, customer-service and management requirements.

Prioritize:

- store-management pipelines;
- HR and government relations;
- customer service and front-end supervision;
- e-commerce support and picking supervision;
- commercial analysis;
- administration, health and safety;
- selected quality roles.

Every expatriate specialist role requires a named Saudi deputy, knowledge-transfer milestones and a target transition date.

## UAE — Emiratisation

Confirm current obligations separately for stores, warehouses, logistics, e-commerce, technology and regional-services entities, including:

- employer-size thresholds;
- entity classifications;
- eligible occupations;
- wage and registration requirements;
- reporting and penalties.

National employees must hold genuine roles supported by job descriptions, payroll, attendance, performance reviews and development plans.

## Qatar — Qatarization

Do not assume a universal private-sector Qatarization quota for grocery retail. Obtain written current advice on:

- whether an obligation applies to the exact entity and activity;
- covered occupations;
- corporate versus store roles;
- reporting and documentation;
- incentives or employment programs.

If a quota is confirmed, include it in the base case. If unclear, use a conservative workforce reserve and require approval before opening.

## Workforce governance

- Country HR owns the workforce plan.
- Legal and compliance validate classifications and evidence.
- The country CEO owns operational delivery.
- The Talent Committee reviews quarterly.
- The board receives monthly dashboard summaries and quarterly compliance reports.

Monitor:

- headcount by nationality and role;
- wage and registration status;
- localization achievement;
- expatriate dependency;
- successor coverage;
- training completion;
- turnover and absenteeism;
- productivity and service quality;
- regulatory exposure and remediation.

Also audit recruitment fees, wage payment, transportation, accommodation, heat exposure, contractor practices, workplace injury and grievance mechanisms.

# 12. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Ownership or licensing assumptions prove incorrect | Activity-specific legal memoranda before signing or leasing |
| Partner becomes a gatekeeper | Independent site sourcing, measurable obligations and limited veto rights |
| JV deadlock | Unified authority matrix, escalation, expert determination and exit rights |
| Partner funding default | Binding capital calls, dilution, step-in and buyout remedies |
| Related-party overcharging | Disclosure, recusal, tendering, benchmarking and Audit Committee approval |
| Bribery or improper influence | Approved intermediaries, anti-bribery controls and no unapproved success fees |
| Weak or overpriced sites | Independent underwriting and investment-committee approval |
| Imported-margin erosion | Local sourcing, direct-import testing, dual sourcing and private label |
| Ramadan stockouts or excess | Scenario forecasts, phased inventory build and post-Eid clearance |
| Localization cost or talent shortages | Role segmentation, academies, succession and productivity modeling |
| Food fraud or safety incident | Supplier audits, traceability, QA teams and recall drills |
| Data misuse by partner | Defined data roles, access controls, affiliate restrictions and audits |
| Delivery losses | Density thresholds, scheduled slots, minimum baskets and store fulfillment |
| Labor-welfare failure | Contractor audits, grievance mechanisms, wage and heat-safety controls |
| Geopolitical or shipping disruption | Alternative routes, dual sourcing and country safety stock |
| Currency or trapped cash | Treasury controls, funding rules and repatriation review |
| Cyberattack | POS, warehouse and delivery resilience, vendor controls and recovery plans |
| Refrigeration or power failure | Backup power, maintenance, temperature monitoring and continuity plans |
| Partner succession or family conflict | Change-of-control provisions, continuity plan and exit rights |
| Cultural or religious misstep | Local review panels, Arabic-content controls and crisis protocols |
| Brand or know-how leakage | IP restrictions, access controls and post-termination provisions |

# 13. Financial and performance gates

Before first-site approval, the investment committee must approve country- and format-specific ranges for:

- maximum capex per store;
- maximum pre-opening loss;
- maximum working-capital exposure;
- maximum country capital exposure;
- minimum sales density;
- maximum rent-to-sales ratio;
- gross-margin range;
- labor and localization-cost ceilings;
- shrinkage and waste ceiling;
- minimum availability;
- maximum delivery loss per order;
- four-wall EBITDA target;
- payback period;
- ROIC hurdle;
- partner funding percentage;
- country stop-loss trigger.

Break-even must be reported separately at:

1. store four-wall level;
2. country operating-company level;
3. country platform level, including overhead;
4. regional level.

### Gate 1 — Structure and partner approval

Required:

- legal structure and licensing memoranda;
- partner diligence clearance;
- verified site pipeline;
- funding commitments and default remedies;
- preliminary unit economics;
- localization and compliance plan.

### Gate 2 — First-site approval

Required:

- independent site underwriting;
- permitted-use and licensing pathway;
- four-wall P&L under base and downside cases;
- labor and localization model;
- supply-chain and cold-chain plan;
- opening and Ramadan calendar;
- technology and delivery economics.

### Gate 3 — Launch-capacity and readiness approval

Required before more than one country opens a first store in the same six-month period:

- sufficient senior management and country leadership;
- construction and real-estate resources;
- food-safety and quality staffing;
- supply-chain and procurement capacity;
- technology and digital readiness;
- Arabic content capability;
- HR and localization support;
- internal audit and compliance coverage.

### Gate 4 — Pilot review

At weeks 13 and 26, review:

- sales per square meter;
- gross margin and price index;
- four-wall EBITDA trajectory;
- rent-to-sales ratio;
- availability, shrinkage and waste;
- labor productivity;
- customer retention and loyalty;
- delivery contribution;
- localization and compliance;
- partner delivery against commitments.

### Gate 5 — Scale, pause or exit

Scale only where the pilot demonstrates:

- credible four-wall profitability;
- acceptable rent, labor and supply economics;
- sustainable repeat purchase;
- viable omnichannel contribution;
- no unresolved material compliance deficiencies;
- partner funding and site delivery;
- workforce-localization performance.

# 15. Final decision

Approve a **UAE-learning, Saudi-scale and Qatar-focused** program with:

- three country-specific JVs;
- retailer control or strong contractual protection over brand, standards, data, technology, private label, food safety and key executives;
- measurable local-partner obligations;
- UAE and Saudi preparation beginning in September 2026;
- Qatar limited initially to diligence and a one-store-plus-digital option;
- no fixed ownership percentages or assumed localization quotas until validated;
- explicit capital caps, stop-loss rules and performance hurdles before scale.

Do not approve:

- broad Qatar rollout;
- large-format stores;
- an assumed UAE regional hub;
- localization budgets based on unverified quotas;
- partner fees unrelated to measurable delivery;
- store leases that bypass independent underwriting;
- simultaneous multi-country openings without a launch-capacity review.

The preferred model is a **controlled operating partnership**: the European retailer supplies the brand, systems, know-how and standards; the local conglomerate supplies verifiable sites, capital, local execution, talent and stakeholder capability; and both parties are bound by measurable economics, disciplined governance, lawful localization and credible renegotiation or exit rights.
