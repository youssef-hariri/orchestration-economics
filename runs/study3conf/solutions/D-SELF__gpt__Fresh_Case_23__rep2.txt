# Pan-African Banking Integration and Market-Consolidation Strategy

**Countries:** Nigeria, Kenya, Morocco  
**Initial program horizon:** 36 months, with timing contingent on readiness  
**Recommended model:** Federated convergence with locally accountable, separately licensed banks

---

## 1. Executive summary

The group should pursue **disciplined convergence rather than forced uniformity**.

The recommended target state is:

> Three locally accountable banks operating under common group standards, shared digital and control capabilities, interoperable technology, and selectively shared services—while retaining country-specific processing where required by regulation, resilience, payment infrastructure, data governance, language, or market conditions.

The group should standardize:

- Cybersecurity and technology controls.
- APIs and integration standards.
- Data governance and management reporting.
- Product components and digital design.
- Financial-crime principles.
- Procurement and selected shared services.
- Governance, risk, and performance frameworks.

It should initially preserve:

- Local legal entities, boards, licenses, and regulatory accountability.
- Domestic payment and settlement connectivity.
- Country-specific data controls.
- Local products, pricing, credit models, and collections.
- Local languages and customer-service models.
- Branch, agent, merchant, and relationship-management capabilities.
- Country-level control functions and resilience arrangements.

### Strategic sequence

1. **Protect and diagnose** all three banks.
2. Establish a **common group control plane**.
3. Converge selected digital journeys above existing cores.
4. Select the first core migration using mandatory readiness criteria.
5. Migrate sequentially with tested fallback and country isolation.
6. Stabilize, validate customer outcomes, and confirm benefits.
7. Expand shared services selectively.
8. Evaluate deeper platform convergence, brand retirement, branch rationalization, and legal consolidation independently by country.

### Immediate recommendation

Approve the federated integration framework and 90-day mobilization program. Defer:

- First core-migration country.
- Final target-core decision.
- Centralized data environment.
- Major vendor consolidation.
- Fixed synergy commitments.
- Legal-entity merger.
- Permanent brand retirement.

# 2. Strategic principles

| Principle | Application |
|---|---|
| **Standardize the platform, localize the proposition** | Common controls, APIs, security, and digital components; local products, payments, languages, and pricing. |
| **Digital before core** | Improve customer journeys above existing cores before undertaking core conversion. |
| **Regulation is a design input** | Engage each supervisor before committing to architecture, outsourcing, data, or migration dates. |
| **No big bang** | Migrate sequentially, with controlled scope, dress rehearsals, fallback, and independent assurance. |
| **Customer continuity first** | Protect balances, loans, cards, mandates, salary payments, standing orders, and access. |
| **Local accountability remains real** | Local boards, CEOs, control functions, and licensed entities retain defined authority. |
| **Exceptions are designed, not accidental** | Record, fund, govern, and periodically review country exceptions. |
| **Resilience over theoretical simplicity** | Do not create a common-mode outage by over-centralizing critical services. |
| **Evidence before repetition** | Do not migrate the next country until the previous migration demonstrates stable performance. |
| **Legal consolidation is optional** | Merge entities only if the country-specific regulatory, customer, capital, tax, and financial case is positive. |

# 3. Decisions and authority

## 3.1 Decisions for approval now

The group board should approve:

1. The federated operating model.
2. The no-big-bang migration principle.
3. Continued local legal entities, boards, licenses, and regulatory accountability during the initial program.
4. Digital convergence before core conversion.
5. Endorsed local brands during transition.
6. A Country Exception Register and Regulatory Dependency Register.
7. Customer continuity and remediation as non-negotiable constraints.
8. Independent assurance at major gates.
9. The 90-day mobilization and diligence program.
10. Phased funding subject to country-level business cases.

## 3.2 Decisions to defer

Defer:

- First core-migration country.
- One-core versus multi-core architecture.
- Centralized data lake or universal customer identifier.
- Permanent centralization of fraud, AML, credit, customer service, or operations.
- Fixed synergy or payback commitments.
- Automatic legal-entity merger.
- Permanent local-brand retirement.
- Guaranteed completion of all migrations within 36 months.

## 3.3 Decision-rights framework

| Decision | Primary authority | Required involvement |
|---|---|---|
| Group standards and target architecture | Group board and relevant group committees | Country boards, legal, compliance, technology risk |
| Country data or processing exception | Local board for local obligations; Group Risk for group impact | Legal, compliance, technology risk |
| First migration recommendation | Group Integration Steering Committee | Country CEO, local board, CRO, COO, CIO |
| Migration funding | Group board or delegated investment committee | Country CFO, country board, Group Risk |
| Country go-live | Local board and country CEO | Group approval and independent assurance |
| Cutover stop or fallback | Designated country incident commander | Immediate escalation to country CEO, local board chair, group sponsor, and supervisor where required |
| Restart after fallback | Country CEO and local-board-designated authority | Group sponsor, risk, technology, and supervisor where required |
| Customer compensation | Country Customer Harm and Remediation Committee | Country CFO, legal, compliance, risk, group customer executive |
| Regulatory submissions | Local licensed entity | Group legal, compliance, and regulatory coordination |
| Permanent country exception | Local board and Group Risk/Legal | Group board if material to risk appetite, capital, or resilience |
| Legal-entity merger | Local and group boards | Applicable statutory and supervisory processes |
| Legacy decommissioning | Country COO/CIO and local board | Risk, compliance, audit, legal, and group architecture |

Local boards retain authority over matters within their statutory, fiduciary, regulatory, and customer-risk responsibilities. The group may withhold funding or prohibit group deployment but should not override a local board on a legally reserved matter.

# 4. Target operating model

## 4.1 Group-owned or group-governed capabilities

### Governance and control

- Group risk appetite and minimum control standards.
- Cybersecurity, identity, privileged-access, and technology-risk standards.
- Operational-resilience and business-continuity methodology.
- Financial-crime-control principles.
- Product governance and customer-treatment standards.
- Incident, issue, audit, and escalation processes.
- Management reporting and benefits-realization methodology.
- Third-party risk and outsourcing standards.
- Internal-audit methodology.
- Leadership, succession, and talent-mobility frameworks.

### Technology standards

- Secure API standards and version control.
- Data classification, lineage, quality, and access principles.
- Secure software development and release management.
- Common service-management and incident taxonomies.
- Digital design and accessibility principles.
- Product metadata and catalogue structures.
- Monitoring and control-evidence standards.
- Recovery and resilience-testing standards.

## 4.2 Country-owned or country-controlled capabilities

Each bank should retain responsibility for:

- Local regulatory engagement and statutory obligations.
- Local boards and control functions.
- Domestic payment and settlement connectivity.
- Core banking and country account identifiers.
- Local KYC and identity-provider integrations.
- Credit-bureau reporting and locally approved credit models.
- Tax, accounting, records retention, and statutory reporting.
- Local data storage, processing, and remote-support arrangements.
- Product eligibility, pricing, fees, credit policy, and collections.
- Local languages and customer support.
- Branch, agent, merchant, and relationship-management models.
- Customer complaints, remediation, and vulnerable-customer treatment.
- Employment, government, and community relationships.
- Local operational fallback and crisis execution.

## 4.3 Shared-services sequence

Centralize gradually:

1. Procurement frameworks and vendor management.
2. Cybersecurity tooling and monitoring, with local incident ownership.
3. Finance consolidation and management information.
4. Technology infrastructure and service management.
5. Data platforms and analytics where permitted.
6. Selected back-office processing.
7. Treasury analytics and liquidity visibility.
8. HR services.
9. Credit operations only where legally and commercially viable.

Customer-facing and judgment-intensive functions should remain local until service quality, control evidence, and regulatory acceptance support consolidation.

# 5. Country-exception governance

Every exception must document:

- Country and legal entity.
- Requirement and rationale.
- Temporary or permanent status.
- Classification:
  - confirmed legal or regulatory requirement;
  - supervisory expectation requiring validation;
  - prudent operating assumption; or
  - commercial/customer-design choice.
- Evidence source and date.
- Accountable executive.
- Risk and cost impact.
- Required mitigation.
- Funding source.
- Review date.
- Exit criteria, if temporary.

Permanent exceptions require local board and Group Risk/Legal approval. Exceptions affecting group risk appetite, capital, or resilience require group board approval.

A dedicated **country-exception budget** should fund local payment, language, data, regulatory, resilience, and control-function requirements.

- Approval.
- Non-objection.
- Notification.
- Registration or authorization.
- Certification.
- Consultation.

- Classification.
- Source and date.
- Required action.
- Status.
- Escalation owner.

# 8. Phased timeline

| Phase | Timing | Primary outcomes |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Protect and diagnose** | Months 0–3 | Stabilize operations, secure environments, map systems/data/vendors, confirm leadership and regulatory obligations |
| **1. Common control plane** | Months 3–9 | Implement group minimum controls, data standards, API governance, service management, and vendor-risk controls |
| **2. Digital convergence** | Months 6–15 | Deploy common digital components above existing cores with country-specific adapters |
| **3. First core migration** | Indicatively Months 12–20 | Execute the least risky viable migration scope after readiness and board approval |
| **4. Stabilize before repeating** | Following first migration | Complete two month-end closes, a peak event, a recovery test, and relevant product/regulatory cycles |
| **5. Remaining migrations** | Months 18–32 or later | Migrate additional countries only after evidence-based approval |
| **6. Optional optimization** | Month 30 onward | Evaluate shared services, vendors, branches, brands, legal entities, and deeper platform convergence |

Countries may progress at different speeds. Phase dates are planning windows, not mandatory simultaneous deployment.

# 9. First-migration strategy

## 9.1 Selection process

At Month 9, management must recommend one of:

1. Full-bank migration candidate.
2. Controlled product or customer-cohort migration.
3. Deferral because no country is ready.

No option may proceed without passing mandatory regulatory, data-integrity, payment, customer-continuity, resilience, fallback, local-board, and control-function conditions.

If no country qualifies, the group should continue digital convergence, data remediation, control strengthening, and regulatory engagement while deferring core migration.

## 9.2 Indicative scorecard

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Regulatory feasibility | 20% |
| Data quality and reconciliation readiness | 15% |
| Core and product complexity | 15% |
| Payment and settlement complexity | 15% |
| Customer and transaction criticality | 10% |
| Target-platform fit and reversibility | 10% |
| Local technology and control capability | 5% |
| Management and workforce readiness | 5% |
| Financial benefit | 5% |

Financial benefit cannot offset a failure in customer safety, regulatory readiness, data integrity, payment resilience, or fallback capability.

## 9.3 Country hypotheses

- **Kenya:** likely digital-product pilot; first core migration only if mobile-money, payment, data, credit-reporting, and third-party dependencies are manageable.
- **Morocco:** possible first core migration if data and core complexity are lower and bilingual, privacy, and supervisory requirements are resolved.
- **Nigeria:** generally second because of scale, USSD, agent, payment, settlement, and fraud complexity, unless the specific bank is unusually simple and resilient.

# 10. Migration controls

## 10.1 Data and financial integrity

Reconcile:

- Customer balances.
- Loan principal, interest, fees, amortization, and tax.
- Cards and tokens.
- Mandates, beneficiaries, standing orders, and direct debits.
- Salary credits and recurring payments.
- General-ledger, suspense, settlement, and liquidity positions.
- Regulatory-reporting output.
- Credit-bureau reporting.

Every item must reconcile or have an approved exception with:

- Named owner.
- Remediation date.
- Customer-impact assessment.
- Financial treatment.
- Appropriate risk approval.

## 10.2 Payments and operations

- Payment, card, switch, mobile-money, and settlement testing complete.
- Production-scale dress rehearsals complete.
- Salary, month-end, and high-volume scenarios tested.
- Contact-center, branch, agent, fraud, and complaints capacity increased.
- Local-language and disclosure testing complete.
- Fallback and disaster-recovery testing successful.
- Relevant product-specific interest, repayment, tax, and reporting cycles tested.

## 10.3 Customer safety

- Required communications delivered.
- Compensation and fee-waiver rules approved.
- Vulnerable and high-risk segments identified.
- Cutover monitoring active.
- Stop, rollback, and remediation authority confirmed.

# 11. Resilience and fallback

Every migration runbook must specify:

- Abort-before-cutover procedure.
- Post-cutover rollback conditions.
- Source-system operating status.
- Duplicate-transaction prevention.
- Manual processing controls.
- Reconciliation and repair.
- Customer compensation.
- Regulatory notification.
- Vendor support.
- Maximum fallback duration.
- Funding authority.
- Stop and restart authority.

Fallback modes include:

1. Abort before cutover.
2. Controlled rollback.
3. Extended coexistence.
4. Segmented migration.
5. Shadow or parallel processing.
6. Manual contingency processing.
7. Country-isolated operating mode.

Each bank must retain:

- Local payment fallback capability.
- Customer-support access.
- Emergency transaction procedures.
- Regulatory-reporting capability.
- Tested data recovery.
- Exit arrangements for critical vendors.

# 12. Country playbooks

## 12.1 Nigeria

### Strategic posture

**Payment integrity, fraud control, and resilience before product breadth or aggressive consolidation.**

### Priorities

- High-volume and peak-load testing.
- Domestic payment-rail reconciliation, subject to entity- and rail-specific validation.
- USSD reliability and low-bandwidth access.
- Agent liquidity and settlement monitoring.
- Beneficiary and transaction-risk controls.
- Scam and account-takeover response.
- Reconciliation across core, cards, switches, mobile channels, and the general ledger.
- Power, connectivity, and disaster-recovery scenarios.

### Priority scenarios

- Salary-payment peak.
- Month-end instant-payment surge.
- USSD or network outage.
- Switch outage.
- Agent liquidity stress.
- Widespread scam campaign.
- Power interruption.
- Delayed settlement.
- Large-scale account takeover.

### Customer and brand approach

Protect salary accounts, government-related payments, recurring transfers, cards, loan schedules, SMEs, and agent-dependent customers.

English should be the principal formal banking language. Additional local-language support should be determined by customer segment, safety need, and commercial relevance.

Retain the local brand with group endorsement initially. Do not lead with group-brand claims until service reliability and fraud response are proven.

---

## 12.2 Kenya

### Strategic posture

**Mobile-first experience, ecosystem reliability, and separate governance for digital credit and alternative data.**

### Priorities

- Mobile-money settlement and reconciliation.
- Applicable payment interoperability.
- Agent and merchant liquidity.
- Reversal and duplicate-debit controls.
- Credit-reference reporting.
- Consent and alternative-data governance.
- English and Swahili journeys.
- Clear responsibility across bank and third parties.

### Priority scenarios

- Mobile-money provider outage.
- Delayed reversals.
- Agent liquidity shortage.
- Merchant settlement failure.
- Digital-credit reporting error.
- Third-party API outage.
- Consent or alternative-data failure.
- Credit-bureau submission failure.

### Customer and brand approach

Protect mobile-money links, salary credits, recurring payments, loan schedules, agent and merchant settlement, and credit-bureau records.

For every material external dependency, identify technical, commercial, and regulatory owners; service levels; fallback; reconciliation; and complaint responsibility.

Kenya is a strong digital-product pilot candidate, but not automatically the first core-migration candidate. Prioritize mobile reliability and interoperability before changing branch identity.

---

## 12.3 Morocco

### Strategic posture

**Bilingual, formal, privacy-conscious, and relationship-led integration.**

### Priorities

- Arabic and French journeys, notices, statements, support, and complaints, subject to product-specific validation.
- Right-to-left interface testing.
- Consistent bilingual legal and product terminology.
- Local or approved data-processing arrangements.
- Domestic payment and card testing.
- Branch-assisted digital servicing.
- Formal approval and documentation gates.
- Local records and remote-access controls.

### Priority scenarios

- Arabic rendering or right-to-left interface failure.
- Bilingual statement inconsistency.
- Branch-assisted digital overload.
- Cloud or remote-support restriction.
- Delayed formal approval.
- Domestic card or payment connectivity failure.
- Bilingual complaint backlog.
- Records or audit-access failure.

### Customer and brand approach

Retain branch and relationship-manager servicing during early integration. Avoid unnecessary re-contracting or documentation changes.

Retain the local brand unless customer research and the commercial case support change. Do not link rebranding automatically to core migration.

# 13. Brand and customer approach

## 13.1 Brand sequence

1. **Months 0–12:** Preserve local names, accounts, contracts, cards, and channels; use limited group endorsement.
2. **Months 12–24:** Align digital design, security messaging, visual principles, and corporate communications.
3. **After operational stability:** Conduct country- and segment-specific brand research.
4. **Month 24 onward:** Decide separately whether to retain, endorse, or retire each local brand.

## 13.2 Customer continuity commitments

Before any material change, communicate:

- What changes and what does not.
- Whether customer action is required.
- Impact on accounts, cards, mandates, loans, and payments.
- Service windows.
- Security and anti-phishing guidance.
- Complaint, escalation, and compensation arrangements.

Use:

- SMS and email.
- Mobile applications.
- Branches and agents.
- Contact centers.
- Websites.
- Relationship managers.

Critical content should be available in:

- English, with locally appropriate support in Nigeria.
- English and Swahili in Kenya.
- Arabic and French in Morocco, subject to product-specific legal and customer-comprehension validation.

## 13.3 Customer harm and remediation

Establish a country-level Customer Harm and Remediation Committee responsible for:

- Automatic reimbursement for defined duplicate debits and failed transactions.
- Compensation bands and approval limits.
- Vulnerable-customer escalation.
- Accounting and provisioning treatment.
- Regulator and board reporting.
- Customer appeals and disputes.
- Maximum response times.
- Root-cause analysis and control remediation.

# 15. Cybersecurity, financial crime, and data governance

## Cybersecurity

Within 90 days:

- Remove unnecessary privileged access.
- Reconcile identities across entities.
- Enforce multifactor authentication for sensitive access.
- Review internet-facing assets and unsupported technology.
- Secure vendor remote access.
- Test backup restoration and ransomware recovery.
- Establish group visibility with local incident command.
- Conduct targeted phishing, payment-fraud, and account-takeover assessments.

Longer term:

- Common security baseline.
- Country-appropriate security operations.
- Incident-response playbooks.
- Independent penetration testing.
- Secure development controls.
- Privileged-access recertification.
- Cyber-fraud fusion.
- Tested recovery procedures.

Security telemetry, logs, and incident data require defined hosting, access, retention, backup, and cross-border-transfer rules.

## Financial crime and model risk

Define separate ownership for:

- Group AML/CFT and sanctions principles.
- Country risk assessments.
- Transaction-monitoring scenarios.
- Alert disposition.
- Fraud and scam operations.
- Regulatory reporting.
- Model validation.
- Cross-border intelligence sharing.
- Customer remediation.

Credit, fraud, AML, sanctions, segmentation, pricing, and collections models require country-level validation, documented data lineage, approval by the appropriate control authority, and monitoring for drift, bias, false positives, and customer harm.

## Data governance

Establish:

- Data inventory and ownership.
- Data classification.
- Customer and account lineage.
- Consent and purpose management.
- Access logging.
- Retention and deletion schedules.
- Data-quality exception ownership.
- Model-data provenance.
- Country-specific transfer and support-access assessments.
- Legal-discovery and audit-access procedures.

- Financial condition.
- Cross-country concentration.

# 17. Financial case and benefits realization

Before major migration funding is released, management must present country-level base, downside, and upside cases covering:

- Core and integration technology.
- Digital channels.
- Cybersecurity and resilience.
- Data remediation.
- Regulatory and advisory costs.
- Workforce and retention.
- Vendor transition and dual running.
- Country exceptions.
- Customer communications.
- Customer-remediation reserve.
- Customer attrition.
- Run-rate savings.
- Revenue synergies.
- Delay and failure scenarios.
- Capital and liquidity impacts.
- Payback range.

Benefits must distinguish:

- Gross savings.
- Net savings after local exceptions and resilience costs.
- Genuine revenue synergies.
- Ordinary market growth.
- Avoided losses.
- One-time remediation.
- Recurring run-rate reductions.

Funding should be released by phase and country. Reapproval is required if cost, timing, regulatory requirements, customer attrition, dual running, or resilience needs exceed approved tolerances.

No benefit should be recognized until the change is live and validated by the country CFO and independent assurance.

- High-severity defects past due.

## Risk and compliance

- Fraud and scam losses.
- Account-takeover and mule indicators.
- AML and sanctions backlogs.
- Credit-bureau accuracy.
- Regulatory-reporting defects.
- Cyber vulnerabilities past due.
- Outsourcing-risk exceptions.
- Open supervisory commitments.
- Customer-remediation cases.

## People and financial

- Critical-role retention.
- Training completion and proficiency.
- Control-function vacancies.
- Employee engagement.
- Succession readiness.
- Knowledge-transfer completion.
- Actual versus approved integration cost.
- Validated savings.
- Dual-running cost.
- Revenue synergies separate from ordinary growth.
- Return on transformation investment.

### Illustrative metric definitions

**Payment success rate:** measured daily and weekly by country and payment rail against the 90-day pre-migration baseline. A deterioration beyond the approved tolerance for two consecutive reporting intervals triggers escalation and a fallback review.

**Reconciliation exception:** any balance, transaction, mandate, settlement, or reporting difference that remains unresolved or lacks approved documentation.

**Material customer harm:** determined by financial loss, access failure, incorrect obligations, duration, scale, vulnerable-customer impact, or regulatory significance—not merely aggregate incident volume.

# 20. Assumptions requiring validation

The following are planning assumptions, not confirmed legal conclusions or guaranteed outcomes:

1. Each bank can remain separately licensed and locally accountable throughout the initial program.
2. Shared digital and control capabilities can be deployed without prohibited data transfer or unacceptable outsourcing risk.
3. Payment connectivity and certification requirements differ by country, entity, and rail.
4. Country-resident processing may be required permanently in some areas.
5. Local-language requirements vary by product, channel, customer segment, and legal context.
6. Core-system complexity, data quality, and vendor support are not yet sufficiently known to select the first migration.
7. Digital modernization can proceed above existing cores without requiring simultaneous core conversion.
8. Existing customer, account, loan, card, mandate, and payment data can be remediated within the program economics.
9. Local technology, regulatory, control-function, and language talent can be retained.
10. Critical vendors will provide adequate transition, support, audit, and exit rights.
11. Shared services will require country-specific resilience and data controls.
12. The cost of dual running and local exceptions may reduce headline synergies.
13. The optimal long-term model may include multiple strategic cores.
14. Legal-entity consolidation may not produce sufficient regulatory, customer, capital, tax, or financial benefit.
15. Any language, payment, data, cloud, outsourcing, or supervisory conclusion must be confirmed by the relevant country legal, compliance, and regulatory owners.

# 21. Stop, pause, and escalation triggers

Pause or reverse a migration if there is:

- An unexplained balance or loan-obligation discrepancy.
- Material settlement or general-ledger failure.
- Payment deterioration beyond approved tolerance.
- Significant fraud, scam, or account-takeover increase.
- Incorrect credit-bureau reporting.
- Material customer-access or vulnerable-customer harm.
- Regulatory objection or overdue critical commitment.
- Failed fallback or recovery testing.
- Loss of critical staff weakening controls or operations.
- Failure to meet communications or remediation readiness.
- Unacceptable common-mode or third-party concentration risk.
- Material cost, timing, or benefits deviation beyond approved tolerance.

The migration runbook must define who can stop, who can restart, who can approve compensation, and how decisions are communicated to regulators, boards, employees, customers, and vendors.

# 22. Legacy decommissioning

Legacy systems should not be decommissioned merely because migration has stabilized.

A separate gate must confirm:

- Final customer, loan, card, mandate, settlement, and regulatory reconciliation.
- Historical statement and transaction-history access.
- Records-retention compliance.
- Legal-discovery and audit access.
- Data ownership, deletion, and anonymization procedures.
- Interface retirement.
- Vendor exit and license termination.
- Cybersecurity closure.
- Local board and control-function approval.
- Ongoing customer-support capability.
- Cost-benefit justification.
- Ownership of defects discovered after shutdown.

A legacy platform may remain read-only or operational for a defined period with an owner, budget, access model, and sunset date.

# 23. First 90 days

## Days 0–30

- Appoint group and country integration leadership.
- Confirm boards, CEOs, control functions, and decision rights.
- Freeze nonessential core and payment-system changes.
- Establish customer, payment, cyber, liquidity, and incident control towers.
- Inventory systems, vendors, data, interfaces, and privileged access.
- Identify critical roles and begin retention.
- Open regulatory and legal dependency registers.
- Preserve local brands and customer continuity.
- Establish remediation-funding principles.

## Days 31–60

- Assess data quality for customers, deposits, loans, cards, mandates, and transactions.
- Map payment, settlement, liquidity, and reconciliation dependencies.
- Define group standards, country exceptions, and architecture options.
- Assess cloud, outsourcing, cross-border access, and data processing.
- Conduct customer and employee research.
- Establish preliminary migration-readiness scores.
- Define KPI baselines and ownership.
- Prepare country business cases.

## Days 61–90

- Hold structured supervisory briefings.
- Approve the common control-plane program.
- Begin identity, cyber, API, reporting, and service-management work.
- Launch country-adapted digital prototypes.
- Sign management compacts.
- Approve migration methodology and fallback design.
- Establish the board dashboard.
- Complete Gate 1 stabilization reporting.

# Final recommendation

Approve the **federated integration framework and 90-day mobilization program**.

Do not yet approve:

- The first core-migration country.
- The final target platform.
- A centralized data environment.
- Major regional vendor consolidation.
- Fixed synergy commitments.
- Legal-entity merger.
- Permanent brand retirement.
- Irreversible shared-service commitments.

Kenya is a likely digital-product pilot, but first core migration should be selected only after evidence-based readiness assessment. Morocco could be first if its core and data are demonstrably simpler and its bilingual, privacy, and supervisory requirements are resolved. Nigeria should generally follow a proven migration unless its specific facts show unusually low conversion risk.

The most durable outcome may be three locally licensed banks operating on a common architecture with permanent country exceptions. Success should be judged not by the number of systems or entities eliminated, but by:

- uninterrupted critical services;
- supervisory confidence;
- customer trust;
- control integrity;
- resilience;
- local market relevance;
- validated financial benefits; and
- risk-adjusted shareholder value.
