# 2026 International Launch Strategy  
## Culturally Configurable AI Emotional-Wellbeing Companion

## Executive decision

Launch as a **privacy-preserving, non-clinical wellbeing and care-navigation service**, not as an AI therapist, artificial friend, diagnostic tool, or emergency service.

### Global value proposition

> **An AI tool that helps people notice how they are doing, try general coping strategies, organize their thoughts, and connect with appropriate human support.**

The companion may support reflection, stress management, sleep and routine goals, general coping exercises, conversation preparation, and local care navigation. It **does not replace professional care, therapy, medical advice, or emergency services**.

The recommended model is:

- **One global safety, privacy, and governance architecture**
- **Country-level legal, clinical, linguistic, and community validation**
- **Regionally differentiated positioning and feature configuration**
- **User choice rather than cultural inference**
- **Staged pilots rather than simultaneous regional rollout**
- **Success measured by safe, informed usefulness—not disclosure, dependence, or time spent**

---

# 1. Universal product principles

## 1.1 Global safety invariants

These apply in every market and cannot be weakened for growth or localization:

- Clearly disclose that the user is interacting with AI.
- State that the service is not therapy, diagnosis, medical care, or emergency support.
- Do not claim to understand feelings, detect suicide risk reliably, or guarantee safety.
- Do not diagnose, prescribe, recommend medication changes, or provide individualized treatment.
- Do not use emotional data for advertising, employment, insurance, education, immigration, eligibility, or disciplinary decisions.
- Do not contact family, employers, authorities, or emergency services autonomously.
- Do not use guilt, exclusivity, romance, simulated need, or manipulative engagement tactics.
- Make check-ins, reminders, memory, and sharing optional and revocable.
- Provide local, verified crisis and referral resources.
- Test for self-harm, abuse, coercive control, psychosis, mania, overdose, child safety, and other high-risk situations.
- Evaluate both missed crises and harmful over-escalation.
- Provide accessible text alternatives to voice and low-bandwidth pathways.
- Maintain clear human-review disclosures where human review exists.

### Recommended product description

> “An AI-powered wellbeing tool that can help you reflect, try general coping exercises, organize your thoughts, and find human support. It is not a therapist, doctor, diagnostic service, or emergency service.”

## 1.2 Locally configurable elements

Subject to central safety, clinical, privacy, and legal approval, countries may configure:

- Language, dialect, script, register, and pronouns
- Tone, warmth, and conversational directness
- Initial entry points, such as sleep, stress, relationships, or reflection
- Practical, reflective, secular, spiritual, or faith-compatible modes
- Trusted-person terminology and permissions
- Notification wording and privacy defaults
- Voice, messaging, web, and offline channels
- Referral directories and emergency pathways
- Accessibility formats and local support content
- Institutional distribution models

## 1.3 Classification of recommendations

Every localization decision must be labelled:

| Label | Meaning | Required action |
|---|---|---|
| **Universal requirement** | Safety, privacy, accessibility, or scope principle | Implement globally |
| **Evidence-supported local requirement** | Supported by local law, research, or service conditions | Implement with documented evidence |
| **Hypothesis to test** | Plausible but not established | A/B test or validate before making default |
| **Prohibited assumption** | Could create harm or stereotyping | Do not infer or implement by geography alone |

Examples: “Faith content should be opt-in” is a universal safety requirement. “Users prefer a restrained tone” is a local hypothesis. “Family is the safest escalation route” is a prohibited assumption.

# 2. Trust, privacy, and consent architecture

## 2.1 Layered consent

Separate, understandable permissions should cover:

- Core service use
- Saving conversation history
- Mood or wellbeing tracking
- AI memory and personalization
- Voice recording and transcription
- Model improvement or research
- Trusted-person sharing
- Institutional participation
- Human safety review
- Wearables or messaging-platform integrations

Users should be able to use basic support without extensive emotional disclosure.

### Required comprehension checks

Before sensitive use, users should be able to explain:

- Who can see their conversations
- Whether an employer, school, insurer, family member, or purchaser can access them
- Whether the AI contacts emergency services
- What data is stored and for how long
- Whether data is used for model improvement
- How deletion works
- Whether voice recordings are retained

If users cannot answer accurately, the consent flow is not adequate.

## 2.2 Data minimization and retention

Recommended defaults:

| Data type | Default approach |
|---|---|
| Conversation history | Off by default or short, user-selected retention |
| Mood tracking | Optional and user-controlled |
| Voice audio | Delete after transcription unless explicitly saved |
| Precise location and contacts | Do not collect by default |
| Sensitive trait inference | Prohibited unless necessary and explicitly governed |
| Safety-event records | Minimized, access-restricted, and retained only as legally and operationally necessary |
| Model training | No use by default; separate opt-in only |
| Backups | Defined deletion interval and disclosed exceptions |

Implement encryption, least-privilege access, identity/content separation where feasible, access logs, penetration testing, prompt-injection defenses, and breach-response procedures.

The company should publish a data-flow description covering model providers, subprocessors, hosting locations, human-review access, analytics, backups, deletion propagation, and government-request handling.

## 2.3 Institutional and trusted-person separation

Employers, universities, insurers, public agencies, and family purchasers must not receive individual:

- Conversations
- Journal entries
- Mood scores
- Risk labels
- Usage histories
- Inferred diagnoses or vulnerabilities
- Lists of nonparticipants

Aggregate reporting must include minimum cohort sizes, suppression rules, repeated-query protections, and re-identification testing.

Use a universal **trusted-person** model rather than a family-default model. Sharing options should include:

- No sharing
- One-time message
- Selected summary
- Shared goal only
- Crisis plan only
- Time-limited access
- Immediately revocable access

Before sharing, ask whether disclosure could cause violence, punishment, outing, loss of housing, employment consequences, or other harm. Users must be warned that revocation cannot undo screenshots or information already viewed.

# 3. Regional launch playbooks

## A. East Asia

**Scope note:** Japan, South Korea, mainland China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, and other markets require separate country strategies. Legal, platform, language, family, work, and privacy conditions differ materially.

### Positioning

**Launch hypothesis:**

> “A private, low-pressure tool for managing everyday stress, sleep, focus, routines, and emotional balance.”

Test practical, reflective, and care-navigation positioning. Avoid framing the product as a performance enhancer that normalizes overwork or study pressure.

### Feature adaptation

- Short, optional check-ins with deeper reflection available
- Sleep, workload, exam, commuting, caregiving, and burnout modules
- Structured journaling and pattern review
- “Prepare what to say” tools for clinicians, teachers, managers, or family
- Discreet notifications and lock-screen controls
- Text-first experience initially; voice only after privacy and accuracy testing
- Low-bandwidth and low-end-device support
- No unmoderated emotional peer communities

### Language and tone

- Test formal, neutral, and casual registers.
- Review Japanese politeness, Korean honorifics, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, Cantonese/Mandarin distinctions, slang, euphemisms, and code-switching.
- Test direct and indirect expressions of self-harm, abuse, burnout, and depression.
- Use calm acknowledgment without simulated intimacy or claims such as “I know exactly how you feel.”

### Family and institutional trust

- Use trusted-person language.
- Do not automatically disclose adult users’ information to parents or spouses.
- Offer family education without revealing user data.
- Require technical and contractual separation for employer and university deployments.
- Test shared-home, dormitory, workplace, and public-transit privacy conditions.

### Crisis safeguards

- Country-specific emergency and crisis resources.
- Copyable messages and text/chat options where available.
- No default escalation to parents, employers, schools, or authorities.
- Separate testing for indirect language, slang, and code-switching.
- Local validation required for mainland China and other markets with distinct platform, data-hosting, content, or licensing conditions.

### Priority channels

Universities, primary care, sleep-health providers, mental-health nonprofits, privacy-governed employers, telecoms, and qualified clinicians or educators.

### Validation priorities

- Guide versus coach versus companion
- Structured versus free-form check-ins
- Practical versus emotional prompts
- Disclosure after explicit deletion demonstrations
- Text versus voice usefulness and privacy
- Employer/school privacy comprehension
- False escalation for indirect crisis language

## B. Middle East

**Scope note:** Gulf, Levantine, North African, and other markets require separate assessments of dialect, religion, gender norms, conflict exposure, service availability, migration, and legal conditions.

### Positioning

**Launch hypothesis:**

> “A private tool for steadiness, reflection, sleep, stress, relationships, and finding appropriate human support.”

Offer three user-selected modes:

1. Practical support  
2. Reflective emotional support  
3. Faith-compatible reflection  

The default must remain neutral. Religion, gender, family safety, and values must not be inferred from geography, name, or language.

### Feature adaptation

- Modern Standard Arabic plus relevant dialect testing
- English and additional migrant-worker languages where needed
- Discreet app mode and notification controls
- Text, voice, and low-literacy alternatives
- Grief, displacement, migration, caregiving, financial stress, and trauma-informed modules
- Silent safety planning for users unable to speak safely
- Referral filters for language, gender preference, cost, remote access, and geography
- Faith-compatible material only through reviewed, versioned content

### Language, culture, and religious safeguards

- Test Gulf, Egyptian, Levantine, Maghrebi, and other relevant varieties.
- Review gendered grammar, politeness, trauma vocabulary, suicide euphemisms, and code-switching.
- Do not imply that distress reflects weak faith, sin, insufficient gratitude, or failure of family duty.
- Do not claim religious authority.
- Religious content should initially use approved retrieval content rather than unrestricted generation.
- Include nonreligious users, religious minorities, and people with religious trauma in review.

### Family and gender safety

- Use trusted person rather than family by default.
- Ask whether sharing could cause punishment, violence, outing, loss of housing, or employment.
- Create a separate domestic-violence and coercive-control pathway.
- Do not automatically recommend a spouse, parent, police, employer, sponsor, or elder.
- Do not encourage voice use where speaking may be unsafe.

### Crisis safeguards

- Provide discreet, text-based, and chat options where available.
- Verify emergency, domestic-violence, child-safety, conflict, displacement, and migrant-support resources country by country.
- Clearly state whether live human review exists, its hours, languages, and response times.
- Do not launch employer-sponsored services without verifiable privacy separation.

### Priority channels

Hospitals, primary care, universities, women’s health organizations, migrant and humanitarian organizations, public-health bodies, qualified clinicians, and carefully governed community or faith partnerships.

### Validation priorities

- Neutral versus practical versus faith-compatible positioning
- Dialect and Modern Standard Arabic safety accuracy
- Shared-device and unsafe-household scenarios
- Silent versus voice workflows
- Trusted-person safety screening
- Crisis responses where police or family contact could increase danger
- Privacy comprehension before sensitive disclosure

## C. Latin America

**Scope note:** Brazil must be treated as a separate Portuguese-language market. Spanish-speaking countries also require country-level language, legal, connectivity, and service assessments.

### Positioning

**Launch hypothesis:**

> “A judgment-free tool to organize your thoughts, manage everyday pressure, take the next step, and find human support.”

Let users choose among:

- Vent or reflect
- Understand what is happening
- Solve a practical problem
- Calm down
- Prepare for a conversation
- Find affordable professional support

### Feature adaptation

- Country-specific Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese
- Low-data and intermittent-connectivity modes
- Voice notes and speech input only after accent, privacy, and recording tests
- Messaging-platform access only with separate consent and clear third-party data disclosures
- Modules for financial stress, caregiving, parenting, migration, grief, relationships, and violence-related fear
- Referral filters for public, low-cost, remote, women-only, LGBTQ+-affirming, and language-matched care
- Conversation preparation for family, clinicians, employers, and community health workers
- Moderated community resources, not open AI-moderated emotional groups

### Language and tone

- Test regional Spanish, Brazilian Portuguese, tú/usted variants, slang, gender-inclusive language, and terms for panic, depression, therapy, and suicide.
- Do not assume warmth, humor, emojis, or familial language are universally preferred.
- Avoid trivializing grief, trauma, violence, or poverty.
- Work with indigenous and Afro-descendant organizations where the product claims inclusion.

### Family and community

- Offer trusted-person scripts, accompaniment planning, shared goals, and community-health-worker pathways.
- Ask whether family involvement could cause violence, control, rejection, or outing.
- Provide private modes for domestic violence and LGBTQ+ safety.
- Employer and insurer programs must prohibit individual access and consequential use.

### Crisis safeguards

- Country-specific emergency, crisis, domestic-violence, child-safety, rural, and low-cost resources.
- Do not recommend confrontation with an abusive person.
- Do not default to family, police, employers, or religious leaders.
- Provide concise, copyable help messages.
- Test violence, migration, crime exposure, and connectivity-loss scenarios.

### Priority channels

Primary care, public-health agencies, universities, NGOs, psychologists, community health workers, telecoms, and carefully governed employer or creator partnerships.

### Validation priorities

- Listening versus problem-solving modes
- Warmth levels and dependency risk
- App, web, messaging, and voice channels
- Data use on low-end devices
- Spanish variants versus Brazilian Portuguese
- Voice accuracy by accent and background noise
- Violence and coercive-control flows
- Referral completion by affordability, geography, and identity-affirming availability

## D. Northern Europe

**Scope note:** Nordic countries, the UK and Ireland if included, the Baltics, and other markets require separate health-system, language, procurement, accessibility, and regulatory review.

### Positioning

**Launch hypothesis:**

> “A transparent, evidence-informed tool for everyday wellbeing, practical self-help, and navigating human care.”

Test “guide,” “coach,” and “companion.” Combine competence and warmth without implying human feelings or clinical authority.

### Feature adaptation

- General self-help exercises with plain-language explanations
- Care navigation and appointment-preparation tools
- Public-service signposting where permitted
- Strong screen-reader, caption, contrast, text-size, keyboard, and cognitive-accessibility support
- Multilingual support for migrant and minority communities
- Seasonal, sleep, isolation, caregiver, and climate-distress modules
- Opt-in wearable integrations with clear inference controls
- User-controlled memory, notifications, and sharing

### Language and social context

- Provide native-language experiences, not English-first translation.
- Test Nordic, Baltic, English, Irish, indigenous, and major migrant languages as relevant.
- Include plain-language and neurodiversity terminology.
- Acknowledge structural contributors such as housing, workload, discrimination, caregiving, isolation, and climate anxiety.
- Do not use “resilience” messaging to individualize social problems.

### Privacy and institutional trust

- Complete GDPR, national health-data, consumer, accessibility, AI, and possible medical-device assessments.
- Explain automated processing, human review, data rights, retention, portability, correction, and complaints.
- Public-sector, employer, and union deployments must use aggregate reporting only.
- Wearables and health-platform connections must be opt-in.

### Crisis safeguards

- Country-specific emergency, crisis, abuse, child-safety, and non-emergency pathways.
- Distinguish general distress, professional-care needs, immediate danger, harm to others, and abuse.
- Do not present risk scores as diagnoses or guarantees.
- Provide non-police and non-family options where appropriate.
- Maintain current local resources and clear human-review disclosures.

### Priority channels

Public-health systems, municipalities, primary care, occupational health, universities, charities, employers and unions with privacy firewalls, and public procurement pilots.

### Validation priorities

- Guide, coach, and companion labels
- Adjustable warmth versus neutral competence
- AI, evidence, privacy, and data-rights comprehension
- Trust by public, employer, clinician, and direct channels
- Accessibility across age and disability
- Minority, migrant, indigenous, and neurodivergent language performance
- Human-like avatar discomfort and dependency
- Public-service referral effectiveness

# 4. Crisis and safeguarding operating model

A version-controlled safety layer must cover:

- Suicide and self-harm
- Harm to others
- Domestic violence and coercive control
- Child safety
- Psychosis and mania
- Overdose, withdrawal, and medication-related emergencies
- Acute trauma and grief

For potentially urgent disclosures, the system should:

1. Acknowledge calmly.
2. Ask only necessary, direct questions.
3. Explain that it cannot reliably assess or ensure safety.
4. Provide verified local emergency and crisis options.
5. Offer text, chat, hospital, or trusted-person options where available.
6. Check whether contacting a person or authority is safe.
7. Provide a copyable help message.
8. Explain human-review availability and limits.
9. Avoid lengthy exercises or false reassurance.
10. Continue supporting the user’s next safe step without claiming emergency intervention.

### Human review must specify

- Staff qualifications
- Languages and operating hours
- Trigger conditions
- Response-time targets
- Information accessible to reviewers
- Whether users can opt out where legally permissible
- Whether reviewers can contact anyone
- Safeguarding and audit procedures

**The model must not autonomously contact a third party.** Any human-initiated safeguarding action must follow a documented, disclosed, legally reviewed, and operationally staffed process.

### Resource governance

Each country requires:

- A named resource owner
- Verification of phone, SMS, chat, cost, hours, language, eligibility, and geography
- Defined update frequency
- User reporting of incorrect resources
- A kill switch for dangerous or outdated referrals
- A fallback when services are closed or unavailable

# 5. Launch sequencing and readiness

## Phase 0: Governance and research — Q1–Q2 2026

- Select a deliberately small first wave, subject to readiness rather than regional coverage targets.
- Complete country-specific legal, privacy, security, child-safety, health-claim, and cross-border assessments.
- Establish local clinical, safeguarding, accessibility, lived-experience, and community advisory groups.
- Map crisis, domestic-violence, child-safety, and professional resources.
- Build multilingual safety glossaries and red-team datasets.
- Define permitted content, prohibited claims, human-review operations, and institutional controls.

## Phase 1: Restricted pilots — Q2–Q3 2026

Begin with a low-risk feature set:

- User-initiated reflection
- General psychoeducation
- Basic grounding, breathing, sleep, and routine exercises
- Appointment-preparation templates
- Verified care directories

Initially exclude or restrict, unless separately validated:

- Minors
- Open-ended high-intensity emotional conversation
- Automated risk scoring presented to users
- Voice
- Messaging-platform integrations
- Memory and extensive personalization
- Wearables
- Institutional deployments
- Generated religious advice

Use diverse recruitment through trusted institutions. Keep research instrumentation separate from production data. Provide defined human safety review during pilot hours.

## Phase 2: Controlled expansion — Q3–Q4 2026

Add countries and features only after readiness gates are met. Introduce employer, university, public-sector, clinician, voice, faith-compatible, or messaging channels only after separate safety and privacy review.

Publish:

- Data-flow and retention documentation
- Safety limitations
- Human-review model
- Resource-update process
- Incident-reporting mechanism
- Institutional data-separation commitments

## Phase 3: Continuous assurance — 2027 onward

- Quarterly independent safety, security, accessibility, and equity audits
- Continuous crisis-resource verification
- Version-controlled model and content changes
- Rapid correction and user notification for material safety issues
- Removal of features that increase dependency, privacy risk, or inequitable performance

# 6. Country go/no-go gate

A country may launch broadly only when it has:

1. Local legal and regulatory approval
2. Completed privacy and cross-border data assessment
3. Native-language and dialect safety validation
4. Verified crisis, emergency, abuse, child-safety, and referral resources
5. Operational age and vulnerable-user safeguards
6. Tested domestic-violence and coercive-control flows
7. End-to-end consent, deletion, export, and sharing revocation
8. Technical and contractual institutional data separation
9. Defined human-review operations, if offered
10. Accessibility validation
11. Disaggregated safety and quality results
12. Demonstrated AI, privacy, and emergency-boundary comprehension
13. Approved local marketing and procurement claims
14. Incident-response drills and resource-update ownership
15. Independent clinical, safeguarding, lived-experience, security, and community sign-off

If these conditions are not met, launch only a restricted low-risk feature set—or defer the country.

# 7. KPIs and measurement standards

Metrics must be defined in advance by feature, country, language, and user group. Results should include confidence intervals, human adjudication, severity weighting, and minimum sample sizes.

## Safety

- Unsafe-response rate
- False-reassurance rate
- Crisis under-escalation and harmful over-escalation
- Domestic-violence referral error
- Child-safety handling accuracy
- Harmful content correction time
- Human-review response time and quality
- Severity-weighted incidents
- Resource accuracy and freshness

## Trust and comprehension

- Correct AI identification
- Correct understanding that the product is not therapy or emergency care
- Privacy and retention comprehension
- Successful deletion and export
- Trusted-person revocation success
- Perceived surveillance
- Reported autonomy
- Human-like language discomfort
- Dependency or exclusivity indicators

Dependency monitoring must use the least sensitive data necessary and must not create an unnecessary psychological profile.

## Access and equity

- Activation and completion by language, age, gender, disability, geography, and device
- Accessibility task completion
- Voice error by accent and background noise
- Data consumed per session
- Low-end-device success
- Referral availability by cost, language, gender preference, and location
- Performance gaps across major user groups

## Usefulness and wellbeing

- Coping self-efficacy
- Usefulness of exercises
- Ability to identify a next step
- Connection to appropriate human support
- Self-reported stress, sleep, or wellbeing changes only with informed consent
- Negative effects, including shame, rumination, avoidance of care, increased anxiety, and dependency

Engagement, streaks, disclosure volume, and session length must not be primary success metrics.

## Commercial integrity

- Institutional contracts with enforceable separation clauses
- Unauthorized access attempts
- Marketing-claim violations
- Consent-withdrawal and cancellation success
- Retention without manipulative reminders
- Safety incidents attributable to growth or channel experiments

---

# 8. Issues requiring explicit local validation

The following must not be treated as settled by the global strategy:

- Whether users prefer formal, casual, warm, neutral, or highly direct language
- Whether “companion,” “coach,” or “guide” builds trust
- Whether AI increases disclosure relative to human support
- Whether practical or emotional entry points are more acceptable
- Whether voice is safer or more accessible than text
- Whether messaging-platform integration is acceptable
- Whether family or other trusted people improve outcomes
- Whether spiritual content is desired or harmful
- Whether employer, university, public-sector, or clinician distribution increases trust
- Whether local care directories are accurate and reachable
- Whether the product’s exercises, care-navigation functions, or claims trigger clinical or medical-device regulation
- Whether minors can be safely supported in the first release
- Whether infrastructure, licensing, data-hosting, or content requirements permit a truthful and safe deployment

Required validation partners include local legal counsel, clinicians, crisis and safeguarding organizations, accessibility specialists, linguists, community organizations, relevant faith and secular advisors, security experts, and people with lived experience.

---

## Final recommendation

Proceed with a **small, country-gated 2026 launch** built on global safety and privacy invariants, with locally configurable language, tone, support modes, referral pathways, accessibility, and community involvement.

The companion should help people take an appropriate next step—reflection, coping, conversation, or professional support—while remaining explicit that **AI emotional-wellbeing support does not replace professional care or emergency services**.

The governing principle is:

> **Maximize informed, safe, useful support and appropriate connection to people—not emotional dependence, maximum disclosure, or time spent with the AI.**
