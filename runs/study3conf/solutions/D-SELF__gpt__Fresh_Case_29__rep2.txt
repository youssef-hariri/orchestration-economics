# Semiconductor Specialty Materials Resilience Strategy, 2026–2029

## Executive recommendation

Implement a **qualification-led, risk-tiered dual-sourcing program** for specialty gases and photoresists.

The objective is not to appoint two suppliers for every material. It is to ensure that critical production demand has an alternate source that is:

- Qualified for the relevant material grade, package, fab, tool, process, and product family
- Customer-approved where required
- Legally accessible under export-control, sanctions, and re-export rules
- Operationally deliverable through compatible equipment, permits, and logistics
- Supported by sufficient conforming capacity within the required recovery window

Because qualification commonly takes 12–24 months—and may take longer for EUV resists, reactive electronic gases, new delivery systems, facility modifications, and customer-controlled products—the company should begin immediately while protecting the interim period.

The strategy should combine:

1. Risk-based material prioritization
2. Immediate alternative-source qualification
3. Targeted inventory and supplier-held stock
4. Staged capacity commitments
5. Controlled production allocation after release
6. Upstream and geographic diversification
7. Explicit financial limits, triggers, and exceptions

The primary resilience metric should be:

> **Percentage of Tier 1 production demand covered by a conforming, customer-approved, legally accessible alternate that can supply within the required recovery window.**

Supplier count alone should not be treated as resilience.

## 1. Assumptions requiring validation

The following assumptions should be validated within 30 days and maintained in an owned assumptions register:

- Alternative qualification generally requires 12–24 months, but timing varies by chemistry, product, node, fab, tool, facility, and customer approval.
- Datasheet compliance does not establish process equivalence.
- Export controls may affect finished materials, precursors, purification equipment, technical data, ownership, re-export routes, and transportation.
- Some advanced materials may remain technically sole-source.
- Demand is cyclical, creating risk under fixed-volume commitments.
- Photoresists are constrained by shelf life, temperature control, lot specificity, and obsolescence.
- Specialty gases may be constrained by purification, fill capacity, cylinders, valves, transport, permits, gas cabinets, point-of-use systems, and abatement.
- Internal engineering, metrology, reliability, facilities, EHS, and customer resources limit the number of qualifications that can proceed concurrently.

Each assumption should have an owner, validation method, due date, confidence rating, impact if false, and contingency.

## 2. Material prioritization

### Risk criteria

Score each material and use case against:

| Criterion | Considerations |
|---|---|
| Production consequence | Could a shortage stop a fab, tool, product family, or critical layer? |
| Geopolitical and export exposure | Are the material, precursor, technology, or route subject to controls or sanctions? |
| Qualification lead time | How long would technical, facility, reliability, and customer release take? |
| Substitutability | Is there a validated recipe or credible process alternative? |
| Quality sensitivity | Could variation affect yield, CD, defectivity, contamination, electrical performance, or reliability? |
| Supply concentration | Are production, purification, precursors, packaging, ownership, or logistics concentrated? |
| Recovery time | How long would it take to restore conforming supply? |
| Inventory feasibility | Can safe, in-date, accessible inventory be held? |
| Demand confidence | Can the company support a commitment through a downturn? |
| Execution feasibility | Are sufficient tool, metrology, engineering, and customer resources available? |

A material should be treated as Tier 1 regardless of score if a shortage could stop production, qualification is expected to exceed 18 months, active export-control uncertainty exists, no alternate recipe is validated, or nominally separate sources share a material common-mode dependency.

### Risk tiers

#### Tier 1 — Fab-stopping or strategically exposed

Typical examples include critical electronic gases, gases without validated substitute recipes, EUV and critical-layer photoresists, and materials subject to active export-control risk.

**Required posture:**

- Two production-released sources where technically and legally feasible
- Independent upstream and jurisdictional exposure where practical
- Tested emergency supply and changeover capability
- Targeted usable inventory or supplier-held stock
- Contractually protected capacity
- Executive-approved exception where a second source is infeasible

#### Tier 2 — Important but partially substitutable

Examples include mature-node resists, materials released for selected products, or materials with broader process windows.

**Required posture:**

- Alternate source for priority products
- Recurring volume sufficient to maintain readiness
- Appropriate usable inventory
- Flexible reservations or minimum-purchase arrangements

#### Tier 3 — Readily substitutable or lower consequence

Use normal competitive sourcing and inventory practices. Strategic reservations require a separate business case.

## 3. Definition of resilience coverage

A source receives production-resilience credit only at the level of:

> **Material grade × package or delivery mode × fab × tool/process × product family × customer approval**

For each source, record:

- Manufacturing and filling sites
- Approved grade and package
- Fab, tool, and process eligibility
- Fab, internal product, and customer-release status
- Qualified, reserved, and shipment-ready capacity
- Emergency ramp time
- Shelf life and storage requirements
- Export-control status
- Upstream dependency confidence

Classify upstream independence as:

1. **Verified independent**
2. **Supplier-certified but not independently verified**
3. **Partially mapped**
4. **Unknown**

Unknown dependencies should receive no diversification credit. Supplier-certified or partially mapped dependencies should receive only partial credit and carry a residual-risk penalty.

Capacity should be classified separately as:

- Installed capacity
- Raw-material-backed capacity
- Qualified capacity
- Contractually reserved capacity
- Shipment-ready conforming inventory
- Capacity requiring requalification

Only qualified, legally accessible, and operationally deliverable capacity should receive full resilience credit.

## 4. Qualification and quality protection

### Qualification gates

#### Gate 0: Feasibility, compliance, and diligence

- Review technical capability, quality systems, financial health, and realistic capacity.
- Map precursors, purification, packaging, ownership, subcontractors, logistics, and export jurisdictions.
- Obtain a written export-control and country-of-origin assessment.
- Define material- and product-specific critical-to-quality requirements.
- Confirm customer approval requirements.
- Reserve internal tool, metrology, facilities, EHS, and reliability resources.

**Exit:** credible supplier, viable legal supply path, realistic capacity, approved business case, named owner, and integrated schedule.

#### Gate 1: Analytical and package qualification

For gases:

- Full impurity fingerprint, including moisture, oxygen, hydrocarbons, metals, and particles
- Cylinder, valve, regulator, and delivery-system compatibility
- Stability and transport testing
- Mixture concentration, homogeneity, and storage stability

For photoresists:

- Polymer and formulation characteristics
- Viscosity, solids, film thickness, sensitivity, and contrast
- Defectivity, filtration, outgassing, and shelf life
- Bottle, liner, dispense, and temperature-excursion compatibility
- Batch-to-batch and lot-to-lot capability

#### Gate 2: Tool, facility, and process compatibility

- Gas cabinets, regulators, filters, purifiers, manifolds, chambers, exhaust, and abatement
- Coater, developer, filtration, and dispense compatibility
- EHS, fire, waste, permit, and safety-system approvals
- Chamber seasoning, memory effects, contamination, and recovery
- Tool-to-tool and lot-to-lot performance
- MES, barcode, traceability, operator training, and incoming inspection
- Metrology capability to detect relevant differences

#### Gate 3: Controlled pilot production

- Engineering lots and monitor wafers
- Split lots on mature or lower-risk products where possible
- Multiple material lots, tools, shifts, containers, and transport cycles
- Yield, defectivity, CD, process-window, electrical, reliability, and downstream-process testing
- Defined lot-hold, containment, rollback, and contamination-recovery procedures

#### Gate 4: Production and customer release

- Release by product family, fab, tool, and delivery mode
- Customer approval where required
- Reliability and electrical qualification
- Final quality, allocation, and change-control agreements
- Emergency ramp demonstration
- Replenishment and logistics validation

A supplier should not receive full coverage credit until it is released for the applicable production demand.

### Statistical and evidence requirements

Each qualification plan should specify:

- Required batches, lots, containers, tools, shifts, and wafers
- Primary and secondary quality characteristics
- Equivalence or non-inferiority margins
- Confidence level and statistical power
- Sample-size basis and randomization
- Outlier and missing-data treatment
- Process-capability requirements
- Escalation, containment, and rollback criteria
- Post-release monitoring duration and exit criteria

There should be no universal yield-loss threshold. Acceptance limits must be product-, node-, layer-, customer-, and reliability-specific.

### Material-specific controls

**Specialty gases:** Assess the full chain from precursor and purification through filling, container fleet, valves, transportation, gas cabinets, point-of-use purification, and abatement. A second supplier is not useful if the fab cannot safely receive or switch to its delivery system.

**Photoresists:** Treat EUV, ArF, KrF, thick-film, lift-off, image-reversal, and other specialty resists as distinct categories. Evaluate CD, stochastic defects, roughness, defectivity, outgassing, bake and developer response, filtration, etch transfer, packaging, shelf life, and lot genealogy.

### Allocation policy

Allocation should follow technical maturity:

1. **Pre-release:** incumbent supplies production; alternate supplies development material.
2. **Pilot:** controlled split lots on approved products and tools.
3. **Initial release:** limited recurring volume sufficient to validate stability.
4. **Stable dual source:** expand allocation as quality, capacity, and customer release mature.
5. **Crisis posture:** shift additional qualified volume only after confirming legal access, conforming capacity, equipment readiness, and contamination controls.

Do not impose an arbitrary alternate allocation percentage on sensitive processes.

## 5. Capacity and contract economics

### Staged commercial model

#### Phase 1: Development agreement

- Small engineering and sample quantities
- Capped development fees
- Defined technical milestones
- Supplier development-capacity commitment
- No large purchase obligation
- Buyer right to stop after failed gates

#### Phase 2: Conditional capacity reservation

Begin only after technical feasibility and legal accessibility are demonstrated.

- Reserve capacity by grade, site, package, and period
- Use a modest fee, preferably creditable against future purchases
- Link payment to technical and capacity milestones
- Require evidence of equipment, staffing, raw-material access, and reserved capacity
- Include reduction or termination rights for failed qualification, regulatory restrictions, or supplier underperformance

#### Phase 3: Production-release agreement

Use three volume layers:

- **Firm base:** minimum volume needed to preserve credible supplier economics
- **Forecast volume:** planning information with limited binding effect
- **Optional surge volume:** additional capacity available under defined notice and ramp conditions

The firm base should be calculated using:

- Supplier minimum efficient operating volume
- Qualified demand
- Conservative demand forecast
- Emergency ramp requirements
- Downside utilization
- Buyer exposure limits
- Required supplier investment

It should not default to a fixed percentage of forecast demand.

Include:

- Volume-flexibility bands
- Rolling forecasts
- Make-up rights
- Priority allocation during shortages
- Price benefits tied to actual purchases
- Step-down rights
- Reopeners for export controls, demand changes, quality events, and product transitions

#### Phase 4: Strategic agreement

Use only for selected Tier 1 materials:

- Three- to five-year term, subject to product-life review
- Dedicated or priority capacity
- Supplier-held emergency inventory
- Joint capacity planning
- Defined response and recovery times
- Audit and continuity rights
- Buyer-funded asset protections
- Transfer, termination, and transition assistance

### Required contract service levels

Tier 1 contracts should define:

- Monthly capacity by grade, site, package, and delivery mode
- Minimum conforming quantity
- Standard and emergency lead times
- Emergency ramp quantity and rate
- Allocation share during shortages
- Supplier-held inventory
- Raw-material coverage
- Maximum recovery time
- Lot-acceptance and quality requirements
- Corrective-action response time
- Change-notice periods
- Remedies for missed commitments or unauthorized changes

“Reserved capacity” must mean physically and legally accessible capacity, not a nonbinding forecast.

### Financing mechanisms

Consider:

- Supplier-owned or consigned inventory
- Vendor-managed inventory
- Reservation fees credited against purchases
- Milestone-based prepayments
- Supply-chain finance
- Parent guarantees
- Equipment leasing
- Shared investment with anchor customers
- Government incentives, treated as upside unless approved and documented

Buyer-funded equipment requires clear title, access, insurance, maintenance, default, transfer, and repayment protections.

### Financial decision rule

For each mitigation option, calculate:

\[
\text{Net resilience value}
=
\text{avoided disruption and recovery cost}
-
\text{qualification cost}
-
\text{reservation and price premium}
-
\text{inventory cost}
-
\text{expiry, obsolescence, and unused-volume exposure}
\]

Include qualification success probability, supplier failure probability, correlated disruption scenarios, lost incumbent rebates, cash-flow timing, and salvage value.

Finance should establish approval thresholds for qualification spending, reservation fees, minimum-purchase liability, buyer-funded equipment, inventory carrying cost, contract duration, maximum unused-volume exposure, and maximum cash or EBITDA exposure.

## 6. Inventory and continuity protection

### Usable inventory

Measure:

\[
\text{Usable days}
=
\frac{\text{in-date accessible inventory} \times \text{approved allocation}}
{\text{validated daily consumption}}
\]

Exclude inventory that is inaccessible due to export or customs restrictions, unqualified, outside storage limits, allocated elsewhere, or incompatible with fab equipment.

### Specialty gases

Use:

- On-site emergency stock within safety limits
- Supplier-held regional cylinder pools
- Independent logistics routes
- Emergency carriers and customs procedures
- Validated alternate cylinders, valves, regulators, and gas cabinets
- Backup bulk connections where feasible
- Point-of-use purification where validated
- EHS, permit, abatement, and emergency-response readiness

### Photoresists

Use:

- Multiple fresh lots rather than excessive aged stock
- Supplier-managed rotation
- Regional temperature-controlled stock
- FEFO controls
- Expiry replacement obligations
- Temperature-excursion monitoring
- Product-specific allocation
- Obsolescence limits

For short-life or highly product-specific resists, supplier-held capacity and rapid qualification are generally preferable to large on-site inventories.

### Continuity playbooks

Each Tier 1 material should have a playbook covering:

- Trigger conditions and decision rights
- Usable inventory and coverage
- Product and customer prioritization
- Alternate activation
- Approved process adjustments
- Gas or chemical changeover
- Lot containment and rollback
- Contamination recovery
- Regulatory escalation
- Customer communication
- Recovery milestones

Conduct annual tabletop exercises and emergency-ramp tests by material class.

## 7. Export-control and geographic diversification

The General Counsel or Chief Compliance Officer should own a formal control plan covering:

- Monthly monitoring of relevant jurisdictions and regulations
- Supplier and parent-company ownership screening
- Export classifications and license records
- Re-export and transit-route analysis
- Supplier certification and periodic recertification
- License lead-time tracking
- Restricted-party screening
- Shipment-hold authority
- Regulatory-change impact assessment
- Record retention
- Written independence determinations for Tier 1 alternates

A source receives geographic-diversification credit only after legal review confirms meaningful independence in manufacturing, precursors, technology, ownership, jurisdiction, and shipment routes.

Regional packaging, blending, or filling may improve logistics resilience but should not be assumed to eliminate export-control or country-of-origin exposure.

## 8. Phased implementation

### 0–30 days: Mobilize and expose risk

Deliver:

- Material Resilience Council and named Tier 1 owners
- Critical-material register and risk scores
- Supplier, country, precursor, purification, packaging, and logistics map
- Usable-inventory baseline
- Contract and renewal review
- Export-control assessment
- Initial supplier-capacity validation
- Qualification budget
- Interim allocation and emergency-logistics actions
- Internal engineering, metrology, facilities, and customer-capacity plan

### 31–90 days: Launch the portfolio

Deliver:

- Alternate candidate for each technically feasible Tier 1 material
- Weighted supplier scorecard and disqualifiers
- Gate 0 reviews
- Qualification evidence plans
- Product-specific acceptance criteria
- Development agreements
- Continuity playbooks
- Inventory and supplier-held-stock decisions
- Customer-approval plans
- Facility-readiness plans
- Initial financial cases

### 3–12 months: Build technical and commercial options

Deliver:

- Analytical and package qualification results
- Tool, facility, EHS, metrology, and abatement readiness
- Pilot-production schedules
- Conditional reservations for candidates passing technical and legal gates
- Quality and change-control agreements
- Initial pilot data
- Tabletop exercise reports
- Updated economic cases where cost or schedule changes

### 12–24 months: Release and exercise alternates

Deliver:

- Product-family and customer approvals
- Controlled production ramps
- Post-release monitoring
- Emergency-ramp demonstrations
- Flexible production agreements
- Updated usable-inventory targets
- Recalculated common-mode and geographic exposure
- Formal sole-source exception decisions where required

### Beyond 24 months: Institutionalize

- Extend qualification to advanced products where justified.
- Develop regional purification, formulation, or finishing only where it creates genuine legal and operational independence.
- Embed alternate-source requirements in new products, process transfers, and fab design.
- Review contracts, inventory, supplier concentration, and economics annually.
- Reduce or terminate commitments when risk, demand, or material relevance changes.

## 9. Governance and triggers

### Governance

Create a **Material Resilience Council** chaired by the COO or Chief Supply Chain Officer.

| Responsibility | Accountable owner |
|---|---|
| Overall program and escalation | COO / Chief Supply Chain Officer |
| Supplier strategy and contracts | Chief Procurement Officer |
| Qualification and process equivalence | CTO / Engineering |
| Production release and quality | Operations and Quality |
| Inventory and logistics | Supply Chain |
| Financial commitments | CFO |
| Export controls and legal exposure | General Counsel / Compliance |
| Customer approval and prioritization | Sales and S&OP |
| Material-level execution | Named Tier 1 owner |

The Council should meet monthly for execution and quarterly for executive review. Single-source exceptions should expire after 12 months unless renewed using updated evidence.

### Decision triggers

| Trigger | Required response |
|---|---|
| License approval time exceeds historical baseline by 30% for two applications | Increase accessible stock; escalate compliance review; protect alternate capacity |
| License expiry is within 120 days without renewal evidence | Executive review and contingency allocation |
| Supplier lead time is 20% above contract for two periods | Review allocation, logistics, and reservation activation |
| Supplier utilization exceeds 85–90% | Exercise optional capacity or shift incremental demand |
| Usable inventory falls below recovery lead time | Start continuity response and product prioritization |
| Qualification is 31–90 days late | Executive resource and schedule review |
| Qualification is more than 90 days late | Reassess inventory, reservations, resources, and candidate viability |
| Qualification is more than 180 days late or fails a critical gate | Re-scope, reselect, or renew sole-source exception |
| Alternate fails equivalence criteria | Stop ramp, contain affected lots, and reassess source or process |
| Quality excursions exceed approved limits | Freeze expansion; initiate corrective action; protect incumbent supply |
| Supplier or parent becomes restricted or changes ownership materially | Legal review; remove affected capacity from coverage |
| Common precursor or country restriction emerges | Re-score all nominally independent sources |
| Demand falls 20–30% below commitment forecast | Use flexibility and make-up rights; renegotiate exposure |
| Supplier financial health breaches defined thresholds | Secure stock, technical data, equipment rights, and transition options |

Each trigger should have a data source, monitoring frequency, decision owner, action deadline, and exit criterion.

During a live disruption:

- Notify the response team the same day
- Complete cross-functional assessment within 24 hours
- Make executive decisions within 72 hours
- Track daily until stabilized
- Use a preassigned crisis commander and deputy

## 10. KPIs

### Leading indicators

- Qualification milestone adherence
- Days of unmitigated Tier 1 exposure
- License approval lead time
- Supplier utilization
- Qualified and reserved capacity versus conservative demand
- Usable and accessible inventory days
- Inventory expiry exposure
- Facility-readiness completion
- Customer-approval progress
- Change-notice compliance
- Upstream dependency-mapping confidence
- Internal tool, metrology, and engineering capacity utilization

### Lagging indicators

- Percentage of Tier 1 demand covered by a second production-released source
- Percentage covered by verified independent upstream and export exposure
- Time to produce conforming alternate material
- Emergency-ramp performance by readiness class
- Lot-acceptance rate
- Yield, defectivity, CD, electrical, and reliability delta versus incumbent
- Material-related excursions
- Corrective-action closure time
- Conforming-material lead time
- Supplier OTIF
- Production-days-at-risk
- Expired or obsolete strategic inventory
- Take-or-pay utilization
- Net resilience value

Emergency readiness should be reported separately as:

- Shipment-ready conforming inventory
- Qualified capacity requiring normal replenishment
- Qualified capacity requiring ramp
- Capacity requiring requalification
- Unqualified development capacity

Targets should be set after the 30-day baseline, but universal targets such as two suppliers for every material, 45 days of inventory, or a fixed yield-loss tolerance should not be imposed across all materials.

---

## Final recommendation

Launch the program immediately, but do not execute binding strategic capacity agreements or major buyer-funded investments without:

1. A completed material risk score and Tier 1 designation
2. Gate 0 technical and legal approval
3. A material-specific qualification and statistical evidence plan
4. A validated supplier capacity certificate
5. A documented financial case within approved limits
6. Contractual service levels, allocation rights, change control, and remedies
7. An integrated qualification and facility schedule
8. A continuity playbook and inventory decision

The company should **qualify early, protect the interim period, commit progressively, and release cautiously**. The desired end state is a portfolio of genuinely independent, production-released alternatives supported by usable inventory, enforceable capacity, tested emergency procedures, and flexible economics—not simply a larger approved-supplier list.
