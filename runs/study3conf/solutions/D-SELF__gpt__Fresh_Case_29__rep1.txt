# Semiconductor Materials Resilience Strategy, 2026–2029

## 1. Executive recommendation

Adopt a **material-specific, risk-weighted dual-sourcing program**, not a universal 50/50 allocation.

For critical specialty gases, photoresists, and enabling inputs:

1. **Prioritize materials by consequence and replaceability**, including fab impact, yield sensitivity, qualification lead time, export-control exposure, upstream concentration, inventory feasibility, and financial exposure.
2. **Start alternate qualification immediately.** A 12–24-month qualification cycle is longer than the likely notice period for export controls, sanctions, logistics disruption, or supplier failure.
3. **Diversify the dependency network**, not merely supplier names or countries. Assess manufacturing sites, precursors, cylinders, packaging, laboratories, logistics, ownership, payment channels, and trade-control jurisdictions.
4. **Use layered protection:** qualified alternates, usable inventory, supplier-held stock, reserved capacity, validated logistics, and pre-approved production-allocation plans.
5. **Use staged commercial commitments**—routine alternate volume, reservation fees, surge rights, rollover, milestone-based step-ups, and shared supplier investment—to support supplier capacity without excessive take-or-pay exposure.
6. **Count a source as resilient only when it is continuity-ready:** technically qualified, production-approved, capacity-demonstrated, legally usable, contractually protected, traceable, and operationally activatable.
7. **Formally accept residual single-source risks** through executive risk acceptance with an owner, mitigation plan, trigger conditions, financial exposure, and review date.

> Fund the minimum combination of qualification, monitored alternate production, usable inventory, independent capacity, and contractual protection that keeps validated recovery time below the tolerable production-interruption window.

Equal supplier shares are not required. The objective is credible continuity at acceptable risk-adjusted cost without compromising quality, EHS, reliability, or customer requirements.

## 2. Material prioritization

Create a material-level risk register covering:

- Specialty and bulk process gases;
- Photoresists, developers, solvents, additives, and underlayers;
- Critical chemical precursors;
- Cylinders, valves, filters, bottles, and packaging;
- Purification, filling, testing, and toll-manufacturing services.

Score each material from 1–5 across:

| Dimension | Assessment question |
|---|---|
| Production impact | Would loss stop a fab, toolset, product, or critical layer? |
| Quality sensitivity | Could variation cause yield, reliability, defectivity, or contamination failures? |
| Substitutability | Is a qualified material or process alternative available? |
| Qualification lead time | How long are technical, reliability, customer, and ramp approvals? |
| Replenishment lead time | How quickly can usable replacement arrive? |
| Geopolitical exposure | Are country, technology, route, payment, or end-use controls relevant? |
| Upstream concentration | Are precursors, cylinders, valves, packaging, laboratories, or utilities single-sourced? |
| Inventory feasibility | Can released material be safely stored within shelf-life and permit limits? |
| Capacity risk | Can suppliers support normal, recovery, and surge requirements? |
| Financial exposure | What are qualification, reservation, inventory, expiry, and switching costs? |

Do not rank by purchase spend alone. A low-volume gas or resist can gate an entire process or product family.

### Risk segments

| Segment | Characteristics | Required response |
|---|---|---|
| **A1 — Fab-stop critical** | Shortage may stop a fab, toolset, or critical product | Immediate alternate qualification, protected inventory, reserved capacity, executive oversight |
| **A2 — Yield/reliability critical** | Variation or shortage may cause substantial yield or reliability loss | Multi-lot qualification, process-window validation, controlled ramp, enhanced monitoring |
| **B — Capacity-constraining** | Shortage reduces output but permits rationing or rescheduling | Flexible backup capacity, targeted inventory, substitution studies |
| **C — Manageable** | Multiple qualified sources or short replenishment | Standard sourcing and supplier-performance management |

Also identify **small-volume/high-consequence** materials separately.

## 3. True source independence

A supplier in another country is not automatically independent. For each priority material, map:

- Manufacturing site and legal entity;
- Parent ownership and control;
- Country and export-control jurisdiction;
- Critical precursors and feedstocks;
- Purification, formulation, and filling sites;
- Cylinders, valves, filters, bottles, and packaging;
- Quality-control and release laboratories;
- Utilities, energy, water, and environmental dependencies;
- Subcontractors and toll manufacturers;
- Ports, borders, logistics routes, insurance, and payment channels.

Assess concentration separately at:

1. Supplier-entity level;
2. Manufacturing-site level;
3. Country level;
4. Export-control-region level;
5. Critical-precursor level;
6. Packaging and cylinder level;
7. Logistics-route level.

A source receives full independence credit only when, where feasible, it has:

- A separate manufacturing site;
- A different export-control jurisdiction;
- No common critical single-source precursor;
- A viable independent logistics route;
- Separate release capability;
- Demonstrated capacity for the required grade, tool, layer, product, and recovery window.

A distributor, reseller, or regional warehouse does not count as a second source if the underlying manufacturing dependency is unchanged.

## 4. Sourcing and capacity architecture

Do not impose fixed splits such as 50/50 or 70/30. Define required capability by:

- Material grade;
- Tool and delivery configuration;
- Product and layer;
- Normal demand;
- Disruption duration;
- Recovery window;
- Required ramp rate.

Track separately:

- Normal purchase volume;
- Firm committed volume;
- Reserved but unused capacity;
- Qualified surge capacity;
- Inventory-supported capacity;
- Uncommitted market supply;
- Capacity dependent on common upstream inputs.

### A1 architecture

**Primary operating source**  
Supplies most normal demand and maintains process history and commercial efficiency.

**Secondary operating source**  
Receives enough routine volume to maintain lot history, process familiarity, supplier viability, and quality surveillance. It may begin at 5–20% of applicable demand and increase after production performance is demonstrated. It need not carry 50% of demand.

**Reserved surge capacity**  
Contractually available during disruption and demonstrated to produce and ship the correct grade, package, cylinder, and volume.

**Contingency layer**  
May include supplier-held stock, regional stock, a second candidate supplier, on-site generation or purification for selected gases, qualified process alternatives, or controlled production allocation.

Only capacity that is technically qualified, legally usable, physically available, and deliverable within the tested recovery window receives resilience credit.

## 5. Qualification and quality framework

Qualification is a manufacturing-capacity investment, not solely a procurement activity. Quality, reliability, contamination-control, and EHS requirements must not be relaxed to meet an emergency deadline. Speed should come from parallel execution and early resource reservation.

### Gate 1 — Supplier and facility screening

Review:

- Quality-management system and audit history;
- Process capability and contamination controls;
- EHS and regulatory compliance;
- Financial and business-continuity health;
- Export-control and sanctions exposure;
- Capacity and expansion plans;
- Sub-tier dependency map;
- Change-control maturity;
- Traceability and record-retention capability.

### Gate 2 — Material and packaging characterization

Compare candidate and incumbent for:

- Purity and trace impurities;
- Moisture, oxygen, metals, particles, hydrocarbons, and ionic contamination;
- Formulation composition and stability;
- Packaging, cylinder, valve, and closure systems;
- Shelf life and transport behavior;
- Analytical-method suitability;
- Within-lot and lot-to-lot variation.

### Gate 3 — Delivery-system and storage compatibility

#### Specialty gases

Assess:

- Cylinder material, treatment, inspection, and history;
- Valve, regulator, manifold, tubing, seal, and elastomer compatibility;
- Pressure, flow, depletion, adsorption, and desorption behavior;
- Leak performance and residual-gas controls;
- Gas cabinet, purge, exhaust, abatement, and emergency-shutdown compatibility;
- Cleaning, passivation, filling, and cylinder traceability;
- Cylinder fleet, return-cycle, and valve availability;
- Facilities permits and EHS readiness.

#### Photoresists

Assess:

- Container and dispense compatibility;
- Filtration and particle performance;
- Temperature and light controls;
- Cold-chain requirements;
- Storage and transport excursions;
- Opening, transfer, and contamination controls;
- Shelf-life behavior and expiration management;
- Compatibility with the intended coating, bake, exposure, and development sequence.

### Gate 4 — Process integration qualification

Use matched, randomized split-lot testing across relevant:

- Tools and chambers;
- Products and layers;
- Underlayers;
- Process conditions;
- Downstream integration steps.

For gases, evaluate delivery stability, chamber behavior, contamination, tool performance, abatement interaction, and wafer-level impact.

For photoresists, evaluate as applicable:

- Dose-to-size and contrast;
- Critical dimension and uniformity;
- Overlay and profile;
- Line-edge and line-width roughness;
- Defectivity, scumming, footing, collapse, and residue;
- Film thickness and uniformity;
- Bake sensitivity;
- Etch, clean, deposition, implant, and packaging interactions;
- Electrical and reliability performance.

A resist qualified for one layer, node, tool, product, or process condition must not be treated as a universal alternate.

### Gate 5 — Reliability and repeatability

Require a statistically justified set of lots and production campaigns covering relevant variation in:

- Raw-material lots;
- Formulation or purification batches;
- Reactors or filling lines;
- Packaging lots;
- Storage age;
- Transport conditions;
- Normal and boundary process conditions.

Acceptance criteria must be defined before testing and approved by process integration, quality, yield, reliability, and operations. Do not use a universal yield-delta target; criteria must reflect product criticality, failure mode, baseline variation, and statistical confidence.

### Gate 6 — Production approval

Required signoff:

- Process integration;
- Process-module engineering;
- Yield and defect engineering;
- Reliability;
- Quality;
- Fab operations;
- Facilities and EHS;
- Product or customer engineering where applicable.

### Gate 7 — Continuity readiness

A source becomes **Green / continuity-ready** only after demonstrating:

- Multi-lot technical and reliability acceptance;
- Approved release and change-control procedures;
- Normal and surge capacity;
- Packaging, cylinder, and logistics availability;
- Independent upstream supply;
- Executable commercial terms;
- Tested activation and allocation procedures.

## 6. Emergency-use policy

Emergency conditions do not authorize unqualified production use.

Only a **pre-qualified, use-case-specific alternate** may be activated through formal manufacturing change control.

If no qualified alternate exists, the response may be:

- Use of qualified inventory;
- Production rationing;
- Product, customer, node, or margin prioritization;
- Resequencing of starts;
- Controlled reduction of affected operations;
- Temporary shutdown of the affected process.

“Shadow production” should mean only:

- Supplier capability runs not used in the fab;
- Segregated engineering lots;
- Controlled production introductions under an approved engineering change or deviation.

Engineering-only material must be physically and electronically segregated from saleable inventory. Procurement cannot unilaterally activate it.

---

## 7. Supplier quality, change control, and traceability

Supplier quality agreements should require:

- Lot genealogy and retained samples;
- Supplier release testing and risk-based incoming verification;
- Statistical process-control data;
- Defined impurity, particle, and performance limits;
- Corrective-action timelines;
- Sub-tier disclosure for critical inputs;
- Audit rights through outsourced manufacturing, testing, filling, and warehousing;
- Recall, containment, and notification procedures;
- Evidence of business-continuity testing.

### Mandatory change-notification triggers

Require early written notification and, for major changes, customer approval before implementation for:

- Site transfers;
- Formulation, synthesis, purification, filtration, or filling changes;
- Raw-material or precursor changes;
- Equipment, reactor, filling-line, or cleaning changes;
- Analytical-method or specification changes;
- Cylinder, valve, packaging, or closure changes;
- Laboratory or release-site changes;
- Ownership, subcontracting, or toll-manufacturing changes;
- Capacity, allocation, or logistics changes;
- Contamination events, recalls, deviations, or out-of-specification results.

Notification timing must reflect qualification lead time; 90 days may be inadequate for critical gases or photoresists.

Traceability must link:

- Raw-material lots;
- Production, formulation, purification, and fill lots;
- Cylinder or container ID;
- Shipment and storage history;
- Tool, chamber, wafer, product, and customer lots;
- Laboratory release results;
- Change notices and deviations.

ERP, MES, laboratory, warehouse, and supplier identifiers should be interoperable. Spreadsheet-only traceability is inadequate for contamination containment.

## 8. Inventory and continuity coverage

Count only **released, usable, accessible, and correctly stored inventory**.

Define required recovery coverage as:

\[
T_{\text{required recovery}} =
T_{\text{notification}}
+
T_{\text{replenishment}}
+
T_{\text{qualification or activation}}
+
T_{\text{ramp}}
\]

Usable inventory is bounded by:

\[
I_{\text{usable}}=
\min[
D_{\text{daily}}\times T_{\text{required recovery}},
I_{\text{safe storage}},
D_{\text{daily}}\times T_{\text{usable shelf life}},
I_{\text{cylinder/packaging capacity}}
]
\]

Emergency supply should be treated as probability-weighted, scenario-specific coverage—not automatically deducted from the requirement. Uncommitted spot-market material receives little or no resilience credit.

Inventory does not count if it is:

- Expired;
- Quarantined;
- Stored outside approved conditions;
- Incompatible with available cylinders or delivery systems;
- Unreleased;
- Inaccessible because of ownership, permits, route, or handling restrictions.

### Gas-specific controls

Account for:

- Hazard classification and storage permits;
- Cylinder ownership, inspection, and return cycles;
- Valve and packaging availability;
- Gas stability and impurity growth;
- Gas cabinet, exhaust, abatement, and emergency-response capacity;
- Local filling and distribution capability;
- Potential on-site generation or purification.

### Photoresist-specific controls

Account for:

- Temperature and light controls;
- Cold-chain monitoring;
- FEFO rotation;
- Container integrity;
- Filtration and particle controls;
- Storage-excursion disposition;
- Node and product obsolescence.

For short-life materials, rotating regional or supplier-held inventory is generally preferable to excessive on-site stock.

## 9. Supplier engagement and offtake structures

Use layered commitments:

| Layer | Recommended structure |
|---|---|
| Normal volume | Minimum annual quantity with monthly or quarterly flexibility |
| Secondary operating volume | Routine volume sufficient to maintain capability |
| Reserved capacity | Capacity reservation fee or option |
| Surge volume | Predefined call-off rights and pricing |
| Development investment | Shared funding, repayment, price credits, or capacity rights |
| Strategic inventory | Supplier-held or consigned stock with defined access rights |

Contracts should include:

- Flex bands, typically ±20–30% where feasible;
- Rollover of a defined portion of unused volume;
- Milestone-based commitment increases;
- Price-down mechanisms after qualification and ramp milestones;
- Allocation priority during declared disruption;
- Emergency call-off rights;
- Capacity shortfall and quality-failure remedies;
- Sub-tier disclosure and audit rights;
- Portability across fabs or products where feasible;
- Termination or reopening rights for export controls, sanctions, demand collapse, node changes, repeated quality failures, or supplier underperformance;
- Periodic capacity demonstrations and source-health reviews.

For low-volume, high-criticality materials, reservation fee plus call-off pricing is generally preferable to unconditional take-or-pay.

Customer-funded tooling or capacity investments should provide defined capacity rights, repayment, price credits, ownership, or step-in protections.

## 10. Financial decision model

Evaluate each arrangement using:

\[
RATC =
C_{\text{purchase}}
+C_{\text{premium}}
+C_{\text{qualification}}
+C_{\text{reservation}}
+C_{\text{inventory}}
+C_{\text{switching}}
+C_{\text{quality}}
+C_{\text{administration}}
+E[L_{\text{disruption}}]
\]

Include:

- Lost wafer starts and contribution margin;
- Scrap, rework, and yield loss;
- Tool cleaning and chamber recovery;
- Customer and reliability requalification;
- Emergency logistics;
- Expired or obsolete inventory;
- Cylinder rental, inspection, and disposal;
- Internal engineering and metrology capacity;
- Supplier insolvency;
- Common-mode failure;
- Probability the alternate is unavailable, legally unusable, technically unsuccessful, or capacity-constrained.

Use base, adverse, and severe scenarios.

For capacity reservations, approve where:

\[
F+P_rQ+C_I+C_Q < \Delta p \times L
\]

where:

- \(F\) = annual reservation fee;
- \(P_r\) = alternate-source premium per unit;
- \(Q\) = alternate volume;
- \(C_I\) = incremental inventory cost;
- \(C_Q\) = annualized qualification cost;
- \(\Delta p\) = reduction in disruption probability;
- \(L\) = loss if disruption occurs.

The avoided-loss estimate must be adjusted for the probability that the alternate is available, lawful, technically successful, properly packaged, and capable under stress.

Finance approval should state:

- Maximum cash exposure;
- Maximum take-or-pay exposure;
- Reservation-fee duration;
- Inventory-at-risk;
- Expiry and obsolescence exposure;
- Termination liability;
- Break-even disruption probability;
- Expected loss reduction per dollar invested;
- Node and product-roadmap risk.

Expected-value economics cannot override mandatory safety, legal, quality, reliability, customer, or regulatory requirements. Where probabilities are uncertain, use explicit strategic-risk justification rather than false precision.

## 11. Contingency playbooks and escalation

Each A1 material requires a tested playbook covering:

- Export-license suspension;
- Sanctions or payment restrictions;
- Manufacturing-site outage;
- Common precursor failure;
- Logistics-route closure;
- Cylinder or packaging shortage;
- Quality excursion and lot quarantine;
- Supplier insolvency;
- Demand surge;
- Storage or shelf-life failure.

Each playbook must define:

- Trigger threshold;
- Decision owner;
- Trade-compliance review;
- EHS and facilities approval;
- Quality release authority;
- Inventory drawdown sequence;
- Allocation by product, customer, node, or margin;
- Customer communication;
- Alternate activation sequence;
- Recovery and requalification actions.

For high-risk gases, conduct physical drills covering cylinder handling, gas-cabinet connection, purge, leak response, emergency logistics, permits, and abatement. For photoresists, test cold-chain interruption, storage excursions, quarantine, expiry, and controlled release.

Procurement cannot activate an unqualified source. Operations cannot waive EHS or quality controls without a formally authorized deviation.

## 12. Governance and status

### Executive steering committee

Include:

- COO or supply-chain executive;
- CFO;
- Procurement;
- Process technology;
- Fab operations;
- Quality;
- Reliability;
- Facilities and EHS;
- Legal and trade compliance;
- Customer or product engineering.

Responsibilities:

- Approve A1 priorities;
- Approve inventory, reservation, and development premiums;
- Resolve quality-versus-continuity conflicts;
- Approve offtake and capacity agreements;
- Accept residual single-source exposure;
- Decide production allocation during disruption.

Meet monthly during the first year and quarterly thereafter.

### Material resilience owner

One accountable owner per A1 material manages:

- Criticality score and dependency map;
- Alternate qualification plan;
- Inventory and capacity target;
- Supplier contract and quality status;
- Traceability and contingency readiness;
- KPI reporting and escalation.

Quality and EHS retain stop-work authority for unsafe, contaminated, or nonconforming material.

### Source status

| Status | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Red** | Unprotected single source; no credible alternate or insufficient usable coverage |
| **Orange** | Candidate identified; audit, EHS, or technical review incomplete |
| **Yellow** | Technical qualification in progress; not approved for production |
| **Blue** | Technically qualified, but capacity, logistics, contract, or upstream independence incomplete |
| **Green** | Continuity-ready: qualified, repeatable, independent, contracted, capacity-demonstrated, and activation-tested |
| **Gray** | Residual single-source risk formally accepted by executives with owner and review date |

Only Green sources receive full resilience credit. Management reporting must distinguish candidate, characterized, technically qualified, production-approved, capacity-demonstrated, contractually protected, and continuity-ready sources.

## 13. Implementation timeline

### 0–30 days: Establish control

- Appoint executive sponsor, program leader, and A1 owners;
- Reconcile book inventory to released, usable inventory;
- Map suppliers, sites, precursors, cylinders, packaging, laboratories, routes, and payment dependencies;
- Identify A1/A2 materials and common-mode failures;
- Confirm shelf-life, storage, EHS, cylinder, logistics, and export-control constraints;
- Freeze uncontrolled A1 changes;
- Define material-specific qualification criteria;
- Establish escalation and risk-acceptance procedures.

### 30–90 days: Protect and launch

- Set temporary A1 inventory and supplier-held-stock targets;
- Secure lawful logistics alternatives, emergency allocations, and capacity options;
- Audit candidate suppliers and facilities;
- Obtain sub-tier, impurity, packaging, process-flow, and change-control data;
- Reserve engineering, reliability, metrology, facilities, and EHS resources;
- Launch first-wave qualifications;
- Maintain second candidates for the highest-risk A1 materials;
- Negotiate milestone-based commercial term sheets;
- Create allocation and rationing playbooks.

### 3–12 months: Qualify and contract

- Complete gas-delivery, resist-storage, material, packaging, and process characterization;
- Execute multi-lot, multi-campaign, reliability, and downstream-integration testing;
- Establish incoming verification and trend monitoring;
- Close supplier audit findings;
- Validate storage, cylinder, packaging, and emergency logistics;
- Convert provisional arrangements into enforceable contracts;
- Conduct tabletop and physical continuity exercises;
- Recalculate economics using actual qualification results.

### 12–24 months: Ramp and demonstrate

- Complete production and reliability approval;
- Ramp alternates gradually under enhanced monitoring;
- Demonstrate repeatability and capacity under competing demand;
- Test activation, logistics, allocation, and recovery;
- Reclassify sources to Green only after all gates pass;
- Obtain executive acceptance for remaining Gray A1 risks.

### 24–36 months: Institutionalize

- Expand to lower-priority materials where justified;
- Evaluate regional production, on-site generation, purification, recycling, and closed-loop options;
- Aggregate demand across fabs to support supplier investment;
- Refresh dependency maps and stress tests;
- Embed resilience requirements in new-product introduction and node transitions;
- Establish a permanent resilience budget and annual review cycle.

## 14. KPIs

### Leading indicators

- A1 materials with accountable owners and approved playbooks;
- Qualification milestones completed on time;
- Time to planned production approval;
- Critical upstream inputs independently sourced;
- Qualified and contractually protected capacity;
- Usable inventory versus tested coverage;
- Supplier audit and CAPA closure;
- Contracts containing allocation, change-control, rollover, and termination protections;
- Trade-control assessments completed for alternate suppliers and routes;
- Source-health review completion;
- Second candidate available for high-risk A1 materials.

### Outcome measures

- A1 production value covered by two qualified independent sources;
- Coverage by material grade, tool, layer, product, and export-control region;
- Time to activate an alternate;
- Recovery time for defined 30-, 90-, and 180-day scenarios;
- Wafer starts protected in stress tests;
- Alternate-source yield, defectivity, and reliability;
- Contracted capacity shortfall;
- Material-related excursions;
- Inventory expiry and obsolescence;
- Take-or-pay utilization;
- Resilience cost per protected wafer;
- Expected loss reduction per dollar invested;
- Number and duration of Gray A1 risks.

Program-level ambitions may include:

- 100% of A1 materials assigned owners and contingency plans within 90 days;
- 100% with predefined qualification criteria and change controls;
- 100% of critical gases with delivery-system, EHS, cylinder, and traceability controls;
- 100% of critical photoresists with storage, lot-consistency, defectivity, and process-window criteria;
- 70–80% of first-wave A1 materials continuity-ready within 24 months, subject to technical and economic feasibility;
- No A1 single-source exposure without explicit executive risk acceptance;
- Tested recovery time below the tolerable interruption window.

These are portfolio ambitions, not substitutes for material-specific engineering and reliability acceptance criteria.

## 15. Assumptions requiring validation

Before approving major commitments, validate:

- Qualification duration by material grade, tool, layer, and product;
- Actual released and usable inventory;
- Storage, permit, shelf-life, and cold-chain limits;
- Cylinder, valve, filling, and packaging bottlenecks;
- Supplier minimum economic volumes;
- Supplier financial condition and investment plans;
- Common precursor, laboratory, technology, and logistics dependencies;
- Export-license and re-export requirements;
- Payment and insurance continuity;
- Fab engineering, reliability, metrology, facilities, and EHS capacity;
- Customer approval requirements;
- Node and product obsolescence risk;
- Contribution margin per protected wafer;
- Disruption probability and duration;
- Alternate failure probability under stress;
- Emergency-route legal and physical feasibility.

Until validated, financial outputs should be presented as ranges, sensitivities, and break-even cases rather than precise savings.

## 16. Trade-offs and final approval rule

### Quality versus speed
Do not lower quality, reliability, contamination-control, or EHS standards. Accelerate through parallel audits, characterization, engineering, reliability, commercial, and trade-compliance work.

### Resilience versus unit cost
A premium may be justified where it materially reduces fab-stop exposure. No resilience credit should be assigned to an unqualified, legally unavailable, upstream-dependent, or capacity-unproven source.

### Inventory versus cash and shelf life
More inventory improves short-term protection but increases working capital, expiry, safety, storage, and obsolescence risk. Use layered, rotating, supplier-held, and regional stock rather than attempting to cover the full qualification period on site.

### Supplier commitment versus financial flexibility
Suppliers need predictable demand to invest. Finance needs protection against demand changes, process transitions, and underperformance. Use reservation fees, rollover, portability, milestone step-ups, price-downs, termination rights, and enforceable capacity remedies.

### Geographic diversification versus real independence
Different countries do not eliminate common precursors, ownership, technology, packaging, payment, or logistics risks. Diversify the dependency network.

### Coverage versus efficiency
A 50/50 split is neither necessary nor automatically resilient. The correct target is the minimum qualified capacity and usable coverage that protect production during the validated disruption and recovery window.

### Final approval rule

Approve dual sourcing, inventory, or capacity reservation only when the arrangement:

1. Reduces validated recovery time below the tolerable interruption window;
2. Provides genuine technical and upstream independence;
3. Passes quality, reliability, EHS, contamination-control, and traceability gates;
4. Has enforceable capacity, allocation, change-control, response-time, and remedy provisions;
5. Has a defensible risk-adjusted economic case or explicitly documented strategic-risk justification;
6. Has an accountable owner, milestones, escalation triggers, and a review date.

The company should proceed immediately with first-wave qualification and near-term continuity protections. Final approval of major commitments should be conditional on material-level business cases, validated usable-inventory data, formal trade-control procedures, enforceable supplier agreements, documented qualification gates, and tested continuity playbooks.
