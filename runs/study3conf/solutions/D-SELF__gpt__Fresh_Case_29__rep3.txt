# Executive Summary

Adopt a **wave-based, material-level dual-sourcing program** for specialty gases and photoresists exposed to export controls, geopolitical disruption, logistics failures, and supplier concentration.

The objective is not a 50/50 supplier split or maximum supplier count. It is to ensure that the most consequential materials have a **technically qualified, legally shippable, recently exercised, and financially supportable fallback**.

The company should:

1. **Start qualification immediately** for materials that can stop a fab or constrain strategic products.
2. Segment materials by business impact, technical substitutability, recovery time, geopolitical exposure, and true supplier independence.
3. Use differentiated sourcing postures: operational dual sourcing, qualified readiness sources, or managed single sourcing with explicit residual-risk approval.
4. Bridge the 12–24 month qualification period with targeted inventory, incumbent protections, rationing plans, and interim capacity options.
5. Use staged commercial commitments—reservation fees, firm minimums, options, readiness lots, and selective co-investment—instead of broad unconditional take-or-pay.
6. Treat quality, EHS, trade compliance, and financial exposure as independent approval gates.
7. Measure resilience by **contingency-capable supply within a defined recovery time**, not by the number of approved suppliers.

---

# 1. Assumptions and Decision Principles

These assumptions should be validated during the first 90 days:

- Qualification typically requires 12–24 months, but delivery-system, facility, reliability, customer, and regulatory requirements may extend the timeline.
- Qualification may be limited to specific fabs, tools, products, layers, or process nodes.
- Suppliers in different countries may share feedstocks, resins, purification technology, packaging, logistics routes, ownership, or export-control exposure.
- Disruptions may be partial; an incumbent may supply only 30%, 50%, or 75% of normal demand.
- For sensitive photoresists and gases, a small yield or defectivity change can cost more than the material price premium.
- Inventory is constrained by hazardous-storage limits, shelf life, temperature control, cylinder availability, and release testing.
- Suppliers may require long-term demand visibility to invest in capacity, but buyer exposure can be capped through staged commitments.
- Contracts cannot compel performance that becomes illegal because of export controls, sanctions, import restrictions, or dangerous-goods requirements.

### Decision principles

1. **Start the qualification clock before the business case is perfect** when qualification lead time exceeds the acceptable recovery window.
2. **Separate technical qualification from production allocation.**
3. Use inventory for short disruptions and qualified capacity for long disruptions.
4. Pay for measurable capabilities—released cylinders, qualified lots, packaging, analytical release, and emergency logistics—not theoretical annual capacity.
5. Apply hard legal, safety, and quality gates; financial benefits cannot offset failure in these areas.
6. Treat dormant, legally blocked, or common-mode sources as partial protection rather than full redundancy.

# 2. Material Segmentation and Recovery Objectives

## 2.1 Segment at the manufacturing-relevant level

Assess risk by:

- Gas molecule, grade, purity, and delivery mode.
- Photoresist formulation, grade, package, and application.
- Cylinder, valve, container, or bulk-delivery configuration.
- Manufacturing site and upstream feedstock.
- Fab, tool, product, layer, and process node.

A supplier providing the same chemical but not the required purity, packaging, cylinder, analytical release, or process-specific formulation is not an equivalent source.

## 2.2 Scoring framework

Score each material on a 1–5 scale for:

- Production-stop and contribution-loss impact.
- Number of fabs, tools, products, and nodes affected.
- Time to recover.
- Technical substitutability.
- Qualification complexity.
- Supplier and site concentration.
- Feedstock, equipment, packaging, and logistics commonality.
- Export-control and regulatory exposure.
- Inventory feasibility and shelf-life constraints.
- Quality, safety, and customer impact.
- Qualification and ongoing resilience cost.

Apply a **maximum-risk override**: a low-spend material that can stop a fab or create a major safety exposure cannot be downgraded based on spend alone.

## 2.3 Recommended categories

| Category | Typical characteristics | Required posture |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Mission-critical** | Production-stopping, long qualification, limited alternatives, high geopolitical or common-mode exposure | Immediate alternate qualification; secure contingency capacity; hold feasible inventory; conduct controlled readiness lots; executive oversight |
| **B — Critical and substitutable** | Significant yield, schedule, or customer impact, with credible alternatives | Qualify an alternate; maintain modest recurring volume or periodic readiness lots; reserve expansion capacity |
| **C — Important but recoverable** | Disruption causes cost or delay but not immediate stoppage | Dual source where economically justified; otherwise use inventory and fallback options |
| **D — Standard** | Multiple sources, short replenishment, low process sensitivity, or low exposure | Optimize cost and service; no automatic dual-source requirement |

Each A and B material must have a defined:

- **Recovery-time objective (RTO)**
- Minimum supply percentage during disruption
- Alternate activation time
- Inventory bridge requirement
- Priority products and fabs
- Maximum acceptable yield or quality delta

Example:

> Maintain at least 60% of stressed demand for 90 days, activate alternate supply within 30 days, and protect designated strategic products.

# 3. Supplier Portfolio and Independence

## 3.1 Supplier roles

- **Primary:** normal-volume source with established quality, yield, and delivery performance.
- **Strategic alternate:** qualified source with reserved capacity and emergency capability.
- **Readiness source:** approved for limited applications and periodically exercised.
- **Fallback candidate:** technically promising but not production qualified.
- **Managed single source:** incumbent retained where alternatives are infeasible, with enhanced protections and formally accepted residual risk.

## 3.2 Independence assessment

An alternate receives full independence credit only after assessing:

1. Different legal entity and manufacturing site.
2. Different production, purification, or formulation route.
3. Different feedstocks, precursors, resins, solvents, and critical sub-tier suppliers.
4. Different cylinders, valves, packaging, and critical equipment.
5. Different ports, carriers, freight forwarders, and logistics corridors.
6. Independent export, import, licensing, and regulatory pathways.
7. Financial and ownership independence.

Classify each dependency as **known, suspected, or unknown**, with evidence quality. Unknown dependencies should be treated conservatively.

## 3.3 Allocation guidance

Do not pursue automatic 50/50 sourcing.

- **A materials:** retain the incumbent as primary where justified, but give the alternate controlled recurring volume or scheduled readiness lots.
- **B materials:** use a smaller routine share or periodic lots, with options for rapid expansion.
- **C materials:** maintain an alternate only where avoided disruption loss exceeds qualification and maintenance cost.
- **No viable alternate:** use inventory, incumbent protections, upstream diversification, redesign, or explicit residual-risk acceptance.

For photoresists, approval may initially be limited to selected layers, products, tools, or nodes. For gases, qualify the full delivery system where it can affect contamination or tool performance.

# 4. Qualification and Quality Plan

A source is not resilient merely because it passes certificate-of-analysis testing. The material, packaging, delivery system, logistics, documentation, and change-control regime must be proven under production-relevant conditions.

## Qualification gates

### Gate 0 — Baseline and acceptance criteria

- Identify affected tools, products, fabs, and process steps.
- Establish the incumbent baseline using multiple representative lots.
- Define critical-to-process attributes: impurities, metals, moisture, particles, stability, packaging, and delivery characteristics.
- Define wafer-level limits for yield, defectivity, critical dimensions, reliability, and process window.
- Identify EHS, facility, customer, and regulatory approvals.

### Gate 1 — Supplier, site, and legal due diligence

Review:

- Manufacturing and filling sites.
- Quality systems, CAPA, deviations, complaints, and audit history.
- Purification, formulation, filtration, and contamination controls.
- Raw-material and sub-tier controls.
- Capacity, cylinders, packaging, and analytical-release evidence.
- Financial health and continuity planning.
- Export/import classifications, permits, dangerous-goods capability, and chemical registrations.
- Ownership changes and restricted-party exposure.

### Gate 2 — Analytical and package screening

Test multiple lots where possible for:

- Identity and concentration.
- Process-relevant impurities, moisture, oxygen, particles, metals, and reactive contaminants.
- Stability, aging, and shipping exposure.
- Cylinder/container cleanliness and integrity.
- Valve, connector, regulator, filtration, pressure, and dispense compatibility.
- Traceability, labeling, and documentation.

### Gate 3 — Tool and delivery-system compatibility

For gases, validate the cylinder or bulk source, valve, regulator, gas cabinet, piping, filters, purifiers, flow, pressure, purge, changeover, leak detection, interlocks, and abatement compatibility.

For photoresists, validate container, filtration, dispense, storage, conditioning, coating, bake, exposure, develop, waste, and tool-specific behavior.

### Gate 4 — Engineering wafer evaluation

Use:

- Multiple alternate lots.
- Incumbent control lots.
- Representative tools and sensitive applications.
- Stable process conditions.
- Predefined metrology, defectivity, yield, and process-window criteria.

Avoid simultaneous changes to material, recipe, tool, and process unless interaction effects are explicitly being tested.

### Gate 5 — Pilot and reliability evaluation

Confirm:

- Average performance and variability.
- Lot-to-lot consistency.
- Yield, defectivity, critical dimensions, profile, roughness, and reliability where applicable.
- Storage-age and shipping performance.
- Reproducibility across relevant tools, products, and production dates.

### Gate 6 — Controlled production release

Initially restrict the alternate by:

- Fab, tool, product, layer, node, and volume.
- Incoming and wafer-level monitoring.
- Lot quarantine and release criteria.
- Formal deviation approval.
- Rollback and material-disposition procedures.

Expand scope only after repeated live-order performance.

## Source-status definitions

Management reporting should distinguish:

1. Candidate
2. Under evaluation
3. Conditionally qualified
4. Production qualified
5. Operationally ready
6. Contingency capable
7. Dormant or readiness expired

A source is **contingency capable** only when it has current technical approval, verified usable capacity, current packaging and logistics capability, legal authorization to ship, recent successful production or readiness lots, and a defined activation time and coverage percentage.

# 5. Interim Safeguards During Qualification

## 5.1 Inventory

Use scenario-based sizing:

\[
\text{Usable stock requirement}
=
\text{Demand during recovery}
+
\text{Demand during qualification gap}
-
\text{Reliable inbound supply}
\]

Measure inventory as **released, in-date, correctly stored, process-compatible stock available at the required location**.

Model:

- Logistics delay: 2–4 weeks.
- Supplier outage: 1–3 months.
- Export-control suspension: 3–12 months.
- Common upstream failure: 6–12 months or longer.
- Partial incumbent supply: 30%, 50%, and 75%.
- Demand surge and forecast error.
- Quality hold or recall.

### Specialty gases

Inventory must respect:

- Hazardous-storage limits.
- Segregation and ventilation requirements.
- Cylinder inspection and recertification.
- Gas cabinet and leak-detection capacity.
- Transport and return-cycle constraints.
- Abatement and emergency-response capability.

### Photoresists

Control:

- Temperature and light exposure.
- Expiry and retest status.
- Lot segregation.
- Shipping excursions.
- Container integrity.
- Dispense compatibility.
- Disposal and write-off risk.

Do not build inventory that is likely to expire before use.

## 5.2 Incumbent protections

Negotiate:

- Forward capacity visibility.
- Emergency allocation rights.
- Supplier-held or regional safety stock.
- Upstream feedstock visibility.
- Alternate logistics routes.
- Advance notice of site, process, formulation, and regulatory changes.
- Priority treatment during partial shortages.

## 5.3 Allocation and rationing

Predefine allocation by:

- Fab.
- Node and product.
- Customer commitment.
- Contribution margin.
- Restart and qualification cost.
- Strategic importance.
- Minimum safe operating level.

Site operations may act within approved bands. Enterprise operations should control cross-fab allocation, while executives approve major customer or production trade-offs.

## 5.4 Readiness lots

For A materials, use controlled readiness lots only when technically justified. Define:

- Approved products, layers, tools, and chambers.
- Stable process conditions.
- Enhanced incoming and wafer monitoring.
- Source and lot traceability.
- Quarantine and rollback procedures.
- Minimum test frequency.
- Maximum dormancy period.
- Revalidation requirements.

Readiness lots do not replace contingency-capable capacity.

# 6. Commercial and Financial Mechanisms

## 6.1 Economic model

Estimate expected disruption loss:

\[
\text{Expected disruption loss}
=
P(\text{disruption}) \times
\text{duration} \times
\text{contribution loss per day}
\]

Include scrap, rework, yield loss, expedite premiums, customer penalties, engineering recovery, fab reallocation, and long-term customer impact.

Model expected quality cost separately:

\[
\text{Expected quality cost}
=
P(\text{quality failure}) \times
(\text{scrap}+\text{yield loss}+\text{requalification}+\text{customer impact})
\]

Compare against:

\[
\text{Resilience cost}
=
\text{qualification}
+\text{reservation}
+\text{minimum-volume shortfall}
+\text{price premium}
+\text{inventory cost and write-off}
+\text{readiness maintenance}
+\text{exit and lock-in cost}
\]

Include probability of qualification success, delay, ramp failure, and partial technical approval.

Proceed immediately when the material can stop a fab and lacks a credible fallback, or when risk-adjusted avoided loss materially exceeds resilience cost. Financial return cannot override legal, safety, or technical gates.

## 6.2 Contract architecture

| Stage | Buyer commitment | Supplier obligation | Expansion gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Candidate** | Evaluation orders and limited funding | Samples, technical data, capacity plan | Technical screening |
| **Development** | Reservation fee and qualification volumes | Engineering support and development capacity | Engineering approval |
| **Qualified** | Firm minimum volume plus options | Validated capacity, quality, change control, logistics | Production release |
| **Operational** | Recurring readiness lots and emergency reservation | Defined response time and contingency supply | Quarterly review |

Preferred mechanisms include:

- Reservation fees partly creditable against purchases.
- Refund or conversion rights if qualification fails for supplier-attributable reasons.
- Firm base volume plus option volume.
- Volume banking across periods, fabs, and approved applications.
- Emergency capacity premiums paid only for verified performance.
- Portfolio-level commitments where volumes are technically fungible.
- Milestone-based qualification funding.
- Targeted co-investment for A materials where ordinary contracting cannot secure capacity.

## 6.3 Contract protections

Specify:

- Reserved capacity by month or quarter.
- Released cylinders, qualified lots, delivered liters or kilograms—not theoretical annual capacity.
- Feedstock, packaging, analytical-release, and logistics obligations.
- Emergency response and recovery times.
- Priority allocation during shortages.
- Quality, OTIF, and change-notification requirements.
- Audit and verification rights.
- Lot traceability and retained samples.
- Remedies for attributable failure.
- Third-party sourcing rights after defined failure.
- Export-control, sanctions, illegality, force-majeure, and suspension provisions.
- Termination, transition, and data-use protections.
- Caps on unused-volume and advance-payment exposure.

## 6.4 Finance controls

Each commitment should show:

- Annual resilience cost.
- Maximum cash-at-risk.
- Unused-volume and stranded-inventory exposure.
- Advance-payment and supplier-credit risk.
- Accounting and capitalization treatment.
- Foreign-exchange exposure.
- Termination liability.
- Recovery or transfer value of funded assets.
- Expected avoided disruption loss.
- Break-even disruption probability and duration.
- Residual risk after implementation.

A 2:1 avoided-loss-to-cost ratio may be an initial guide, but strategic, customer, safety, or national-security considerations may justify exceptions with executive approval.

---

# 7. Trade Compliance, EHS, and Legal Gates

Before a source is labeled contingency capable, confirm:

1. Export and import classifications.
2. Required licenses and permits.
3. End-use, end-user, and re-export requirements.
4. Screening of suppliers, sub-tiers, logistics providers, banks, and insurers.
5. Approved shipping routes and carriers.
6. Dangerous-goods and chemical-registration compliance.
7. Destination-fab storage, handling, waste, and emergency approvals.
8. Controls over technical data, software, and remote engineering support.
9. A documented emergency licensing and escalation process.

Ownership should be explicit:

- **Trade compliance:** classification, licensing, screening, and regulatory monitoring.
- **EHS/facilities:** storage, handling, delivery-system, and site readiness.
- **Legal:** contract enforceability, priority allocation, third-party sourcing, co-investment, consortium, and sanctions clauses.

# 8. Governance, Decision Rights, and KPIs

## Governance

### Executive steering committee

Include operations, procurement, process engineering, quality, finance, legal, trade compliance, EHS, risk, and business-unit leadership.

Approve:

- A-material sourcing postures and exceptions.
- Funding, inventory, reservations, and co-investments above delegated limits.
- Quality-versus-continuity trade-offs.
- Managed single-source residual risk.
- Redesign or structural remediation.

### Resilience program office

Maintain:

- Dependency maps.
- Qualification schedules and evidence.
- Contract and capacity registers.
- Inventory and scenario models.
- Readiness status.
- Disruption playbooks and exercises.
- Executive dashboards and exception logs.

### Functional authority

- **Procurement:** supplier selection and commercial negotiation.
- **Process engineering:** technical qualification.
- **Quality:** quality-system approval, release, and deviations.
- **Trade compliance:** classification, licensing, screening, and routes.
- **EHS/facilities:** safe storage, handling, and site readiness.
- **Finance:** exposure limits and investment approval.
- **Operations:** allocation and contingency execution.
- **Legal:** enforceability and regulatory terms.
- **Executive committee:** exceptions and risk acceptance.

Publish a formal RACI for every A-material program.

## Escalation triggers

- Export-control, sanctions, licensing, or import changes.
- Supplier financial distress, ownership change, or loss of key personnel.
- Plant outage, utility instability, labor event, or disaster.
- Capacity utilization exceeding reserved levels.
- New common upstream, packaging, or logistics dependency.
- Material, formulation, precursor, site, package, or process change.
- Quality hold, contamination, recall, or deteriorating trend.
- Inventory below scenario-based usable coverage.
- Qualification delay beyond the material’s RTO.
- Alternate yield, defectivity, or reliability outside limits.
- Readiness-lot failure or dormancy-limit breach.
- Carrier, cylinder, packaging, or route failure.

## Core KPIs

### Resilience

- A-material demand covered by legally shippable alternate capacity.
- Demand covered by contingency-capable sources within the required RTO.
- Usable inventory coverage under partial incumbent supply.
- Emergency capacity available within 30, 60, and 90 days.
- Common upstream, packaging, and logistics exposure.
- Time since last successful alternate lot.

### Qualification and quality

- Time to engineering approval and production release.
- Qualification success and delay rates.
- Yield, defectivity, critical-dimension, and reliability deltas.
- Lot-to-lot variation.
- Open critical deviations.
- Change-control cycle time.
- Successful alternate lots in the last 12 months.

### Commercial and financial

- Verified versus required usable capacity.
- Purchase-credit recovery.
- Minimum-volume shortfall.
- Maximum cash and stranded-volume exposure.
- Annual resilience cost per material.
- Cost per protected good wafer.
- Expected residual disruption loss.
- Supplier OTIF and emergency response.

Every metric should have a target, threshold, owner, frequency, data source, and prescribed action.

# 9. Phased Roadmap

## 0–30 days: Mobilize and protect

Focus on the highest-risk 10–20 materials or the portfolio covering most fab-stop exposure.

- Appoint executive sponsor and program office.
- Freeze avoidable specification changes for A materials.
- Establish material, site, upstream, packaging, and route maps.
- Identify immediate single points of failure.
- Request incumbent capacity, allocation, change-control, and continuity information.
- Establish temporary inventory, rationing, and escalation rules.
- Define initial RTOs and contingency requirements.

**Deliverable:** initial A-material exposure and continuity report.

## 31–90 days: Segment and secure options

- Complete A–D segmentation and independence scoring.
- Select first-wave candidates.
- Define critical material attributes and wafer-level acceptance criteria.
- Complete supplier, quality, financial, EHS, regulatory, and trade due diligence.
- Approve qualification plans, budgets, and P50/P90 schedules.
- Negotiate reservation and option agreements.
- Set usable inventory targets and verify storage feasibility.
- Establish RACI and executive dashboard.

**Decision gate:** approve, defer, or stop each first-wave program.

## 3–6 months: Begin qualification

- Execute analytical, packaging, cylinder, and delivery-system screening.
- Establish incumbent controls and multi-lot sampling.
- Begin engineering-lot and tool-compatibility work.
- Finalize staged contracts and change-control terms.
- Identify common upstream dependencies and alternate routes.
- Conduct exercises for export delays, partial allocation, quality holds, and supplier outage.

## 6–12 months: Demonstrate feasibility

- Complete engineering wafer evaluations and early reliability work.
- Conduct controlled pilot lots.
- Resolve facility, EHS, storage, logistics, and regulatory gaps.
- Verify capacity in released production units.
- Initiate controlled readiness lots where justified.
- Update economics using observed yield, quality, and capacity data.
- Start the second qualification wave.

**Decision gate:** expand, restrict, redesign, or terminate each alternate.

## 12–24 months: Release and operationalize

- Complete production-equivalent qualification and customer approvals.
- Execute live orders and validate logistics, documentation, packaging, and emergency response.
- Release alternates by defined application scope.
- Establish recurring readiness lots for A materials.
- Certify sources as operationally ready or contingency capable.
- Rebalance inventory, source shares, and reservations.
- Require an explicit executive decision for every A material still lacking a robust alternate.

## Beyond 24 months: Sustain and reduce structural exposure

- Revalidate sources after dormancy or major change.
- Review source, upstream, packaging, and route concentration quarterly.
- Run annual disruption exercises and periodic emergency-order tests.
- Refresh supplier financial, regulatory, and capacity assessments.
- Embed alternate-source requirements in new products, nodes, and process changes.
- Pursue redesign, regional capacity, upstream investment, or additional incumbent protection for persistent single-source risks.

# 10. Required End-State Evidence

For every A and B material, management should be able to identify:

- The disruption scenario and RTO.
- The percentage of demand the alternate can cover and when.
- Whether supply is legally shippable and site-ready.
- Whether the material and delivery system passed production-relevant testing.
- The last successful alternate production lot.
- Remaining upstream, packaging, and logistics commonality.
- Annual cost and maximum cash exposure.
- Quality and yield evidence.
- Activation, rationing, rollback, and requalification procedures.
- The residual risk and accountable executive owner.

This approach balances qualification lead time, quality risk, continuity, and supplier economics through **early qualification, segmented allocation, practical buffers, auditable independence, staged commitments, strict legal and technical gates, and explicit residual-risk decisions**.
