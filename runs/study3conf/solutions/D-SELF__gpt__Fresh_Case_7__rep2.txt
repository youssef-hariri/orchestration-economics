# 2026 International Launch Strategy  
## AI Emotional-Wellbeing Companion

## Executive summary

Launch the companion as a **transparent, user-controlled, non-clinical wellbeing tool**:

> “A way to check in, explore practical support, and decide what next step may help—including connecting with people or professional services.”

The product is not a therapist, doctor, emergency service, continuous safety monitor, or substitute for professional, family, faith-based, or community support. It must not diagnose, provide medication advice, claim clinical certainty, or imply that a human is monitoring a conversation unless that service genuinely exists.

The recommended strategy combines:

1. **A globally consistent safety, privacy, and non-dependency foundation**
2. **Country- and language-level localization rather than region-wide defaults**
3. **User-configurable communication, privacy, memory, faith-sensitivity, and support-circle settings**
4. **Verified local crisis and professional-care pathways**
5. **Controlled pilots with explicit safety, privacy, accessibility, and regulatory go/no-go gates**

Regional insights in this strategy are **research hypotheses**, not assumptions about all users. Preferences should be validated by country, language, age, gender, disability, socioeconomic context, prior care experience, connectivity, and privacy risk.

A 2026 launch should not attempt to cover every country in the four regions simultaneously. The company should select a limited first-wave country set and explicitly defer markets where language quality, crisis infrastructure, regulatory certainty, staffing, or privacy controls are insufficient.

# 1. Global foundation and safeguards

## 1.1 Product boundaries

The companion may provide:

- Emotional check-ins and reflection
- Stress, sleep, routine, and wellbeing support
- Non-diagnostic coping exercises
- Journaling and practical planning
- Help preparing to contact a trusted person or professional
- Local referral and crisis-resource navigation
- User-controlled summaries or message drafts

It must not:

- Diagnose depression, anxiety, PTSD, suicidality, psychosis, mania, eating disorders, or other conditions
- Provide psychotherapy or claim therapeutic equivalence
- Advise users to stop medication or disregard professional care
- Guarantee safety, confidentiality, or immediate response
- Present risk classifications as clinical judgments
- Claim to understand users perfectly
- Encourage users to avoid people or rely exclusively on the AI
- Automatically notify family, employers, schools, insurers, faith leaders, or authorities by default

The product should remind users of these boundaries during onboarding, when they request diagnosis or treatment, during high-risk conversations, and whenever language or translation capability is limited.

## 1.2 Crisis and safeguarding framework

Crisis support must be consistent in principle and localized in execution.

### General distress

The assistant should acknowledge the experience, offer low-risk practical options, and ask whether the user wants:

- Reflection
- A practical next step
- Help contacting someone
- Professional-care information

### Concerning or ambiguous language

For statements such as “I cannot do this anymore,” indirect idioms, or concerning somatic descriptions, the assistant should ask a clear, respectful safety question:

> “When you say you cannot go on, are you thinking about hurting yourself or in immediate danger?”

It must not rely primarily on sentiment, engagement decline, mood scores, or apparent calmness.

### Self-harm, suicidal thoughts, or inability to stay safe

The assistant should:

- Prioritize immediate human and local urgent support.
- Provide verified local crisis and emergency resources.
- Encourage contacting someone nearby only if safe.
- Offer a call script or message draft.
- Keep responses concise and actionable.
- Avoid lengthy exercises that could delay urgent help.
- State whether any human service is actually available.

### Imminent danger or active attempt

The assistant should:

- Say clearly that immediate emergency help is needed.
- Provide the appropriate local emergency route and alternatives.
- Encourage contacting a nearby person or emergency facility if safe.
- Never claim that it has contacted anyone.
- Never imply that a responder is monitoring the conversation unless true.

The crisis specification must also cover threats toward others, third-party risk, abuse, domestic violence, psychosis, mania, intoxication, eating disorders, conflict-related trauma, and inability to access emergency services.

### Resource governance

Each country and marketed language requires a maintained resource registry covering, where available:

- Emergency services
- Crisis and suicide-prevention services
- Domestic-violence and sexual-violence services
- Child-protection services
- LGBTQ+ support
- Refugee and migrant services
- Public, low-cost, remote, and multilingual care

A named resource owner must verify operating hours, language, cost, eligibility, accessibility, and contact accuracy on a defined schedule, with immediate withdrawal procedures for failed or obsolete resources.

## 1.3 Human support boundaries

The product must distinguish:

1. Automated referral navigation
2. User-generated call or message scripts
3. User-requested asynchronous review
4. Real-time human crisis response

For every launch country, specify whether each function exists, its hours, staffing, languages, qualifications, response times, escalation authority, data access, and consent process.

If no live human service exists, state plainly:

> “No live responder is monitoring this conversation. I can help you find local support or draft a message.”

## 1.4 Privacy, consent, and data lifecycle

Separate consent must be obtained for:

- Conversation storage
- Memory and personalization
- Voice recording and transcription
- Location use
- Analytics
- Research
- Model improvement or training
- Human review
- Support-circle sharing
- Partner or sponsor integrations

The product must collect and retain only what is necessary. The data inventory must include conversations, memories, inferred profiles, risk flags, embeddings, prompts, classifier outputs, voice, metadata, device identifiers, referral clicks, support tickets, backups, and vendor copies.

Required controls:

- Encryption in transit and at rest
- Role-based access and access logging
- Short, defined retention periods
- Individual-entry deletion
- Memory reset
- Account closure
- Consent withdrawal
- Usable export
- Deletion from active systems, analytics stores, vendors, and backups where applicable
- Clear, narrow, time-limited exceptions for legal retention
- No indefinite retention of risk flags or inferred emotional profiles

The product must not sell emotional data, use distress for advertising, or provide individual data to employers, insurers, schools, telecoms, sponsors, or public agencies for eligibility, underwriting, performance, or disciplinary decisions.

Avoid absolute claims such as “completely confidential,” “no one can ever see your data,” or “safe from authorities.” Explain risks from device access, account sharing, backups, vendors, and lawful requests.

## 1.5 Account, device, and notification protection

Before launch, decide and threat-model:

- No-account or pseudonymous use
- Phone, email, or social login
- Account recovery
- Multiple-device access
- Shared subscriptions
- Shared devices
- Voice storage
- Local versus cloud history

Provide, where appropriate:

- Neutral lock-screen notifications
- Notification suppression
- Session timeout
- Local-history controls
- Account-access warnings
- Safe deletion and export
- Voice privacy warnings
- Optional additional account protection

Disguised app names or icons should not be deployed automatically; they require domestic-violence, coercive-control, and usability review.

## 1.6 Support-circle architecture

For adults:

- No family access by default
- User-defined relationships, including friend, relative, partner, clinician, mentor, faith leader, or community worker
- Granular sharing of plans, general status, or requests for contact
- No transcript sharing by default
- Recipient confirmation
- Visible access list
- Easy revocation
- Protection against silent reauthorization
- Safety check before suggesting disclosure

The product should ask:

> “Would involving someone be supportive and safe for you?”

Users must be able to choose no one and continue receiving support.

## 1.7 Anti-dependency and engagement

Use flexible check-ins, pause modes, user-defined goals, transparent recommendations, and guilt-free re-entry.

Prohibit:

- Possessive or exclusive language
- “I miss you” messages
- Shame-based streaks
- Rewards for repeated distress disclosure
- Fear-based reminders
- Compulsive notification patterns
- Claims that the AI is a friend, therapist, or replacement for people

## 1.8 Age and capacity policy

Before distribution, set a country-specific minimum age and decide whether minors and dependent adults are:

- Excluded from the first release
- Routed to a separate governed experience
- Included only in a controlled program

Any youth program requires age assurance, dedicated safeguarding, guardian-consent analysis, child-specific crisis pathways, limits on memory and personalization, and clear explanations of privacy from guardians and other household members. Adolescents should not be included in a general adult launch by default.

# 2. Regional launch strategies

## A. East Asia

### Scope and assumptions

Treat Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and mainland China or other Greater China markets as separate launch programs. They differ in language, scripts, regulation, platforms, healthcare access, and institutional trust.

**Hypotheses to test:** Some segments may prefer structured, private, practical entry points involving stress, sleep, focus, study, work, or recovery. Other users may prefer direct emotional language or clinically familiar terminology.

### Positioning

Position the companion as:

- A private reflection and self-management tool
- A practical way to understand stress, sleep, energy, or concentration
- A low-pressure first step before seeking human support
- A supplement to, not a substitute for, professional care

Example:

> “A private space to check in with yourself and take a small helpful next step.”

Do not imply that mental-health support should be hidden or shameful.

### Feature adaptation

Test and offer:

- Emotion, body-sensation, sleep, energy, concentration, and practical check-in modes
- Structured prompts before open-ended conversation
- Formal, neutral, and conversational registers
- Appropriate honorifics and politeness levels
- Quiet mode and minimal-notification settings
- Sessions without persistent memory
- Content related to academic pressure, workplace hierarchy, caregiving, loneliness, and social withdrawal
- Direct and indirect safety questions localized by language
- Clear distinction between privacy from the app and access by someone controlling a device or account

The system must not infer low risk from brief, polite, indirect, or understated language.

### Trust and privacy safeguards

Threat-model:

- Employer and school monitoring
- Family access
- Shared devices
- Platform or network surveillance
- Cross-border data processing
- Workplace or university-sponsored plans

No employer or school should receive individual conversations, risk flags, mood trends, or inferred mental-health data. Distribution through institutions must use technically separate environments and independent user consent.

### Engagement and partnerships

Test consumer, university, healthcare, and employer channels separately. Favor partners able to demonstrate privacy boundaries and local safety competence.

Use routine-based engagement around study, work transitions, sleep, and recovery only where validated. Avoid public social features, leaderboards, and disclosure-based incentives.

### Risks

- Indirect language may be missed in crisis detection.
- Discreet positioning may reinforce stigma.
- Formal language may reduce warmth or clarity.
- Institutional distribution may create surveillance fears.
- Regional grouping may obscure country-level differences.

### Success measures

- Privacy and AI-boundary comprehension
- Safety performance for indirect, somatic, and understated language
- Use of structured and quiet modes
- Low rates of unsafe reassurance and unnecessary escalation
- Appropriate referral to professional or urgent support
- No individual-data exposure through institutional partners

## B. Middle East

### Scope and assumptions

Separate Gulf countries, Levant markets, Arabic-speaking diaspora populations, Iran, Türkiye, Israel, and other relevant markets. Arabic, Persian, Turkish, and Hebrew require independent localization and safety programs.

**Hypotheses to test:** Some users may value dignity, control, privacy, practical coping, and optional values-sensitive support. Others may prefer secular, direct, or clinically oriented language.

### Positioning

Position the product as:

- A respectful, private tool for reflection and coping
- A complement to personal, professional, community, and optional faith-based support
- Support for stress, sleep, grief, uncertainty, caregiving, migration, and life transitions

Example:

> “A private place to pause, reflect, and choose a next step.”

Do not imply that distress reflects weak faith, moral failure, family failure, or poor character.

### Feature adaptation

Provide:

- Modern Standard Arabic plus specifically tested dialects
- Separate programs for Persian, Turkish, Hebrew, and other languages
- Neutral default communication
- User-selected practical, emotional, social, professional, or values-sensitive modes
- Faith-sensitive content only when explicitly selected
- Formal, conversational, gender-neutral where feasible, and user-selected gendered language
- Support for somatic, indirect, grief, shame, family-conflict, and religious-concern expressions
- Low-bandwidth and asynchronous use
- Neutral lock-screen notifications
- Session timeout, local history, and account protection for shared-device risks

Faith-sensitive content must be labeled, non-authoritative, non-moralizing, independently reviewed, and available in secular or neutral form. The system must not infer religion, gender, political identity, or family safety.

### Trust and privacy safeguards

Threat-model:

- Shared devices
- Partner or family monitoring
- Forced disclosure
- Reputational harm
- State or institutional access
- Cross-border processing
- Domestic violence and coercive control

Do not automatically provide family, spouse, parent, authority, or faith-leader access. Do not launch disguised branding without safety testing.

### Engagement and partnerships

Use independent mental-health, women’s safety, youth, migrant, refugee, disability, and LGBTQ+ organizations for validation. Faith-based partnerships should be voluntary, independently governed, and non-prescriptive.

Avoid initial distribution that could make use appear compulsory through employers, families, authorities, or sponsors.

### Risks

- Dialect gaps may create unsafe crisis responses.
- Faith-sensitive content may be moralizing or exclusionary.
- Support-circle sharing may expose users to coercion.
- Privacy claims may be misunderstood as absolute secrecy.
- Gendered or family-oriented design may exclude or endanger users.

### Success measures

- Crisis quality by dialect and language
- Shared-device privacy comprehension
- Safe handling of family, partner, faith, gender, migration, and conflict-related content
- Accurate and reachable local resources
- User control over values-sensitive content
- Absence of unsafe disclosure prompts

## C. Latin America

### Scope and assumptions

Plan separately for Brazil, Mexico, Central America, the Southern Cone, Andean markets, the Caribbean, and relevant Indigenous and migrant communities.

**Hypotheses to test:** Some segments may prefer warmth, voice, practical support, low-data access, and support-circle options. Others may prefer concise, neutral, or competence-focused interactions.

### Positioning

Position the companion as:

- Accessible, practical support for everyday wellbeing
- A bridge to human and professional care
- Useful for stress, sleep, grief, caregiving, financial pressure, migration, and work strain

Example:

> “Practical support for the days when you need to talk, reset, or find a next step.”

Do not present it as a psychologist, continuous caregiver, or replacement for scarce professional services.

### Feature adaptation

Provide:

- Country-reviewed Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese
- Country-specific terms for distress, therapy, crisis, and professional care
- Tone choices: warm, direct, practical, reflective, and brief
- Text and voice, with explicit microphone consent and text fallback
- Low-data and asynchronous exercises
- Content on financial stress, grief, violence exposure, migration, caregiving, and relationship conflict
- User-controlled support-circle sharing
- National, local, public, low-cost, remote, and NGO referral tiers
- Testing for slang, humor, sarcasm, code-switching, spelling variation, and voice transcription

Voice must include audio-retention controls and privacy warnings for shared homes. Essential safety information must not be paywalled.

### Trust and privacy safeguards

Threat-model:

- Shared homes and devices
- Foreign-company distrust
- Telecom or sponsor access
- Voice exposure
- Prepaid-data costs
- Uneven connectivity
- Individualized analytics by employers, insurers, funders, or carriers

Telecom and sponsor partnerships may subsidize access but must not receive individual emotional analytics, transcripts, risk flags, or inferred conditions.

### Engagement and partnerships

Use public-health, community, university, primary-care, pharmacy, migrant, LGBTQ+, disability, Indigenous, caregiver, and telecom partners where data separation is technically enforceable.

Do not launch public peer-support communities initially without robust moderation, anonymity, and safeguarding.

### Risks

- Warmth may feel artificial or patronizing.
- Voice may expose users in shared environments.
- Limited care access may encourage substitution.
- Generic Spanish may be linguistically inappropriate.
- Family or partner sharing may create danger.

### Success measures

- Low-data, affordability, and voice usability
- Country-level Spanish and Portuguese quality
- Privacy and audio-retention comprehension
- Safe support-circle use
- Referral success where professional care is limited
- No evidence that engagement depends on dependency or repeated distress disclosure

## D. Northern Europe

### Scope and assumptions

Plan separately for the Nordics, the United Kingdom and Ireland, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and minority-language communities.

**Hypotheses to test:** Some users may prioritize autonomy, transparent data practices, evidence boundaries, and low-pressure engagement. Others may still prefer warmth and companionship.

### Positioning

Position the product as:

- A transparent self-management and reflection tool
- A supplement to public, private, student, workplace-independent, or community services
- A practical aid for stress, sleep, routines, and preparation for human support

Example:

> “Clear, user-controlled support for everyday wellbeing.”

### Feature adaptation

Provide:

- Warm and neutral communication modes
- Direct, non-euphemistic language about distress and safety
- Layered consent and data-flow explanations
- Separate controls for memory, personalization, analytics, research, and model improvement
- Accessibility aligned with applicable standards
- Country-specific referral information without implying healthcare integration
- Optional calendar, sleep, or routine integrations requiring explicit permission
- Minority-language support only where quality and safety thresholds are met

Use plain-language summaries with expandable technical detail to avoid consent fatigue.

### Trust and privacy safeguards

Show:

- What is stored
- Where it is processed
- Who can access it
- Whether it is used for model improvement
- Retention periods
- Deletion and export processes

Employer, university, insurer, public-sector, and consumer environments must be technically separated. Participation must not affect employment, benefits, insurance, education, or services.

### Engagement and partnerships

Consider public-health, primary-care, mental-health charity, university, accessibility, minority-language, and carefully governed employer partnerships.

Employer or public-sector sponsorship must never imply that individual conversations are visible to the sponsor.

### Risks

- Detailed consent can overwhelm users.
- Institutional distribution can feel coercive.
- Low anthropomorphism may reduce engagement for some users.
- “Evidence-informed” may be interpreted as product-specific clinical evidence.
- Minority-language users may receive lower-quality safety support.

### Success measures

- Consent, data-flow, and AI-boundary comprehension
- Accessibility performance, especially in crisis flows
- Evidence-claim comprehension
- Safety and referral quality by language
- Technical separation from institutional partners
- User-controlled engagement and low dependency risk

# 3. Cross-region research and validation

## 3.1 Discovery

For each initial country, conduct research with:

- Students and young adults
- Older adults
- Current and former care users
- People who avoid professional care
- Caregivers
- Disabled users
- Migrants and multilingual users
- People using shared devices
- People with crisis, domestic-violence, or coercive-control experience
- Local clinicians, crisis workers, linguists, cultural advisors, and lived-experience reviewers

Use interviews, diary studies, usability research, and pilot behavior—not stated preference alone.

## 3.2 Language and safety testing

Test:

- Direct and indirect suicidality
- Euphemisms, understatement, humor, and sarcasm
- Somatic distress
- Dialects, code-switching, spelling variation, and voice errors
- Grief, religious concern, family conflict, migration, and violence
- LGBTQ+ identity distress
- Requests for diagnosis or medication
- Domestic violence and unsafe disclosure
- Threats toward others
- Psychosis, mania, intoxication, and eating disorders
- Refusal or inability to answer safety questions

Evaluate safety, clarity, cultural fit, uncertainty, actionability, referral accuracy, false reassurance, unnecessary escalation, and response length.

If a language cannot meet safety thresholds, restrict functionality and disclose the limitation rather than market it as fully supported.

## 3.3 Configuration governance

Every localized behavior must record:

- Country and segment scope
- Evidence source
- Product owner
- Safety reviewer
- User-overridable settings
- Excluded or potentially harmed groups
- Review date
- Revalidation trigger

Revalidate after changes to the model, prompts, memory, voice, data practices, crisis flows, or partnerships.

# 4. Operating model and accountability

Create a country-level RACI with named accountable owners for:

- Country launch
- Clinical and product safety
- Crisis protocols
- Resource registry
- Language and cultural review
- Accessibility
- Privacy and data protection
- Security
- Regulatory classification
- Human-review operations
- Partner governance
- Marketing claims
- Incident response
- Model release and rollback
- User complaints and appeals

The safety, privacy, and clinical functions must have authority to block launch or suspend a feature, language, country, partner, or model version. Commercial leadership must not override a documented safety stop without formal executive and board-level risk acceptance.

# 5. Phased launch roadmap

## Phase 0: Country selection and governance — Q1 2026

Deliverables:

- Select a limited first-wave country set.
- Identify deferred countries and reasons.
- Complete regulatory classification memos.
- Map data flows, vendors, backups, and retention.
- Define supported languages and safety thresholds.
- Verify crisis and safeguarding resources.
- Establish local advisors and named owners.
- Complete threat models for devices, partners, coercion, and surveillance.
- Approve claims and prohibited claims.
- Finalize adult-only or youth-exclusion policy.

## Phase 1: Controlled adult pilots — Q2 2026

Pilot requirements:

- Predefined country and language cohorts
- Informed consent and participant protections
- Human-review coverage or explicit absence of live review
- Independent safety evaluation
- Incident taxonomy and severity levels
- Daily or scheduled safety monitoring
- Model-change freeze or controlled release process
- Stop conditions for severe incidents, resource failures, privacy breaches, or major disparities
- Participant compensation and support procedures

Before piloting, define metric numerators, denominators, sample sizes, confidence intervals, owners, targets, and remediation actions.

## Phase 2: Limited public expansion — Q3 2026

Expand only after:

- Crisis performance meets preapproved country-language thresholds.
- False reassurance and unnecessary escalation are within approved limits.
- Users correctly understand the AI, non-clinical, and emergency boundaries.
- Privacy, memory, sharing, and deletion controls are understood and functional.
- Referral resources are current and reachable.
- Support-circle features pass coercion and domestic-violence testing.
- Partner data separation is technically verified.
- Accessibility and low-connectivity requirements are met.

## Phase 3: Controlled scaling and assurance — Q4 2026 onward

Maintain:

- Quarterly safety, bias, privacy, and security reviews
- Continuous language and dialect evaluation
- Local user advisory panels
- Independent audits and red-team testing
- Public aggregate transparency reporting
- Partner audits
- Change-control review for every material model or feature update
- Rapid rollback and language-suspension capability
- Reapproval after changes to crisis behavior, memory, voice, data use, or distribution

Youth expansion requires a separate approval program.

# 6. Success measures and launch gates

## Safety

Measure by country, language, and relevant demographic groups:

- Appropriate safety-question rate
- Crisis sensitivity and precision
- False reassurance
- Unnecessary escalation
- Unsafe, dismissive, moralizing, or culturally inappropriate responses
- Resource accuracy and failed-contact rate
- Time to display urgent guidance
- Appropriate referral navigation
- Harmful dependency language
- Support-circle disclosure errors
- Accessibility failures in crisis flows

## Trust and privacy

Measure:

- Understanding that the product is AI
- Understanding that it is not therapy or emergency monitoring
- Understanding of storage, memory, training, and sharing
- Successful deletion, export, and consent withdrawal
- Perceived user control
- Misunderstanding of “private” or “confidential”
- Human-review access violations
- Privacy incidents and remediation time
- Vendor and backup deletion completion

## Usefulness and equity

Measure:

- First-check-in completion
- Usefulness after sessions
- Use of coping tools
- Progress on user-defined goals
- Appropriate connection to human or professional support
- Voice, accessibility, and low-data success
- Safety disparities across languages, genders, ages, disabilities, and relevant minority groups
- Retention without compulsive use, guilt, or repeated distress disclosure

No country should launch or expand if:

- Crisis resources are inaccurate or unavailable in a marketed language.
- Users mistake the product for therapy or emergency monitoring.
- Privacy and sharing controls are not understood.
- Claimed human support cannot be delivered.
- Deletion or consent withdrawal fails.
- Major language or demographic safety disparities remain unresolved.
- Partner access cannot be technically constrained.
- Domestic-violence and coercion risks have not been assessed.

# 7. Implementation checklist

## Strategy and scope

- [ ] Select initial countries and explicitly defer others.
- [ ] Define supported languages, dialects, and functionality limits.
- [ ] Convert all regional recommendations into evidence-based, reviewable configurations.
- [ ] Approve global positioning and prohibited claims.

## Safety and care boundaries

- [ ] Approve crisis response specifications and edge-case protocols.
- [ ] Verify country-specific emergency, crisis, safeguarding, and professional resources.
- [ ] Define whether any real-time or asynchronous human review exists.
- [ ] Implement explicit non-clinical and non-emergency disclosures.
- [ ] Test psychosis, mania, intoxication, abuse, eating disorders, and third-party risk flows.

## Privacy and security

- [ ] Complete data inventory and vendor register.
- [ ] Set retention and deletion requirements for all data categories.
- [ ] Implement separate consent for storage, memory, voice, analytics, research, training, review, and sharing.
- [ ] Test account, device, backup, notification, and shared-access risks.
- [ ] Prohibit sponsor use of individual emotional data.
- [ ] Complete security testing and incident-response exercises.

## Family, minors, and accessibility

- [ ] Implement support-circle authorization, recipient verification, and revocation.
- [ ] Complete coercion and domestic-violence threat modeling.
- [ ] Set the minimum launch age and youth/dependent-adult policy.
- [ ] Test accessible consent, deletion, and crisis flows.
- [ ] Provide text alternatives, captions, screen-reader support, and low-bandwidth paths.

## Localization and partnerships

- [ ] Complete human linguistic, clinical, cultural, accessibility, and lived-experience review.
- [ ] Test dialects, idioms, directness, humor, code-switching, and voice.
- [ ] Contractually and technically separate partner data.
- [ ] Establish resource-registry ownership and verification schedules.
- [ ] Create user reporting, correction, appeal, and harmful-response channels.

## Release governance

- [ ] Assign a named country launch owner and local safety owner.
- [ ] Define pilot metrics, thresholds, sample sizes, and stop conditions.
- [ ] Establish model release, rollback, and language-suspension procedures.
- [ ] Conduct independent prelaunch review.
- [ ] Publish accurate privacy, safety, and capability documentation.
- [ ] Revalidate after any material model, prompt, memory, voice, data-use, or partnership change.

**Strategic conclusion:** The launch should scale only where the company can demonstrate not merely cultural relevance, but safe local-language performance, understandable privacy controls, reliable crisis navigation, genuine professional-care boundaries, and protection from coercion and institutional surveillance.
