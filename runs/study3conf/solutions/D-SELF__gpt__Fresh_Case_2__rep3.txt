# Global AI Compliance and Customer Communication Strategy  
## AI-Powered Hiring Platform | Executive Decision Document

**Audience:** Leadership, Commercial, Product, Legal, Compliance, Security, Customer Success, Marketing, and Support  
**Status:** Strategic operating framework; jurisdiction-specific legal validation required before deployment or claims

---

## 1. Executive summary

The company should operate **one global AI hiring brand supported by a common compliance baseline and controlled jurisdictional overlays**.

This approach avoids two unworkable extremes:

- Building fifteen separate country products; or
- Treating one product configuration as legally suitable everywhere.

The company will maintain:

1. **One global brand promise** centered on secure, transparent, reviewable, human-supported AI.
2. **One global minimum standard** for security, privacy, fairness, accessibility, transparency, auditability, and human oversight.
3. **Feature- and use-case-level classification**, based on intended purpose and actual workflow impact.
4. **Jurisdiction-specific overlays** covering product settings, data location, transfers, notices, contracts, support, and feature availability.
5. **Technical enforcement** through policy controls, not contracts alone.
6. **Clear deployment statuses:** approved, approved with controls, limited, pilot only, pending legal clearance, or unavailable.
7. **A formal conflict-resolution and withdrawal process** where laws cannot be reconciled.
8. **Evidence-backed customer and marketing communications** that distinguish product capabilities from legal compliance.

The company should not claim that the platform is “compliant everywhere.” It should communicate that the platform has a consistent responsible-AI foundation and is configurable for local requirements, subject to the customer’s use case, deployment location, and applicable law.

### Executive decision requested

Approve:

- The global-baseline/controlled-overlay model;
- The governance and decision-rights structure;
- Technical enforcement of country and use-case approvals;
- The restriction or removal of unsupported high-risk functionality;
- The implementation roadmap and minimum launch controls;
- The prohibition on unsupported compliance, fairness, accuracy, certification, and localization claims.

## 2. Global brand position

### Core promise

> **Responsible AI for hiring: secure, transparent, reviewable, and configurable for local requirements.**

This is a qualified capability statement, not a legal certification.

All formal materials should include an appropriate scope statement:

> Capabilities, processing locations, feature availability, and customer obligations vary by product configuration, deployment location, intended use, and applicable law.

### Brand principles

The brand should remain globally consistent while local execution may vary. The following may differ by jurisdiction:

- Feature availability;
- Human-review requirements;
- Candidate and worker notices;
- Hosting and processing regions;
- Support-access arrangements;
- Retention settings;
- Contract terms;
- Customer assessment materials;
- Regulatory documentation.

The following claims are not permitted without highly specific, substantiated support—and generally should not be used:

- “Bias-free”;
- “Objective” or “neutral”;
- “Error-free”;
- “Fully automated hiring”;
- “Replaces human judgment”;
- “Compliant everywhere”;
- “EU AI Act certified,” unless an applicable recognized certification supports the precise claim;
- “Sovereign” or “fully localized,” unless the full promised processing and access scope is met.

## 3. Strategic principles

### 3.1 One brand, not one legal configuration

The company will offer one coherent customer experience while allowing jurisdiction-specific product and documentation settings.

### 3.2 Intended purpose governs

Legal and risk assessment will focus on what the system is designed, marketed, configured, and used to do—not merely what the feature is called.

### 3.3 Feature-level accountability

Candidate sourcing, filtering, ranking, recommendations, assessment scoring, interview analysis, and workflow automation must be assessed separately.

### 3.4 Safer global baseline

The baseline may be stricter than a particular local law. That is a company policy choice, not a claim that every jurisdiction imposes identical requirements.

### 3.5 Technical controls over contractual promises

If a safeguard is important, the product should enforce it through configuration, permissions, workflow gates, APIs, or deployment controls.

### 3.6 No informal exceptions

Where legal requirements are uncertain or conflicting, the deployment is marked **pending legal clearance** or **unavailable** until the issue is resolved.

### 3.7 Evidence before claims

Every compliance, security, fairness, transparency, accuracy, or localization claim must be tied to evidence, scope, product version, geography, and limitations.

### 3.8 Customer responsibility is not liability dumping

The company remains responsible for obligations within its control. Customers remain responsible for their hiring processes, notices, lawful data use, reviewers, local consultations, and final employment decisions.

## 4. Confirmed obligations, recommendations, and questions for local counsel

### 4.1 Confirmed strategic and product obligations

The company should treat the following as firm operating requirements:

- Maintain a feature and use-case inventory.
- Assess legal roles based on actual conduct and control.
- Prevent unsupported prohibited or high-risk uses.
- Require meaningful human review for high-impact workflows.
- Maintain security, privacy, fairness, accessibility, transparency, and auditability controls.
- Map data flows, subprocessors, backups, support access, and model processing.
- Use technical enforcement for country, feature, and service-profile restrictions.
- Maintain incident, post-market monitoring, and model-change processes.
- Substantiate public and customer-facing claims.
- Provide customers with deployment guidance and relevant technical information.
- Distinguish provider obligations from customer obligations.
- Obtain current legal validation before making jurisdiction-specific market-access or compliance statements.

For EU deployments, applicable obligations may include requirements relating to:

- High-risk classification;
- Risk management;
- Data governance;
- Technical documentation;
- Logging;
- Human oversight;
- Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity;
- Quality management;
- Conformity assessment;
- Declaration, marking, registration, post-market monitoring, and incident reporting, where applicable.

The precise obligations depend on the system, role, functionality, timing, and applicable EU provisions.

### 4.2 Company recommendations

The company should:

- Apply a common global baseline to all markets.
- Disable customer-data training by default.
- Prohibit or tightly restrict emotion recognition, sensitive-trait inference, biometric categorization, and fully automated rejection.
- Offer standardized service profiles rather than bespoke country products.
- Use abstention and uncertainty states where evidence is insufficient.
- Provide candidate correction and reconsideration pathways.
- Require deal-desk approval for new countries, sensitive features, automated decisions, and unusual data arrangements.
- Conduct independent assurance progressively, based on risk and commercial priority.
- Withdraw features where localization, security, fairness, or legal requirements cannot be reconciled.

### 4.3 Questions requiring local counsel or specialist advice

Local counsel should confirm, for each country and relevant use case:

- Whether the feature is regulated, restricted, prohibited, or subject to registration.
- Whether employment, equality, privacy, or sector rules impose additional requirements.
- Whether automated decision-making or profiling is restricted.
- What candidate, worker, recruiter, or employer notices are required.
- Whether biometric, emotion, health, or inferred data may be processed.
- Whether works-council or collective consultation is required.
- Whether data, logs, models, support access, or backups must remain local or regional.
- Which transfer mechanism and supplementary safeguards apply.
- Whether public-sector or regulated procurement requirements apply.
- Whether accessibility, licensing, insurance, or certification requirements apply.
- What provider, deployer, controller, processor, or joint-controller roles arise.
- Whether local law conflicts with requirements in another deployment jurisdiction.

This document is an operating strategy, not legal advice.

## 5. Global baseline

### 5.1 Human accountability

The platform will be designed as decision support for hiring—not as a substitute for accountable human judgment.

Controls should include:

- Meaningful human review before material employment action;
- No automatic rejection or selection based solely on AI output under the company baseline;
- Reviewer access to relevant source information and limitations;
- Ability to disregard, override, or request further information;
- Uncertainty and abstention states;
- Review-quality and rubber-stamping monitoring;
- Human-review gates that cannot be disabled through ordinary administration, APIs, exports, or webhooks without approval.

A recruiter’s formal click or approval will not automatically be treated as meaningful human involvement.

### 5.2 Fairness and accessibility

The company will:

- Test performance and error patterns before release and periodically thereafter;
- Assess discriminatory proxies and disparate performance where lawful and proportionate;
- Prohibit sensitive-trait inference for hiring decisions unless specifically approved;
- Maintain controls for any demographic data used for fairness testing;
- Separate validation data from decision data;
- Provide accessible interfaces and accommodation pathways;
- Document limitations where testing data is incomplete or unrepresentative.

### 5.3 Data governance

The company will:

- Use data necessary for the approved purpose;
- Maintain provenance, licensing, collection, accuracy, and permitted-use records;
- Separate production, validation, testing, telemetry, support, and training data;
- Disable customer-data model training by default;
- Govern prompts, outputs, telemetry, corrections, and support data separately;
- Provide retention, deletion, correction, access, and export controls;
- Maintain dataset, model, and test-set versioning;
- Assess historical bias, missing data, representativeness, and proxy risk;
- Integrate product data inventories with records of processing.

Public availability of data does not by itself establish lawful reuse for hiring or model training.

### 5.4 Transparency and contestability

Customer and candidate communications should explain, as applicable:

- That AI is used;
- Which hiring stage is affected;
- The system’s purpose and role;
- Key limitations;
- The role of human review;
- How to correct inaccurate information;
- How to seek reconsideration;
- Who is responsible for the employment decision;
- How to submit a complaint.

### 5.5 Security and resilience

The baseline should include:

- Encryption;
- Tenant isolation;
- Least-privilege access;
- Privileged-access logging;
- Secure software and model supply chains;
- Vulnerability management;
- Penetration testing;
- Prompt-injection, data-poisoning, model-abuse, and output-privacy testing;
- Cross-tenant leakage testing;
- Backup, recovery, rollback, and kill-switch capability;
- Incident response and business continuity.

## 6. Global baseline and local-overlay model

### 6.1 Baseline controls

Applied globally:

- Human-review design;
- Fairness and performance testing;
- Security and privacy controls;
- Data minimization and provenance;
- Audit logging;
- Candidate correction and complaint pathways;
- Claims governance;
- Model-change management;
- Restricted-use policy;
- Incident response.

### 6.2 Local overlays

Applied where required or commercially necessary:

- Country or regional hosting;
- Backup and disaster-recovery location;
- Restricted support access;
- Transfer mechanisms and supplementary safeguards;
- Retention and deletion settings;
- Candidate and worker notices;
- Local assessment materials;
- Contract terms;
- Registration or conformity documentation;
- Feature restrictions;
- Local consultation support;
- Support and escalation arrangements.

### 6.3 Deployment statuses

Every country/use-case combination should receive one status:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Approved** | Baseline is sufficient following documented review. |
| **Approved with specified controls** | Overlay is required and implemented. |
| **Limited** | Only named features, customers, or uses are permitted. |
| **Pilot only** | Controlled deployment while defined issues remain open. |
| **Pending legal clearance** | No production deployment or public sales commitment. |
| **Unavailable** | Prohibited, unsupported, or commercially withdrawn. |

These are operational statuses, not legal conclusions.

## 7. Jurisdiction-management method

### 7.1 Country and use-case matrix

For each of the fifteen countries, assess:

- AI law and regulator guidance;
- Employment and equality rules;
- Automated decision-making and profiling;
- Candidate and worker transparency;
- Human-review obligations;
- Sensitive, biometric, emotion, and inferred data;
- Privacy roles, lawful bases, retention, and data-subject rights;
- Localization and transfer requirements;
- Cybersecurity and incident duties;
- Documentation, registration, licensing, and certification;
- Works councils and collective consultation;
- Public-sector procurement;
- Accessibility;
- Marketing restrictions;
- Enforcement authorities;
- Local-counsel questions.

Each record should include the source, citation, binding status, effective date, translation status, confidence level, owner, and next review date.

### 7.2 Conflict-of-laws protocol

The company should not assume that the stricter rule automatically controls. First determine which law applies to which:

- Actor;
- Data;
- Processing activity;
- Location;
- Customer purpose;
- Product role.

Then:

1. Do not enable prohibited uses.
2. Identify all independently applicable mandatory requirements.
3. Apply the stricter control as a company standard only where it does not create another legal violation.
4. Use separate configurations, regions, or service profiles where feasible.
5. Restrict or withdraw the feature if the conflict cannot be resolved.
6. Escalate conflicts involving localization, government access, candidate rights, surveillance, retention, or regulatory reporting.
7. Record the analysis, decision, affected customers, deadline, and reassessment date.
8. Notify affected customers where processing, availability, terms, or workflow requirements change.

### 7.3 Regulatory monitoring

Use both scheduled and event-driven reviews.

Suggested service levels:

- Potential prohibition, enforcement threat, or market-access issue: immediate escalation and preliminary assessment within 24–48 hours.
- Other material legal development: assessment within five business days.
- Routine development: assessment during the next scheduled review.

The emergency process must authorize temporary feature blocking, suspension of new sales, customer notification, product-configuration changes, and market withdrawal.

## 8. EU AI Act workstream

### 8.1 High-risk analysis

For EU deployments, assess whether intended purpose falls within the employment-related high-risk framework, including systems used for:

- Targeted job advertising;
- Application filtering;
- Candidate analysis;
- Candidate evaluation;
- Candidate ranking or recommendation;
- Promotion, termination, task allocation, or performance evaluation.

A generic matching or ranking feature may require high-risk treatment if marketed or configured to influence selection.

### 8.2 Separate prohibited-practice review

Review separately for:

- Workplace emotion recognition;
- Biometric categorization inferring sensitive characteristics;
- Unlawful biometric identification;
- Sensitive-trait inference;
- Covert or manipulative monitoring;
- Other restricted practices under applicable EU law or guidance.

Potentially prohibited functionality must be blocked or removed unless the specific use is legally confirmed as supportable. Disclosure or consent is not a substitute for a prohibition.

### 8.3 2026–2027 applicability calendar

Because the operating scenario is in 2026, the company should maintain a live calendar covering:

- Provisions already applicable by August 2026;
- Relevant prohibited-practice and AI-literacy requirements;
- High-risk obligations applicable from 2 August 2026 where relevant;
- Separate timing for high-risk systems embedded in regulated products, if applicable;
- General-purpose AI obligations and relevance to the company’s role;
- Later obligations and transition arrangements;
- Consequences of substantial modification;
- Implementing measures, standards, guidance, registration arrangements, and enforcement materials.

The calendar must be validated against current official EU sources before any readiness, market-access, or compliance statement is made.

### 8.4 Market-access release gate

Before placing a relevant high-risk system on the EU market or putting it into service, the company must confirm the applicable:

- Risk-management evidence;
- Data-governance controls;
- Technical documentation;
- Logging;
- Human oversight;
- Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity testing;
- Quality-management procedures;
- Conformity-assessment route;
- Declaration, marking, and registration requirements, where applicable;
- Post-market monitoring;
- Serious-incident procedures.

The company must not assume that every employment-related high-risk system requires notified-body involvement. It must document whether internal control is available and whether biometric or other functionality triggers a different route.

## 10. Customer and marketing communication framework

### 10.1 Customer implementation materials

Provide customers with:

- Approved use and prohibited-use guidance;
- System and data-flow documentation;
- Human-oversight instructions;
- Known risks and limitations;
- Performance and subgroup-testing information;
- Logging and retention guidance;
- Candidate-notice templates;
- Complaint and reconsideration processes;
- Information for privacy, equality, employment, security, accessibility, and relevant fundamental-rights assessments;
- Configuration requirements;
- Incident-reporting instructions.

State clearly that these materials support but do not replace the customer’s legal obligations.

### 10.2 Customer messaging examples

**Global positioning**

> “Our platform provides AI-assisted hiring workflows designed to support structured, reviewable human decision-making. Available features, processing locations, and customer responsibilities vary by deployment.”

**EU customer**

> “For relevant EU employment use cases, we provide documentation, human-oversight guidance, logging information, and implementation materials to support your obligations. Your organization remains responsible for its role as employer or deployer and for customer-specific assessments and notices.”

**Regional processing**

> “A regional-processing service profile is available for specified services and locations. The applicable scope covers identified storage, backup, inference, support, logging, and subprocessor pathways; some global support or monitoring functions may be limited.”

**Pending clearance**

> “This use case is not currently available for production deployment in this location while legal and operational requirements are being assessed.”

### 10.3 Candidate-facing message example

> “We use an AI-assisted tool to help organize and review information during this stage of the hiring process. The tool does not make the final hiring decision. A member of the hiring team reviews the relevant information. If you believe information about you is inaccurate or would like to ask how the tool was used, please contact [customer contact].”

The final wording must be adapted to the applicable jurisdiction, process, data, and legal requirements.

### 10.4 Internal messaging examples

**For sales**

> “Do not promise global compliance, bias-free results, local hosting, or fully automated hiring. Confirm the approved country, use case, service profile, and claims before making a commitment.”

**For customer success**

> “If a customer changes the country, hiring purpose, data categories, human-review process, or downstream integration, pause expansion and initiate reassessment.”

**For product and engineering**

> “A material model, threshold, workflow, data, hosting, subprocessor, or API change requires documented legal, privacy, security, fairness, and deployment review before release.”

**For support**

> “Escalate candidate harm, suspected discrimination, automatic rejection, prohibited use, residency deviation, regulator contact, and material data or security issues through the AI incident process.”

## 12. Implementation roadmap

### Phase 1: 0–90 days

- Appoint governance owners and emergency suspension authority;
- Inventory features, models, data, subprocessors, integrations, and locations;
- Complete EU high-risk and prohibited-use screening;
- Identify automated rejection, biometric, emotion, and sensitive-trait functionality;
- Freeze unsupported claims;
- Prioritize countries and use cases;
- Establish deployment statuses;
- Establish deal-desk, incident, and regulatory-monitoring procedures;
- Begin priority local-counsel reviews;
- Establish the EU applicability calendar.

### Phase 2: 3–6 months

- Finalize EU obligations and conformity route;
- Implement policy-engine requirements;
- Add human-review, abstention, logging, API, and kill-switch controls;
- Complete baseline fairness, performance, privacy, transfer, security, and accessibility testing;
- Define localization service profiles;
- Draft modular contracts and country annexes;
- Launch customer assessment materials;
- Train relevant teams;
- Establish claims evidence files.

### Phase 3: 6–12 months

- Complete applicable EU technical documentation, conformity, declaration, marking, and registration work;
- Enforce country and use-case statuses technically;
- Launch post-market monitoring;
- Conduct independent assurance or internal audit;
- Test incident and regulator-response procedures;
- Audit human-review behavior and downstream integrations;
- Publish a scoped compliance center;
- Review insurance, continuity, exit, and procurement requirements.

### Ongoing

- Event-driven and quarterly regulatory reviews;
- Pre-release review of material changes;
- Continuous performance, fairness, security, accessibility, and drift monitoring;
- Annual independent review;
- Periodic local-counsel refresh;
- Annual documentation and training refresh;
- Board-level reporting on residual risks and remediation.

## 13. Metrics

### Readiness and governance

- Percentage of features with current intended-purpose, role, and classification records;
- Percentage of countries and use cases with current approval status;
- Percentage of relevant EU high-risk systems with complete evidence and release gates;
- Open issues by severity and age;
- Time to assess legal and product changes;
- Emergency issues addressed within service levels.

### Technical and product outcomes

- Restricted configurations blocked;
- Automatic-rejection attempts prevented;
- Human-review completion and override patterns;
- Rubber-stamping indicators;
- Error, abstention, and subgroup performance;
- Model drift and rollback events;
- Residency and access-control exceptions;
- Security-vulnerability remediation time;
- Unapproved downstream integrations detected.

### Customer and candidate outcomes

- Completed deployment assessments;
- Administrator training and comprehension;
- Candidate complaints and resolution time;
- Correction and reconsideration outcomes;
- Out-of-purpose use;
- Customer understanding of AI assistance versus employer responsibility;
- Accessibility issues identified and resolved.

### Communication integrity

- Claims with current substantiation;
- Unsupported claims identified and corrected;
- Sales exceptions and approval time;
- Inconsistencies among marketing, contracts, documentation, and product behavior;
- Localized claims reviewed and approved.

The company should not target zero reported incidents. It should measure detection, reporting, containment, remediation, recurrence, and candidate impact.

| Downstream automatic rejection | API controls, metadata, webhook restrictions, contract terms |
| Third-party model changes | Supplier controls, change notifications, reassessment |
| Regulatory change creates urgent exposure | Event-driven monitoring and emergency suspension authority |
| Excessive compliance cost | Standardized profiles, risk-based prioritization, minimum launch controls |
| Customer confusion about responsibility | Modular contracts, implementation guidance, clear messaging |

## 15. Final operating principle

The company should maintain **one global brand and operating experience**, but not one undifferentiated legal or technical configuration.

The durable strategy is:

- One global promise;
- One responsible-AI baseline;
- Feature- and use-case-level assessment;
- Controlled country and service overlays;
- Technical enforcement;
- Evidence-backed documentation and claims;
- Clear provider and customer responsibilities;
- Meaningful human oversight;
- Active monitoring and incident response;
- Restriction or withdrawal where a deployment cannot be lawfully or responsibly supported.

Where legal requirements cannot be reconciled through configuration or service segregation, the company should not improvise an exception. It should restrict or withdraw the affected feature, document the decision, notify affected customers where appropriate, and reassess when the legal or technical position changes.
