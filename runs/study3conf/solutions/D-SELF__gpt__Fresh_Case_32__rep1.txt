# Global Labor-Relations Strategy for a Delivery Platform, 2026

## Executive summary

Adopt a **global conduct floor with locally constituted representation and process rights**.

The platform should apply consistent standards worldwide for:

- Respect for lawful worker voice;
- Non-retaliation and non-interference;
- Safety escalation;
- Meaningful review of significant account actions;
- Accessibility and language support;
- Data protection;
- Accurate communications;
- Documentation, remediation, and escalation.

The company should **not impose one global representation model**. In each market, it must determine:

1. Which worker categories are covered;
2. Which entity or entities are responsible;
3. The applicable worker-status and reclassification rules;
4. Which representatives have legal authority;
5. Whether the required process is information, consultation, negotiation, collective bargaining, co-determination, approval, or voluntary dialogue;
6. What collective-action, competition, privacy, and confidentiality rules apply;
7. What obligations rest with fleet partners or other intermediaries.

Voluntary rider forums and informal collective engagement may supplement—but must never replace—legally required unions, works councils, employee representatives, bargaining, consultation, or protected collective action.

The immediate priorities are to:

- Establish a global labor-relations governance structure;
- Map worker status, representation, responsible entities, and partner exposure by market;
- Create a policy-change gateway for pay, algorithms, deactivation, safety, data, and restructuring decisions;
- Implement anti-retaliation, collective-action, and partner controls;
- Create independent review for retaliation and serious due-process cases;
- Validate high-risk country playbooks with current local legal advice.

This is a strategic governance framework, not legal advice. Market-specific implementation must be validated against current law, authoritative guidance, binding agreements, actual operating practices, and qualified local counsel.

# 1. Global principles

## 1.1 Global hierarchy

The following hierarchy applies:

1. Mandatory local law and regulatory orders;
2. Binding collective agreements, recognition agreements, settlements, codes, permits, and contracts;
3. Global minimum standards;
4. Local voluntary practices.

A global standard may exceed local legal requirements only after assessing whether it could create classification, contractual, interference, competition, privacy, or other legal consequences.

Any exception requires:

- Written local legal analysis;
- A documented business reason;
- Regional approval;
- A review date where feasible.

## 1.2 Worker voice and choice

The company will respect rights recognized and protected by applicable law to:

- Form, join, or support worker organizations;
- Elect or appoint representatives;
- Seek information, consultation, or bargaining;
- Raise individual or collective concerns;
- Participate in lawful collective action;
- Contact regulators or use protected reporting channels.

Where legal status is uncertain, the company should apply the protective controls in this framework while avoiding unsupported legal conclusions about status or representation rights.

## 1.3 No retaliation or interference

The company prohibits direct or indirect retaliation for lawful:

- Organizing or union activity;
- Participation in a statutory body, voluntary forum, or informal collective;
- Complaints about pay, safety, algorithms, data, or account access;
- Protected reporting or lawful public advocacy;
- Collective action where protected by applicable law.

Prohibited conduct includes:

- Selective deactivation or account audits;
- Reduced access to orders;
- Unfavorable allocation;
- Targeted incentive changes;
- Verification delays;
- Organizer surveillance;
- Pressure through fleet partners;
- Blacklisting;
- Exclusion from forums because of criticism or organizing;
- Improper disclosure of organizing or complaint information.

This protection does not immunize violence, threats, harassment, fraud, sabotage, unauthorized access, property damage, or other independently actionable conduct. Enforcement must be based on documented conduct, applied consistently, and reviewed for pretext.

## 1.4 Meaningful due process

For decisions materially affecting access to work, earnings, safety, or legal rights, provide, as applicable:

- Notice;
- Understandable reasons;
- An opportunity to submit information;
- Human review;
- Appeal;
- Correction of inaccurate data;
- A reasoned response to representative proposals;
- Remediation where harm is identified.

The company’s global policy provides for meaningful human review of significant account actions, subject to applicable law, narrow emergency exceptions, technical feasibility, and locally validated procedures. Reviewers must have authority to reconsider and reverse the original decision.

## 1.5 Safety before continuity

Safety concerns may bypass ordinary operational channels. The company may reduce or suspend service where necessary to protect riders, customers, or the public. Continuity plans must not suppress safety complaints or target riders who raise them.

## 1.6 Accessibility and inclusion

Engagement and complaint processes should provide, as locally appropriate:

- Relevant local languages;
- Plain-language materials;
- Disability accommodations;
- Non-digital access;
- Accessible meeting locations;
- Reasonable scheduling;
- Support for workers with limited literacy or digital access.

## 1.7 Data and confidentiality

Worker and representative data must be:

- Collected for legitimate purposes;
- Limited to what is necessary;
- Protected against unauthorized use;
- Retained according to applicable law;
- Shared only after privacy, trade-secret, competition, and security review.

The company should not maintain a centralized organizer database. Pattern monitoring should use privacy-preserving methods and restrict access to authorized legal, compliance, and independent-review personnel.

## 1.8 Competition-law safeguards

Independent riders may be separate economic actors. Discussions of rates, fees, incentives, capacity, exclusivity, geographic allocation, or coordinated commercial conduct may raise competition-law concerns.

Before discussing these subjects with independent contractors or competing rider businesses, obtain local competition-law advice. The company must not:

- Facilitate agreements among competing riders;
- Share one group’s confidential commercial demands with another;
- Exchange unnecessary current or future pricing information;
- Use competition law as a pretext to avoid protected bargaining;
- Assume that confidentiality arrangements resolve competition concerns.

# 2. Worker categories and responsible entities

## 2.1 Covered persons

For each issue, identify whether the affected population includes:

- Employees;
- Platform workers;
- Economically dependent workers;
- Independent contractors or sole traders;
- Fleet-partner or subcontractor personnel;
- Agency personnel;
- Other delivery-related personnel.

Applying a procedural safeguard does not, by itself, determine employment status.

## 2.2 Representation categories

| Category | Required approach |
|---|---|
| Statutory works council or employee representative body | Follow statutory rules on scope, timing, access, confidentiality, consultation, and approval or co-determination |
| Trade union or recognized bargaining representative | Follow recognition and collective-bargaining rules |
| Platform-worker or economically dependent worker body | Confirm the specific local legal framework and authority |
| Health-and-safety committee | Provide required information, consultation, access, and incident follow-up |
| Voluntary rider council or forum | Use only where lawful; operate transparently; never present it as a statutory or exclusive representative |
| Informal collective | Engage respectfully without assuming it lacks legal rights or granting exclusive status |
| Individual channel | Maintain regardless of collective structures |

“Worker council” should not be used as a generic legal term unless expressly defined as a non-legal umbrella term.

## 2.3 Responsible-entity assessment

For each market and issue, determine whether responsibility rests with:

- The global parent;
- The local platform entity;
- A fleet or logistics partner;
- An employment or staffing agency;
- A technology or payment intermediary;
- Multiple entities jointly.

The analysis must consider actual control over dispatch, pay, discipline, account access, data, communications, and deactivation. Contractual allocation alone is not determinative.

# 3. Engagement structures

## 3.1 Formal representatives and unions

Where a lawful representative exists, establish a written protocol covering:

- Representative identity and authority;
- Covered worker population;
- Scope and mandate;
- Information, consultation, bargaining, and approval categories;
- Notice and meeting timelines;
- Required documents;
- Language and accessibility;
- Confidentiality and data controls;
- Access to constituents;
- Minutes and reasoned responses;
- Dispute escalation;
- Lawful compensation or expenses.

The protocol may not narrow statutory rights or imply authority that the representative does not possess.

## 3.2 Voluntary rider councils

A voluntary forum may be established only where local counsel confirms that it will not displace or interfere with independent representation.

Minimum safeguards include:

- Published charter;
- Neutral eligibility rules;
- Transparent selection;
- Confidential voting;
- Independent election administration in higher-risk markets;
- No candidate screening based on criticism, organizing history, or political views;
- Geographic, demographic, and operating-model diversity;
- Term limits and removal rules;
- Constituent access;
- Forum control over part of its agenda;
- Interpretation and accessibility;
- Lawful, transparent compensation or expense support;
- Protected complaint channels;
- Published response tracking;
- A clear statement that the forum is not a union, statutory works council, exclusive representative, or substitute for required bargaining.

Management may provide logistics but may not select “cooperative” representatives, condition platform access on participation, or prevent independent advocacy.

## 3.3 Informal collectives

The company should not require incorporation, union registration, or individual membership lists before hearing a collective’s concerns.

It may ask proportionately about:

- The group’s constituency;
- Whether it speaks for all riders or a defined group;
- Its issue-specific mandate;
- Its spokesperson or decision process;
- Affiliation with another organization.

Use attestations or anonymized estimates wherever possible. Do not collect names, account identifiers, immigration information, or other sensitive data unless necessary and legally approved.

Repeated or substantive engagement requires a **substance-and-frequency review**, particularly where a group:

- Claims broad representation;
- Presents economic demands;
- Requests recognition;
- Receives company support;
- Seeks a written agreement;
- Meets regularly on terms and conditions.

# 4. Process taxonomy

Every material engagement must be classified before it begins.

| Process | Minimum expectation |
|---|---|
| Information | Provide legally required information in the required form and time |
| Consultation | Provide information early enough for views to be considered and give a reasoned response |
| Joint review | Collaboratively assess an issue without implying that agreement is required |
| Negotiation | Exchange proposals through authorized participants with an expectation of reaching an arrangement |
| Collective bargaining | Follow applicable recognition, good-faith, and bargaining rules |
| Co-determination or approval | Do not implement before required agreement or approval |
| Voluntary dialogue | Listen and respond without implying statutory rights or guaranteed agreement |

Before proceeding, identify:

- Legal source;
- Covered population;
- Responsible entity;
- Eligible representative;
- Required information;
- Timing;
- Whether views must be considered;
- Whether agreement or approval is required;
- Confidentiality, data, and competition-law limits;
- Consequences of non-compliance.

A process cannot be made voluntary merely by labeling it voluntary.

# 5. Bargaining and consultation boundaries

## 5.1 Potentially covered subjects

Depending on worker status, representative authority, and local law, the following may require information, consultation, negotiation, bargaining, or approval:

- Pay, fees, deductions, incentives, and payment timing;
- Safety, equipment, insurance, and accident support;
- Dispatch, allocation, ratings, pricing, and performance systems;
- Data collection and monitoring;
- Deactivation, suspension, identity verification, and appeals;
- Availability or scheduling expectations;
- Customer conduct and complaints;
- Discrimination, harassment, and accommodation;
- Contractor and fleet-partner structures;
- Market entry, exit, restructuring, or major service changes.

## 5.2 Reserved management decisions

Unless local law or a binding agreement provides otherwise, management retains responsibility for:

- Product strategy;
- Customer and public safety controls;
- Anti-fraud measures;
- Technical architecture;
- Capital allocation;
- Vendor selection;
- Market-entry and exit decisions;
- Individual account decisions, subject to due process.

Reserved authority does not eliminate information, consultation, bargaining, approval, notice, or standstill requirements. No manager may implement a decision until the policy-change gateway confirms that implementation is legally permissible.

## 5.3 Meeting and agenda controls

Before meetings involving independent contractors or mixed worker populations, review:

- Worker status;
- Participants;
- Economic subjects;
- Competitively sensitive information;
- Confidentiality;
- Applicable representation rights;
- Approved attendees and materials;
- Negotiator authority.

Mixed-population meetings require documented justification and legal approval where they could confuse rights, expose confidential information, facilitate contractor coordination, or undermine statutory representation.

---

# 6. Policy-change gateway

No material change to pay, incentives, algorithms, deactivation, safety, data, verification, partner structures, restructuring, or market operations may launch without documenting:

1. Affected worker categories;
2. Factual status assumptions;
3. Responsible entity;
4. Applicable representatives;
5. Required information, consultation, bargaining, or approval;
6. Timing and any standstill requirements;
7. Competition-law constraints;
8. Privacy, trade-secret, and cybersecurity limits;
9. Retaliation and disparate-impact risks;
10. Safety consequences;
11. Appeal, transition, and remediation measures;
12. Communications and escalation plans.

The gateway must issue one of five outcomes:

- **Approved**;
- **Approved with mitigation**;
- **Pilot only**;
- **Delayed pending consultation, bargaining, or approval**;
- **Escalated or prohibited pending legal resolution**.

High-risk changes must not proceed based solely on a preliminary legal matrix.

# 7. Collective-action protocol

Each market must maintain a locally validated protocol addressing:

- Strikes;
- Coordinated log-offs or refusals to accept work;
- Slowdowns;
- Picketing;
- Demonstrations;
- Online organizing;
- Boycotts;
- Blockades;
- Secondary activity;
- Company service reductions or suspensions;
- Substitute or replacement delivery capacity.

When collective action is threatened or occurring, the company must:

1. Identify affected worker categories and responsible entities;
2. Obtain labor and competition-law advice;
3. Assess whether conduct is protected, unprotected, or legally uncertain;
4. Establish a worker-contact channel;
5. Communicate factual, non-threatening information;
6. Protect safety and public access;
7. Apply continuity plans neutrally;
8. Prohibit targeted deactivation, allocation changes, incentive manipulation, or surveillance;
9. Separate lawful protest from violence, threats, fraud, sabotage, unauthorized access, or property damage;
10. Preserve relevant records and document decisions.

Continuity measures must not use organizer or participant status as a selection factor. The company must not characterize collective action as unlawful or anticompetitive without legal advice.

# 8. Regional applications

## 8.1 European Union

Treat the EU as a collection of national regimes within a broader European framework. Validate:

- National worker-status rules;
- Works councils and employee representatives;
- Trade unions and collective bargaining;
- Platform-work implementation measures;
- Algorithmic-management requirements;
- Health and safety;
- Data protection;
- Collective action;
- National implementation dates and transitional rules;
- Possible cross-border or group-level representation;
- Local subsidiary, fleet, and partner roles;
- Thresholds for representative bodies and collective redundancies.

Use existing statutory bodies, unions, safety committees, and legally recognized platform-worker bodies. Maintain separate voluntary forums for populations not covered by a mandatory structure, where lawful.

Do not use a voluntary survey or rider forum as a substitute for a statutory process.

## 8.2 Latin America

Treat internal market categories as risk classifications, not legal conclusions. Assess separately:

- Control, dependency, personal service, substitution, exclusivity, pricing, ratings, deactivation, equipment, and fleet-partner facts;
- Labor, tax, social-security, and occupational-injury exposure;
- Union, bargaining, and collective-action rights while status is disputed;
- Divergent tests used by labor, tax, social-security, and regulatory authorities;
- Municipal licensing and delivery rules;
- Partner and joint-liability exposure;
- Competition-law treatment of contractor collectives;
- Transition options for employee or intermediate-status models.

Managers must not make unapproved statements about rider status. Legal uncertainty must not be used to refuse substantive engagement or mislead workers or regulators.

## 8.3 Southeast Asia

For each market, determine whether a code or standard is:

- Statutory;
- Government guidance;
- A license or permit condition;
- An industry requirement;
- Contractually incorporated;
- Administratively monitored;
- Entirely voluntary.

Each code implementation plan should cover:

- Scope and covered persons;
- Safety;
- Earnings and payment transparency;
- Data use;
- Account actions;
- Grievances;
- Consultation;
- Audits and reporting;
- Complaints and remediation;
- Partner obligations;
- Public representations.

Communications must distinguish commitments that are legally required, code-based, contractual, or voluntary.

## 8.4 Informal city collectives

Use a standard protocol with:

- Named contact;
- Urgent and ordinary meeting channels;
- Proportionate mandate verification;
- Issue classification;
- Agendas and minutes;
- Response deadlines;
- Non-retaliation controls;
- Neutral treatment of competing groups;
- Safety and site-access rules;
- Escalation to country and regional leadership.

The company may use neutral criteria—affected population, issue relevance, demonstrated mandate, urgency, and safety—to manage competing groups, but should not grant exclusivity without legal basis.

---

# 9. Restructuring, market exit, and mass account actions

Before a market closure, city withdrawal, major outsourcing, fleet-partner replacement, substantial service reduction, collective redundancy, or mass account action, assess:

- Affected worker categories;
- Responsible entities;
- Notice periods;
- Consultation, bargaining, approval, or government-notification requirements;
- Collective-dismissal or equivalent rules;
- Partner and contractor consequences;
- Transition, payment, severance, or remediation obligations;
- Communications and records preservation;
- Neutrality of continuity and replacement measures.

No restructuring plan may use organizing status, complaint history, or collective-action participation as a selection factor.

# 10. Due process, remedies, and systemic review

## 10.1 Account actions

Significant account actions should generally include:

- Notice, unless immediate action is necessary;
- Meaningful reasons;
- An opportunity to submit evidence or correct data;
- Human review;
- A time-bound appeal;
- Restoration of access, payment, ratings, or eligibility where appropriate.

Actions involving organizers, representatives, complainants, or collective-action participants require enhanced independent review.

## 10.2 Individual remedies

Depending on facts and law, remedies may include:

- Restoration of account access;
- Payment or incentive correction;
- Restoration of ratings or eligibility;
- Correction or removal of inaccurate data;
- Reimbursement of improperly imposed costs;
- Corrective communications;
- Partner-directed remediation.

## 10.3 Systemic review triggers

Trigger systemic review for:

- Repeated similar complaints;
- Representative submissions;
- Unusual geographic or demographic concentration;
- Sudden changes following organizing;
- Serious safety events;
- Regulatory inquiries;
- Significant reversal rates;
- Evidence of partner retaliation;
- Multi-city collective action.

Systemic remedies may include algorithm or policy changes, reprocessing accounts, retroactive payment correction, representative consultation, independent monitoring, partner remediation, and additional training.

# 11. Governance and escalation

## 11.1 Global Labor Relations Council

The council will:

- Maintain global standards and the legal matrix;
- Approve high-risk exceptions;
- Review major disputes;
- Coordinate regional implementation;
- Monitor metrics;
- Commission independent reviews;
- Report material risk to senior management and the board.

It does not replace local counsel, statutory bodies, or bargaining representatives.

## 11.2 Independent worker-voice oversight

Establish an ombudsperson or independent ethics/compliance function with:

- Direct access to serious-case board reporting;
- Authority to access relevant account, partner, and investigation records;
- Authority to require interim protection or reconsideration;
- Independence from the operations team that made the original decision;
- Responsibility for retaliation, interference, serious due-process, and systemic account-action cases.

## 11.3 Escalation ladder

1. City or local operations;
2. Country labor-relations owner;
3. Regional lead and counsel;
4. Global Labor Relations Council;
5. Executive crisis committee;
6. Board or board committee where thresholds require.

Critical triggers include serious safety events, alleged retaliation, widespread deactivation, multi-city collective action, government investigations, significant algorithmic harm, reclassification proceedings, major service disruption, and material partner misconduct.

Each stage requires a named owner, response deadline, communication lead, written decision, and follow-up date.

## 11.4 Records and legal holds

Maintain controlled records of:

- Consultation and bargaining;
- Representative communications;
- Forum selection;
- Account actions and appeals;
- Algorithm versions and impact reviews;
- Complaints and investigations;
- Collective-action events;
- Partner conduct;
- Remediation;
- Legal advice and approvals, subject to privilege rules.

Define retention periods, access controls, audit logs, and legal-hold triggers. A legal hold should apply upon litigation, regulatory inquiry, serious complaint, credible retaliation allegation, or other defined trigger.

# 13. Implementation phases

## Phase 1: 0–30 days — interim controls

- Appoint global, regional, country, and city owners;
- Issue non-retaliation and anti-interference instructions;
- Review or pause high-risk pending changes;
- Triage active disputes, organizing, and safety concerns;
- Establish protected reporting;
- Preserve relevant records;
- Identify markets requiring immediate legal validation.

## Phase 2: 31–90 days — priority-market controls

- Produce a preliminary legal and representation matrix;
- Map representatives, collectives, regulators, partners, and responsible entities;
- Validate highest-risk market playbooks;
- Train managers and partner managers;
- Implement the policy-change gateway;
- Establish collective-action, restructuring, and partner-retaliation protocols;
- Create service levels for urgent safety, retaliation, appeal, recognition, and collective-action matters.

## Phase 3: 90–180 days — institutional implementation

- Complete priority-country legal validation;
- Establish formal consultation and bargaining protocols;
- Launch only legally reviewed voluntary forums;
- Implement data-sharing classifications;
- Deploy grievance, appeal, and retaliation systems;
- Add partner audit and remediation controls;
- Introduce independent account-action review;
- Establish representative-support and expense controls.

## Phase 4: 6–18 months — testing and improvement

- Conduct independent reviews of forum legitimacy and effectiveness;
- Run collective-action, safety, reclassification, restructuring, and regulator-investigation exercises;
- Integrate labor review into product, market-entry, and restructuring governance;
- Reassess markets affected by new law, litigation, or codes;
- Publish appropriately qualified progress reporting;
- Obtain worker and representative feedback on the framework itself.

# 14. Metrics

Metrics must measure access, fairness, and outcomes—not complaint suppression.

## Legal and governance

- Markets with current validated playbooks;
- Material changes reviewed before launch;
- Required processes completed on time;
- Missed consultation or bargaining deadlines;
- Open classification and representation issues;
- Partner compliance;
- Training and recordkeeping completion;
- Legal holds and remediation completed on time.

## Worker voice

- Representation coverage by worker category;
- Participation and accessibility rates;
- Proposal-response time;
- Proposals receiving reasoned responses;
- Worker and representative confidence;
- Forum-interference allegations;
- Candidate exclusion complaints;
- Diversity and geographic coverage.

## Due process and safety

- Human-review rates and reversal rates;
- Appeal timeliness;
- Payment corrections;
- Safety-response time;
- Repeat complaints;
- Retaliation allegations and substantiation;
- Account-action patterns following protected activity;
- Systemic reviews completed;
- Partner-retaliation incidents.

## Operational and reputational

- Collective actions and duration;
- Service disruption;
- Regulator inquiries;
- Litigation;
- Partner incidents;
- Corrective-action completion;
- Recurrence of similar disputes;
- Restructuring or market-exit process failures.

An increase in complaints may indicate improved access. Metrics should be interpreted with worker trust, resolution quality, recurrence, independent-review findings, and evidence of under-reporting.

# 15. Local-law validation caveat

Every high-risk market playbook must validate:

- Worker-status tests and factual assumptions;
- Covered worker categories;
- Responsible legal entities;
- Union, works-council, and representative rights;
- Recognition and employer-interference risks;
- Information, consultation, bargaining, approval, and standstill rules;
- Collective-action and protest protections;
- Replacement and continuity rules;
- Restructuring and collective-redundancy requirements;
- Anti-retaliation and discrimination protections;
- Algorithmic-management and account-review requirements;
- Data, confidentiality, and representative-access rules;
- Competition-law treatment of independent-worker collectives;
- Fleet-partner and joint-liability exposure;
- Code, permit, and government-guidance status;
- Representative compensation and election-support rules;
- Language, accessibility, notice, and recordkeeping requirements;
- Dispute-resolution and regulator-notification duties;
- Public communications and classification exposure;
- Pending legislation, litigation, and enforcement developments.

Each entry should identify its source and date, assumptions, confidence level, required controls, responsible counsel, open questions, and next review date.

This framework should be treated as a strategic standard, not a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal advice.

# Final company position

> The platform will comply with applicable labor and representation laws and will not impose a single global representation model. It will distinguish employees, platform workers, economically dependent workers, independent contractors, and partner personnel, and will identify the responsible entity and lawful representative for each issue. The platform will engage constructively with recognized representatives, informal collectives, and individual riders while preserving the distinct legal rights of each population. It will maintain global minimum standards for safety, non-retaliation, non-interference, accessibility, data protection, meaningful human review, and due process. Voluntary forums will supplement—not replace—legally required representation, consultation, bargaining, or collective-action rights. Every material change affecting pay, safety, algorithms, data, account access, restructuring, or working conditions will undergo local worker-status, representation, competition, privacy, retaliation, and partner-liability review before implementation.

This model provides global consistency without treating EU representative bodies, Latin American reclassification processes, Southeast Asian codes, and informal city collectives as legally equivalent. It gives management a practical rule: identify the affected population and responsible entity, determine the applicable legal pathway, engage the correct representative or collective, protect lawful activity, document and remediate outcomes, and escalate unresolved risk through accountable governance.
