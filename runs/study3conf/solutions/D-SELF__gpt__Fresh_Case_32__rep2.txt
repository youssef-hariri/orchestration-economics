# Global Labor-Relations Strategy for a Delivery Platform — 2026

## Executive summary

The platform should implement a **global labor-relations control system**, not a universal worker-council or bargaining model.

The company should apply common global safeguards for:

- Freedom of association and lawful collective action;
- Non-retaliation;
- Safety, dignity, and inclusion;
- Fair account-access, deactivation, and appeal processes;
- Appropriate transparency and human oversight of algorithmic management;
- Accessible worker voice; and
- Timely, lawful engagement.

Local implementation must be determined separately for each jurisdiction, platform entity, operating model, and materially distinct worker segment. Country annexes must establish:

- Worker classification and reclassification exposure;
- Representative authority and constituency;
- Information, consultation, bargaining, or co-determination duties;
- Competition-law constraints;
- Data-protection and confidentiality requirements;
- Social-security, tax, insurance, and licensing obligations;
- Remedies and service levels;
- Effective dates and transitional rules; and
- Financial and operational consequences.

The governing standard is **equivalent respect, protection, process, and access to voice—not identical structures in every market**.

### Immediate executive decisions

Within the first 90 days, leadership should:

1. Establish the Global Labor Relations Council and approval matrix.
2. Identify priority countries, worker segments, and high-risk systems.
3. Issue binding freedom-of-association and non-retaliation requirements.
4. Require legal and worker-impact review before material changes to pay, allocation, monitoring, ratings, or account access.
5. Begin engagement with statutory representatives, unions, associations, and legitimate informal collectives.
6. Fund local legal, labor-relations, translation, mediation, data-audit, and financial-planning capacity.
7. Require reclassification and collective-arrangement scenarios in high-risk markets.

# 1. Global principles and minimum standards

These standards apply globally unless local law or a valid collective agreement requires stronger or different protections.

## 1.1 Freedom of association and collective activity

The platform must not interfere with, discriminate against, or retaliate against workers or representatives because they:

- Form, join, or support a union, council, association, or collective;
- Raise concerns collectively;
- Serve as representatives;
- Participate in lawful collective action;
- Speak publicly about working conditions; or
- Use courts, regulators, labor authorities, or other external channels.

The platform must not require disclosure of union membership, collective membership, or organizing activity unless legally necessary, proportionate, and appropriately protected.

A company-created forum may supplement, but may not replace, a statutory representative body or exclusive bargaining representative.

## 1.2 Non-retaliation and privacy

Prohibited adverse actions include:

- Deactivation or suspension;
- Reduced order access or priority;
- Changes to incentives or payments;
- Adverse ratings;
- Increased audits or identity checks;
- Account restrictions;
- Disciplinary action; and
- Other measures affecting earnings or access to work.

The platform must not:

- Routinely collect protected-activity information;
- Create general profiles of organizers;
- Use inferred organizing activity in fraud, allocation, performance, or enforcement systems; or
- Provide managers with unnecessary access to such information.

Where adverse action closely follows collective activity or a representative complaint, the company should conduct a privacy-safe independent review using objective evidence, such as timing, enforcement anomalies, audit results, comparable cases, and representative complaints.

## 1.3 Fair process

Subject to local law, workers should receive:

- Clear contractual and operating terms;
- Notice of material changes;
- Understandable reasons for serious adverse decisions;
- An opportunity to respond;
- Human review of significant automated decisions;
- Accessible appeals;
- Restoration or correction where error is established; and
- Corrective payment or removal of improper penalties.

Alleged retaliation, serious safety concerns, mass account errors, and systemic payment failures require expedited handling.

## 1.4 Safety, dignity, and inclusion

Global standards should address:

- Road and personal safety;
- Extreme weather;
- Violence, harassment, and discrimination;
- Accident and emergency reporting;
- Customer and merchant misconduct;
- Protective equipment where appropriate;
- Gender-based violence;
- Disability and accessibility;
- Language access;
- Immigration-related vulnerability;
- Shared-account and substitute-rider practices; and
- Workers with limited digital access.

# 2. Legal operating architecture

## 2.1 Country and worker-segment legal matrices

Each market must maintain a current legal matrix, validated by qualified local counsel, covering:

1. Contracting, employing, and operating entities;
2. Actual platform practices and degree of control;
3. Worker classification and reclassification theories;
4. Statutory representation rights;
5. Exclusive or subject-specific representatives;
6. Works-council and health-and-safety thresholds;
7. Information, consultation, bargaining, and co-determination triggers;
8. Competition-law restrictions and exemptions;
9. Data-protection and confidentiality requirements;
10. Social-security, insurance, tax, and licensing obligations;
11. Dispute forums, remedies, and limitation periods;
12. Record-retention obligations;
13. Effective dates and transitional rules; and
14. Current legislation, case law, regulator guidance, and enforcement trends.

Classification must be based on actual practices rather than contract labels. Reviews should consider pricing control, account restrictions, performance management, scheduling influence, route direction, monitoring, incentives, and disciplinary mechanisms.

High-risk matrices must be updated at least quarterly and whenever law, enforcement, or operating practices materially change.

## 2.2 Representation and constituency determination

Before every formal engagement, the platform must document:

- The worker constituency;
- Worker status and applicable rights;
- Relevant entity and geography;
- Representative body;
- Whether representation is exclusive, concurrent, or subject-specific;
- Subjects within the body’s authority;
- Whether the process is information, consultation, bargaining, or co-determination; and
- Whether any resulting instrument is binding.

The representation hierarchy is a **non-bypass rule**, not a universal legal priority order:

1. Statutory works council, worker council, health-and-safety body, union, or exclusive representative;
2. Representative body created or protected by platform-work legislation;
3. Legally recognized union or independent representative organization;
4. Independent association or informal collective where no mandatory or exclusive body applies; and
5. Direct worker engagement, subject to non-bypass rules.

Employees, agency workers, dependent contractors, genuine independent businesses, and other platform workers must not be combined in one process where their legal rights or competition-law treatment differ.

# 3. Regional operating models

## 3.1 European Union

The platform should use **Member State legal annexes**, not a generic EU worker-council framework.

Each annex should distinguish among:

- National works councils;
- Trade unions and bargaining representatives;
- Health-and-safety representatives;
- Platform-work-specific bodies;
- European Works Councils, where applicable;
- Data-protection consultation rights; and
- Restructuring, transfer, redundancy, and other national procedures.

A European Works Council, where applicable, does not replace national representation or consultation bodies.

The platform should track national implementation of Directive (EU) 2024/2831, including:

- The rebuttable presumption of employment;
- Procedures for establishing or challenging employment status;
- Algorithmic-management transparency;
- Human oversight of automated decisions;
- Information and consultation concerning automated systems; and
- Protection against retaliation.

Each annex must record implementation status, effective and transitional dates, covered workers and entities, relevant guidance, and the responsible legal owner.

Before a material change, counsel must determine whether the process is:

- Information only;
- Information and consultation;
- Collective bargaining;
- Prior agreement;
- Co-determination; or
- Individual notice or consent.

The platform must not present a predetermined decision as consultation or implement a legally restricted change before the required process is complete, absent a narrowly defined emergency exception.

Management rights remain subordinate to mandatory law, collective agreements, representation rights, data-protection rules, and health-and-safety duties.

## 3.2 Latin America

Latin America must be managed through country-specific plans. Priority annexes should separately address Brazil, Mexico, Colombia, Chile, Argentina, Peru, and other material markets.

Each plan should cover:

- Employment, subordination, and economic-dependence tests;
- Platform-specific legislation and pending reforms;
- Court and regulator positions;
- Collective and association rights;
- Social-security, tax, insurance, and occupational-injury exposure;
- Joint-employer and agency risks;
- Retroactive wages, benefits, taxes, severance, and contributions;
- Limitation periods;
- Record preservation;
- Migrant-worker considerations; and
- Enforcement trends.

Classification must be assessed by country, entity, operating model, and materially distinct worker segment using actual practices and current law.

High-risk markets should prepare scenarios for:

1. Continued contractor operations with enhanced protections;
2. A statutory intermediate category, where one exists;
3. Employee classification;
4. A hybrid workforce model;
5. A collective or sectoral arrangement; and
6. A government-directed transition.

Each scenario should address pay, benefits, social security, insurance, working time, taxation, account continuity, equipment, onboarding, termination, representation, pricing, service continuity, historic liabilities, financial reserves, and investor or regulatory disclosure.

The platform must not make superficial changes merely to create an appearance of independence. Any operating-model change must be genuine, documented, and legally reviewed.

## 3.3 Southeast Asia

Each market must classify relevant rules as:

- Binding legislation;
- Government regulation or licensing condition;
- Enforceable code or guideline;
- Tripartite standard;
- Industry code; or
- Voluntary company commitment.

Country annexes should address worker status, social protection, accident coverage, insurance, representation rights, licensing, reporting, dispute mechanisms, effective dates, and transition periods.

Singapore’s Platform Workers Act, for example, requires separate analysis of statutory protections, representation structures, and social-protection obligations. It must not be treated as merely voluntary self-regulation.

Even where formal bargaining duties are limited, safety, data, licensing, consumer, wage, and anti-retaliation laws may restrict unilateral action. Voluntary consultation should be used for material changes affecting earnings, access to work, safety, monitoring, or dignity.

# 4. Engagement structures

## 4.1 Statutory bodies and bargaining representatives

Where a statutory body or exclusive representative exists, the platform must:

- Engage through the legally authorized body;
- Follow applicable election, access, notice, information, confidentiality, and timing rules;
- Avoid direct bargaining with represented workers on covered subjects;
- Avoid individual side deals intended to undermine collective representation;
- Provide relevant information in usable form;
- Bargain in good faith where required; and
- Document proposals, responses, decisions, and implementation dates.

The platform must not engage in surface bargaining, deliberate delay, unilateral implementation before bargaining is complete, or unjustified refusal to provide relevant information.

## 4.2 Independent worker organizations

Where no exclusive representative exists but an independent union, association, or organization has practical legitimacy, the platform may establish a written protocol covering:

- Constituency and geographic scope;
- Representative mandate;
- Meeting frequency;
- Information rights;
- Consultation or bargaining subjects;
- Access to workers;
- Translation and accessibility;
- Confidentiality;
- Dispute escalation;
- Non-retaliation; and
- Review and termination.

Recognition must not depend on abandoning public criticism, collective action, independent funding, or legal claims.

## 4.3 Informal rider collectives

Informal collectives may be engaged where they have practical legitimacy, but legitimacy does not automatically confer bargaining authority.

Each forum must identify whether it is:

- **Advisory:** provides views without formal decision rights;
- **Consultative:** receives information, provides views, and receives a response before implementation;
- **Joint decision-making:** has authority only where lawfully required or agreed; or
- **Bargaining-capable:** has legal authority or a valid agreement granting that authority.

The platform must not:

- Select representatives;
- Approve candidates or viewpoints;
- Require membership lists without a lawful basis;
- Condition access on favorable positions;
- Present a forum as representing all riders without a mandate;
- Use a forum to bypass a statutory representative; or
- Require a collective to abandon public or lawful collective action.

Collectives should control their own membership and selection processes. Where competing groups claim authority, use an independent facilitator and document only the constituency and mandate relevant to the specific engagement. Documentation must not become a recognition test or control over the collective’s internal governance.

## 4.4 Direct worker voice

Direct channels should remain available, including:

- In-app feedback;
- Anonymous surveys;
- Focus groups;
- Safety reporting;
- Town halls;
- Pilot testing;
- Multilingual helplines;
- Independent ombuds services; and
- Individual appeals.

Direct engagement must not be used to bypass an exclusive representative or obtain individual acceptance of a matter subject to collective bargaining or mandatory consultation.

# 5. Consultation, bargaining, and management boundaries

## 5.1 Process definitions

Every process must identify its legal category:

- **Information:** disclosure without a duty to obtain views or agreement;
- **Consultation:** timely exchange of views with a genuine opportunity to influence the outcome;
- **Bargaining:** good-faith negotiation with an authorized representative;
- **Agreement:** binding or nonbinding according to its terms and governing law;
- **Co-determination:** representative participation or agreement required before implementation; and
- **Worker voice:** a general policy term that does not itself create legal rights.

“Engagement” must not be used to obscure a mandatory process.

## 5.2 Subjects normally open to engagement

Subject to local law and representative authority, the platform should be prepared to consult or bargain over:

- Pay and minimum earnings;
- Incentives, deductions, and payment timing;
- Fuel, equipment, insurance, and maintenance costs;
- Account suspension and deactivation;
- Ratings and complaint systems;
- Safety and extreme-weather protocols;
- Harassment and discrimination;
- Data collection and monitoring;
- Algorithmic management;
- Access to work and allocation systems;
- Classification transitions;
- Social protection;
- Training; and
- Material operational changes affecting working conditions.

## 5.3 Management rights

The platform may retain responsibility for:

- Corporate and commercial strategy;
- Product development;
- Technology architecture;
- Customer and merchant offerings;
- Brand standards;
- Service-area decisions;
- Security and fraud controls; and
- Emergency safety measures.

These rights are not absolute. Their implementation may trigger consultation, bargaining, co-determination, data-protection, safety, or employment-law duties.

# 6. Competition-law controls

Before participating in industry-wide or multi-platform discussions involving rates, earnings, service areas, incentives, acceptance behavior, or coordinated action, the platform must obtain jurisdiction-specific competition-law advice.

Controls must provide that:

- Worker representatives may discuss their own working conditions, subject to local law;
- The platform must not exchange competitively sensitive information with competing platforms;
- Multi-platform meetings require counsel-approved agendas, attendance, and minutes;
- The platform must not facilitate rate, territory, customer, or acceptance coordination among genuine independent businesses;
- Agreements involving self-employed workers must be assessed for applicable labor-law exemptions;
- Sector-wide earnings or benefit discussions require legal review; and
- Lawful collective action must not automatically be classified as fraud, collusion, or account abuse.

The platform must withdraw from discussions that exceed approved legal boundaries.

# 7. Worker Impact and Legal Review

## 7.1 Materiality

A change is presumed material if it is reasonably likely to affect:

- Earnings or deductions;
- Access to work or order allocation;
- Ratings or performance measures;
- Monitoring or identity verification;
- Deactivation or appeals;
- Worker classification;
- Working time;
- Safety;
- Representative rights; or
- A substantial number of workers or a particularly vulnerable group.

A documented fast track may be used for genuinely minor changes. Cumulative changes must be assessed together, and local legal requirements override the global threshold.

## 7.2 Mandatory launch gate

No material change to pay, incentives, allocation, ratings, monitoring, identity verification, fraud detection, deactivation, or performance assessment may launch without a completed Worker Impact and Legal Review.

The review must identify:

- Worker-status implications;
- Applicable information, consultation, bargaining, or co-determination duties;
- Required representative or regulator engagement;
- Data-protection requirements;
- Competition-law risks;
- Disparate impacts;
- Human-review and appeal requirements;
- Worker communications and translations;
- Pilot and rollback criteria;
- Decision owner;
- Completion evidence; and
- Post-launch monitoring.

If required consultation is incomplete, disputed, conducted with an unauthorized body, or materially deficient, implementation must pause unless a lawful emergency exception applies.

## 7.3 Emergency exception

An emergency exception is available only where immediate action is necessary for safety, security, or legal compliance, no reasonable alternative exists, and delay would create material harm.

It requires:

- Written necessity and proportionality findings;
- Executive authorization;
- Defined duration;
- Temporary safeguards;
- Notice where feasible;
- Retrospective consultation or review where required;
- Council notification within a defined period;
- Post-event audit; and
- Rollback or remediation criteria.

Ordinary commercial urgency, routine fraud-model tuning, release deadlines, and operational convenience are not emergencies.

# 8. Governance and accountability

## 8.1 Global Labor Relations Council

The Council should include labor and employment legal, operations, product, algorithm governance, trust and safety, data protection, compliance, public policy, communications, finance, tax, insurance, and regional leadership.

It should:

- Set global minimum standards;
- Approve country labor-relations plans;
- Review high-risk classification and technology changes;
- Maintain a global issue register;
- Oversee retaliation investigations;
- Monitor regulatory developments;
- Approve global exceptions; and
- Report material risks to the board.

## 8.2 Decision-rights framework

| Decision | Accountable owner | Required review or approval |
|---|---|---|
| Country legal annex | Country legal owner | Local counsel and regional labor lead |
| Worker classification | Country legal and business owners | Employment counsel, finance, tax, insurance, operations; Council for high-risk cases |
| Constituency and representative authority | Country labor-relations owner | Local counsel; independent facilitator where disputed |
| Consultation-trigger analysis | Labor legal owner | Local counsel and affected representative where legally required |
| Worker Impact Review | Product and labor-relations owners | Data protection, competition, safety, and local counsel as applicable |
| Material launch | Product executive | Completed review; Council approval for high-risk changes |
| Collective agreement or financial commitment | Country and labor-relations owners | Legal, finance, tax, insurance, regional lead, and Council as required |
| Emergency implementation | Designated executive | Written legal basis and Council-chair or delegate approval |
| Global-policy exception | Regional lead | Local counsel and Council; board notice for material risk |
| Serious retaliation allegation | Independent investigation owner | Legal, ethics, and Council oversight |
| Board notification | General counsel or Council chair | Material investigations, fatalities, systemic exposure, or major disputes |

Local operating leaders may not override a legal stop, required consultation, launch gate, or independent-investigation requirement.

## 8.3 Enforceability

Global standards must be incorporated into:

- Binding internal policies;
- Manager certification and performance objectives;
- Disciplinary procedures;
- Supplier, franchise, and acquisition controls;
- Audit programs;
- Product-launch procedures;
- Reporting obligations; and
- Remediation plans.

Repeated or serious violations require escalation, corrective action, and potential disciplinary consequences.

# 9. Dispute resolution, service levels, and remedies

## 9.1 Escalation levels

1. **Local resolution:** payment errors, app issues, safety hazards, merchant disputes, and individual account matters.
2. **City or country review:** systemic complaints, representative concerns, repeated deactivations, or collective issues.
3. **Regional review:** cross-border disputes, regulatory developments, coordinated action, or classification concerns.
4. **Global executive review:** serious retaliation allegations, fatalities, major collective disputes, investigations, or systemic failures.

## 9.2 Governance service levels

Subject to local law, safety, fraud, privacy, and due-process requirements:

| Case type | Acknowledgment | Interim review | Final decision or action plan |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Immediate safety risk | Same day | Same day | Action or status update within 24 hours |
| Alleged retaliation | 1 business day | 2 business days | Finding or substantive update within 10 business days |
| Mass account or payment error | 1 business day | 2 business days | Remediation plan within 5 business days |
| Serious deactivation | 1 business day | 3 business days | Decision or status update within 10 business days |
| Standard individual dispute | 2 business days | As locally prescribed | Resolution within 15 business days where practicable |
| Systemic representative complaint | 2 business days | 5 business days | Written response and action plan within 15 business days |

These are governance targets, not universal legal deadlines. Missed targets require escalation, explanation, and documented remediation.

## 9.3 Independent channels

Workers and representatives must retain access to:

- Ethics and retaliation channels;
- Independent ombuds review;
- Mediation;
- Lawful arbitration;
- Labor authorities;
- Courts; and
- Other statutory mechanisms.

Use of an external channel must not result in adverse treatment.

## 9.4 Remedies

Where error or wrongdoing is established, remedies may include:

- Reinstatement or restoration of account access;
- Back pay or corrected incentives;
- Removal of retaliatory ratings or penalties;
- Reconsideration of a policy or algorithm;
- Corrective communications;
- Representative notification where appropriate;
- Independent investigation; and
- Manager discipline.

Systemic failures require root-cause analysis and corrective-action plans covering relevant policies, systems, managers, and affected workers.

# 10. Information, access, privacy, and records

The platform should establish representative-access standards covering:

- In-app, email, SMS, or physical communications;
- Secure representative portals where appropriate;
- Lawful access to workers;
- Translation and accessibility;
- Meeting scheduling;
- Privacy and cybersecurity; and
- Neutral reimbursement of interpretation, accessibility, or participation expenses where lawful.

Support must be transparent, proportionate, and never conditioned on favorable views, silence, or continued recognition.

The platform must retain, for legally required periods:

- Classification analyses;
- Consultation and bargaining materials;
- Meeting minutes and responses;
- Worker Impact Reviews;
- Algorithmic-impact assessments;
- Deactivation and appeal records;
- Collective complaints;
- Training records;
- Remediation evidence; and
- Regulatory correspondence.

Protected-activity data must have defined collection, access, retention, deletion, logging, and investigation controls. Litigation, investigation, and regulatory holds override ordinary deletion schedules.

# 11. Financial and commercial integration

Each material classification, representation, or collective-arrangement scenario must be reviewed by finance, tax, treasury, insurance, commercial, investor-relations, and operations teams.

The assessment must include base, adverse, and transition cases covering:

- Pay and benefit costs;
- Social-security and tax liabilities;
- Insurance availability and premiums;
- Historic-liability reserves;
- Payroll and accounting conversion;
- Merchant and customer pricing;
- Cash-flow impacts;
- Service continuity;
- Investor disclosure;
- Systems requirements; and
- Operational staffing.

Major classification changes, collective agreements, or legally required earnings guarantees should not be approved without a documented financial and operational plan.

# 12. Implementation roadmap

## First 90 days

1. Establish the Global Labor Relations Council and regional leads.
2. Complete country, entity, worker-status, and representation mapping.
3. Rank priority markets, worker segments, and high-risk systems.
4. Issue binding freedom-of-association and non-retaliation rules.
5. Review recent deactivations and enforcement actions for retaliation indicators.
6. Prohibit nonessential high-impact changes without legal and worker-impact review.
7. Establish independent escalation channels and interim-remedy procedures.
8. Begin dialogue with statutory bodies, unions, associations, and collectives.
9. Create the labor-relations and legal-change register.
10. Approve the RACI, exception, emergency, and representation-dispute protocols.
11. Begin financial, tax, insurance, payroll, and systems planning for reclassification scenarios.
12. Train senior, product, operations, and frontline managers.

## Three to six months

1. Finalize country legal annexes and engagement protocols.
2. Implement EU Member State consultation calendars.
3. Complete reclassification scenarios in priority Latin American markets.
4. Translate Southeast Asian legal and code requirements into operational controls.
5. Establish rider forums where informal collectives have practical legitimacy.
6. Make Worker Impact Reviews mandatory launch gates.
7. Publish worker-facing rights, representation, and appeal information.
8. Establish mediation and independent-review capacity.
9. Adopt representative-access and information-sharing standards.
10. Implement document-retention and litigation-hold procedures.
11. Add labor-relations controls to procurement, franchise, and acquisition processes.
12. Set service-level targets, metric owners, and escalation thresholds.

## Six to twelve months

1. Finalize lawful collective agreements or consultation arrangements.
2. Audit algorithmic-management systems and account enforcement.
3. Test responses to coordinated disputes and regulatory investigations.
4. Review actual operating practices against classification assumptions.
5. Publish an aggregate worker-voice and labor-relations report.
6. Conduct independent effectiveness and retaliation reviews.
7. Integrate labor-relations risk into enterprise risk management.
8. Establish financial reserves and operational plans for material classification changes.
9. Review staffing, technology, budget, and shared-services adequacy.
10. Transfer successful practices only after local legal validation.

# 13. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Principal mitigation |
|---|---|
| Misclassification or reclassification | Country- and segment-specific assessments; operating-practice audits; transition scenarios; reserves |
| Unlawful bypass of representatives | Constituency and authority determination; non-bypass rules; local counsel review |
| Retaliation or perceived retaliation | Privacy-safe audits; independent investigations; human review; manager discipline |
| Informal forums becoming sham representation | Neutral protocols; no employer-controlled elections; clear authority limits; independent facilitation |
| Competition-law exposure | Counsel-approved agendas; information segregation; no rate or territory coordination |
| Product changes violating consultation rights | Mandatory Worker Impact Review; launch gate; stop-and-escalate rule |
| Safety or fraud concerns during review | Narrow emergency exception; interim safeguards; retrospective review |
| Financial shock from reclassification | Base, adverse, and transition scenarios; reserves; payroll and insurance planning |
| Underreporting or low worker participation | Multilingual channels; independent ombuds; direct and representative voice; access audits |
| Fragmented regional implementation | Global standards; local annexes; Council oversight; audits; exception tracking |
| Data or privacy violations | Purpose limitation; access controls; small-cell suppression; retention and deletion rules |
| Operational disruption | Early engagement; issue escalation; contingency planning; lawful continuity measures |

# 14. Metrics and accountability

Metrics should measure fairness, compliance, safety, trust, and resolution—not merely the absence of strikes.

## Representation and engagement

- Markets with current legal and representation matrices;
- Material changes receiving documented trigger analysis;
- Required consultations completed before implementation;
- Worker proposals receiving written responses;
- Representative access by language and worker segment;
- Meeting attendance and resolution rates; and
- Participation across gender, migrant status, vehicle type, and work intensity where lawful and proportionate.

## Fair process and non-retaliation

- Appeal and human-review completion times;
- Reversal, restoration, and corrective-payment rates;
- Adverse actions following collective complaints;
- Retaliation complaints and substantiated findings;
- Geographic and demographic enforcement disparities; and
- Remediation completion times.

## Safety

- Serious incidents and fatalities;
- Response time to safety reports;
- Resolution of hazardous-location complaints;
- Extreme-weather interventions; and
- Harassment and violence complaints.

## Legal and governance

- Classification reviews completed and updated;
- Consultation or bargaining breaches;
- Regulatory inquiries;
- Audit findings and remediation time;
- Manager training completion;
- Product launches passing the legal gate; and
- Document-retention compliance.

Each metric must have:

- A target;
- A tolerance threshold;
- A named data owner;
- A review forum;
- An escalation trigger;
- A remediation requirement; and
- Independent validation where appropriate.

Metrics must be segmented only where lawful and proportionate, with data-protection review, aggregation, small-cell suppression, restricted access, and underreporting analysis.

The platform must not reward managers for low complaint volume, low collective activity, rapid but low-quality closure, or suppression of organizing. Low complaint volume or low collective activity is not evidence of healthy labor relations.

Business measures such as service continuity, retention, customer satisfaction, and dispute cost may be monitored, but may not justify suppressing lawful representation or collective action.

---

## Final legal caveat

This framework is a global policy strategy, not legal advice. Worker classification, representation rights, bargaining duties, consultation triggers, competition-law treatment, data obligations, effective dates, remedies, and enforcement requirements must be determined separately for each jurisdiction, platform entity, operating model, and materially distinct worker segment.

Before implementation, the platform should obtain written validation from qualified local counsel and, where required or appropriate, consult the relevant councils, unions, associations, collectives, regulators, or other representatives. Local law, collective agreements, regulator guidance, and court decisions control in the event of conflict with this framework.

The governing standard is:

> **Common global safeguards, locally lawful structures, no bypass of legitimate representation, no retaliation, and no material labor-impact change without documented legal and worker-impact review.**
