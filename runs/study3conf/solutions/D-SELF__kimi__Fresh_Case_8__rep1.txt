**CO-COMPETITIVENESS ALLIANCE: A CBAM PARTNERSHIP STRATEGY FOR STRATEGIC SUPPLY RESILIENCE**
*Steel and Aluminum Supplier Partnerships Across India, Turkey, and North Africa*

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) enters its definitive financial phase on 1 January 2026. For our European industrial operations, this is not a routine regulatory adjustment; it is a restructuring of trade competitiveness that imposes an asymmetric carbon-cost burden on long-standing suppliers who played no part in Europe’s historical industrial emissions.

Our suppliers in India, Turkey, and North Africa must now monitor, verify, and pay for embedded direct (Scope 1) and indirect (Scope 2) emissions—measured in tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (tCO₂e)—or face punitive EU default values that would render them, and therefore us, uncompetitive. Approaching this transition as a transactional audit exercise will fracture relationships, trigger sovereignty backlash, and default our supply chain into penalty pricing.

This document establishes a **Co-Competitiveness Alliance**. It treats CBAM as a joint obstacle to market access, not a supplier defect. We will tier our support to match technical readiness, co-invest in monitoring infrastructure, share the commercial upside of verified low-carbon production, and authentically acknowledge the fairness concerns our partners hold. In doing so, we convert a unilateral regulatory shock into a shared competitive moat.

**Strategic Outcomes**
- **Supply Continuity:** Zero-disruption transition through the January 2026 financial phase via tiered capability uplift and dual-source transition mapping.
- **Cost Avoidance:** Verified actual-emissions reporting across ≥90% of strategic steel and aluminum volume by Q1 2026, avoiding punitive default-value penalties.
- **Bilateral Value Creation:** Contractual mechanisms that improve supplier margins—not merely compliance subsidies—through carbon-efficiency bonuses, floor-price protections, and joint marketing rights.
- **Scope 3 Reduction:** Measurable embedded-carbon decline through renewable power procurement, scrap optimization, and process efficiency, lowering our own regulatory and reputational exposure.

---

## 1. STRATEGIC IMPERATIVE

The transition from CBAM’s transitional reporting phase to full financial compliance is a restructuring event. Default values under the Implementing Regulation are designed to be punitive; actual emissions reporting is the only viable path to margin preservation.

However, our suppliers face structural disadvantages no European competitor endured during legacy industrialization. Many perceive CBAM—legally and politically—as inconsistent with the Paris Agreement’s Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC), and potentially inconsistent with WTO Enabling Clause and GATT Article XX disciplines. Dismissing these views as mere resistance is commercially fatal.

Our choice is binary:
- **Transactional extraction:** Pass down compliance demands, enforce unilateral audits, and accept default-value inflation as a cost of doing business.
- **Strategic alliance:** Co-develop compliance capacity, share investment and returns, and position our joint supply chain as a lower-carbon, lower-cost source than rivals who cannot match our verified performance.

We choose alliance.

These principles are binding on all procurement, sustainability, and executive engagement teams.

## 3. REGIONAL PARTNERSHIP PLAYBOOKS

### 3.1 INDIA: PRIVATE-SECTOR RESILIENCE AMID SOVEREIGNTY SENSITIVITY

**Strategic Context**
India presents the most acute protectionism narrative of the three regions. Government and peak industry bodies have publicly framed CBAM as a unilateral violation of CBDR-RC and a disguised trade barrier. The private sector is deeply bifurcated: Tier 1 integrated steel and primary aluminum majors possess nascent lifecycle assessment (LCA) and ISO 14064-1 capabilities, while a long tail of coal-based direct reduced iron (DRI) and induction furnace (IF) operators lacks sub-metering and mass-balance discipline. For aluminum, India’s coal-heavy grid (~0.57–0.60 kgCO₂/kWh) drives total embedded emissions to 10–14 tCO₂e/t, a catastrophic Scope 2 exposure relative to EU benchmarks.

**Supplier Value Proposition**
We offer long-term offtake certainty, co-funded renewable Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) that lower operating costs, and technology partnerships with shared IP—not compliance extraction. We explicitly de-link our engagement from Indian state rhetoric; this is private-sector supply chain resilience, not political endorsement of EU policy.

**Action Items**
- **Green Corridor Partnerships (Tier 1):**
  - Execute multi-year offtake agreements with integrated steel mills and primary aluminum smelters capable of actual-emissions reporting.
  - Co-develop marginal abatement cost curves focused on scrap-EAF ecosystem expansion, coal-DRI efficiency improvements, and selective green-hydrogen piloting where public-private capital is available. Do not propose economy-wide gas-DRI migration, which is structurally unrealistic given India’s gas import dependency.
  - For aluminum, urgently co-fund renewable PPAs in Rajasthan and Gujarat to slash Scope 2 liability. Unbundle and track green attributes per I-REC/Guarantee of Origin (GO) standards acceptable under EU CBAM indirect-emissions methodology.
- **Structured Uplift (Tier 2 & 3):**
  - Enroll mid-tier steel rerollers and aluminum extruders in a **Supplier CBAM Academy**: 90-day sprints to install MRV for direct and indirect emissions.
  - Deploy standardized calculation workbooks pre-mapped to Implementing Regulation 2023/1773, with explicit guidance on steel precursor attribution (pig iron, DRI, ferroalloys) and system-boundary yield ratios.
  - Contract an in-region service provider in Mumbai to serve as a **Shared Service Center (SSC)** hub, aggregating verifier costs and administrative support for SMEs.
- **Fairness & Diplomacy:**
  - Open executive dialogues with explicit validation: *“We recognize that CBAM imposes a unilateral carbon cost on your current production that no European competitor faced during historical industrialization. That asymmetry is real, and the WTO consistency of this mechanism is legitimately contested.”*
  - Pivot to commercial moat logic: *“Our competitors in higher-carbon jurisdictions face identical barriers. If we verify your actuals below default values, your material becomes more competitive inside the EU than theirs. CBAM becomes a fence around our joint business.”*
- **Risk Mitigation:**
  - Avoid “audit” language. Use “technical cooperation” and “joint data architecture.”
  - Maintain geographic diversification to buffer against potential Indian retaliatory tariff measures.
  - Offer guaranteed floor-price clauses in long-term contracts to prevent Tier 1 majors from rationally allocating lowest-carbon output to domestic or ASEAN buyers.
  - For critical Tier 3 suppliers at risk of silent exit, provide non-recourse Transition Grants tied to MRV milestone completion, not emission outcomes, respecting structural constraints.

### 3.2 TURKEY: CAPTURING THE EAF ADVANTAGE THROUGH VERIFICATION EXCELLENCE

**Strategic Context**
Turkey possesses the most mature industrial data ecosystem among our regions. With approximately 70% scrap-based electric arc furnace (EAF) steel production, Turkish mills hold a structural competitive advantage: direct emissions of 0.5–0.8 tCO₂e/t and total embedded emissions frequently in the 0.9–1.3 tCO₂e/t range. However, this advantage is only bankable if actuals are verified. Critical gaps remain: a shortage of EU-recognized local verifiers, absence of an officially published CBAM-compliant national electricity emission factor, and scrap mass-balance complexity. The industry depends on EU offtake (~40–45% of exports) and lobbies pragmatically for ETS linkage and Customs Union modernization.

**Supplier Value Proposition**
We provide immediate cash-flow returns via the Actual-vs-Default Savings Share mechanism, co-funded verifier engagement, and advocacy for domestic revenue retention through ETS linkage. We treat Turkish EAF producers as preferred partners, not compliance subjects.

**Action Items**
- **Fast-Track Actuals & Savings Share (Tier 1):**
  - Prioritize immediate enrollment of EAF steel producers and aluminum rollers/extruders into actual-emissions reporting.
  - Co-fund engagement with EU-accredited verifiers (e.g., TÜV Rheinland, Bureau Veritas, SGS) and support Turkish audit bodies in achieving EU-equivalent accreditation to address the verifier bottleneck before Q4 2025.
  - Activate the **Actual-vs-Default Savings Share**: when verified actuals fall below applicable EU default values, the avoided CBAM certificate cost is split 50/50 as a **Carbon Efficiency Bonus**, giving suppliers a tangible, recurring cash-flow reason to report accurately.
  - *Caveat:* We will not assume automatic default-value superiority. We will monitor Implementing Regulation fallback methodology evolution to ensure our savings-share baseline reflects regulatory reality.
- **Technical Precision:**
  - Collaborate with the Turkish Steel Producers Association (TÇÜD) and energy regulators to publish a CBAM-compliant national grid emission factor.
  - Provide system-boundary templates for semi-finished goods (billets, slabs) and complex goods to ensure precursor attribution is audit-ready.
  - Address scrap mass-balance rigor: document alloy additions, contamination rates, and DRI/pig iron inputs to prevent verification failure.
- **Capability Uplift (Tier 2 & 3):**
  - Deploy SSC support through an established Istanbul-based service provider to standardize MRV training and XML reporting to the CBAM Transitional Registry.
  - Leverage geographic proximity for just-in-time inventory arrangements, reducing working-capital pressure from CBAM certificate prepayment.
- **Fairness & Diplomacy:**
  - Acknowledge that Turkish industry currently pays Brussels rather than retaining carbon revenues domestically.
  - Support supplier participation in the **Supplier Council on Climate Trade** to advocate for accelerated Turkish ETS linkage and Customs Union update as a *revenue-retention* strategy, not capitulation.
- **Risk Mitigation:**
  - Insert contractual **Alliance Integrity Attestations** to prevent carbon diversion: low-carbon metal must not be allocated to the EU while higher-carbon affiliate output serves domestic markets.
  - Maintain flexible contract terms so that delays in ETS linkage do not trigger force-majeure disputes.

### 3.3 NORTH AFRICA: EURO-MEDITERRANEAN PARTNERSHIP & SHARED INFRASTRUCTURE

**Strategic Context**
The North African supply base is heterogeneous and fragile. Morocco offers emerging institutional strength and world-class renewable resources. Egypt suffers acute FX shortages, energy subsidy distortions, and captive fossil power that complicate Scope 2 calculations. Tunisia’s SME extruders face administrative fragility. Algeria carries state-intervention risk. Data infrastructure is the weakest of the three markets, and no local CBAM verifier accreditation exists. Yet proximity and solar/wind potential present a long-term Scope 2 advantage if captured early.

**Supplier Value Proposition**
We bring Euro-Mediterranean blended finance, hard-currency working-capital flexibility, and pooled technical infrastructure. We commit to hiring local analysts for our regional service layer, signaling rooted industrial partnership rather than commodity extraction.

**Action Items**
- **Morocco — The Renewable Aluminum Hub:**
  - Fast-track Green Corridor Partnerships for primary and semi-finished aluminum leveraging wind and solar assets to achieve near-zero indirect emissions.
  - Access blended climate finance through **EFSD+ (European Fund for Sustainable Development Plus)**, **EBRD**, and **KfW** programs for MRV infrastructure and behind-the-meter renewable integration.
- **Egypt — Addressing FX and Energy Subsidy Complexity:**
  - Support behind-the-meter solar installations to reduce grid Scope 2 liability and bypass energy-subsidy accounting complexity.
  - Offer hard-currency drawdowns from the **CBAM Compliance Co-Investment Facility** to mitigate FX-shortage-driven supply insolvency.
  - Utilize SSC support through Cairo/Alexandria logistics channels.
- **Tunisia — Consortia for SME Survival:**
  - Aggregate SME extruders and rerollers into industry consortia that share verifier costs and SSC administrative support via a Casablanca/Tunis hub.
  - Provide non-recourse Transition Grants tied to MRV milestone completion.
- **Algeria — State-Aligned Engagement:**
  - Engage through large state-managed industrial groups or formal government-to-business channels. Avoid SME-focused compliance pushes where state oversight creates friction. Maintain flexible volume commitments to absorb potential state-mandated export redirection.
- **Cross-Cutting Technical Support:**
  - Contract an established service provider in **Casablanca** to serve the Maghreb with pooled MRV support, template localization (French/Arabic), and logistics coordination.
  - Engage EU-accredited verifiers for periodic fly-in or remote-assurance programs until local accreditation is achieved.
- **Fairness & Diplomacy:**
  - Explicitly acknowledge neo-colonial trade concerns: *“We understand that mechanisms designed in Brussels can resemble external conditionality. Our commitment is a long-term industrial partnership, not a compliance extraction.”*
  - Co-invest in local technical employment by requiring the regional SSC to hire local MRV analysts and account managers.
- **Risk Mitigation:**
  - Monitor for suppliers quietly accepting punitive default values rather than reporting; intervene with SSC support before Q3 2025.
  - Strengthen contract terms and relationship value to prevent SMEs from pivoting to less administratively demanding AfCFTA or MENA markets.
  - Navigate Euro-Mediterranean association agreements and rules of origin to ensure CBAM compliance does not conflict with existing diagonal cumulation benefits.

## 4. TIERED ENGAGEMENT & SUPPORT FRAMEWORK

We segment the supply base by **technical readiness** and **emissions intensity**, not merely by volume.

### Tier 1: CBAM-Competitive
*Profile: Large Turkish integrated and EAF mills, major Indian aluminum smelters with ISO 14064-1, Moroccan/Tunisian operators with existing LCA capability.*

**Action Items**
- Lock in multi-year offtake agreements with premium pricing for verified low-embedded-carbon material (e.g., <1.5 tCO₂e/t for aluminum; <0.8 tCO₂e/t for crude steel benchmark).
- Grant co-branding rights for joint “EU-Compliant Low Embodied Carbon” product lines, with **shared marketing revenue** from any downstream premium captured.
- Limit buyer technical support to EU declarant interface alignment (quarterly XML submission to the CBAM Transitional Registry).

### Tier 2: Developing
*Profile: Mid-size Indian steel rerollers, Turkish semi-finished exporters, North African extruders.*

**Action Items**
- Enroll in the **Supplier CBAM Academy**: 90-day sprint to install MRV for Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions.
- Provide standardized Excel/XML templates pre-mapped to EU CBAM methodology for complex goods (steel precursors: pig iron, ferroalloys) and simple goods (aluminum billets).
- Assign a Technical Liaison from our sustainability team to resolve system-boundary questions (e.g., front-end waste/loss yield ratios for steel).
- Co-fund 50% of first limited-assurance verification cycle.

### Tier 3: At-Risk
*Profile: Coal-based DRI steel plants in India, older integrated operations, suppliers with no LCA function and acute cash constraints.*

**Action Items**
- **Path A (Strategic Importance):** Intensive support. Deploy on-site or embedded remote MRV support for six months. Co-fund third-party verification. Explore process improvements realistic to the local context (scrap-EAF where scrap availability permits; DRI efficiency upgrades; behind-the-meter solar for electricity-intensive processes).
- **Path B (Structural Mismatch):** If embedded emissions are irreducibly high and the supplier views CBAM as hostile protectionism, avoid forced compliance confrontation. Explore **commercial restructuring** with dignity: shift to tolling or semi-finished supply arrangements that reduce CBAM liability (e.g., importing direct reduced iron with lower embedded emissions than finished hot-rolled coil), or gradually transition volume to Tier 1/2 suppliers while preserving a declining-volume relationship to prevent supply shock. Offer Transition Grants to support orderly pivot to non-EU markets or domestic value-added processing.

## 5. TECHNICAL ARCHITECTURE & MRV ROADMAP

CBAM compliance hinges on accurate calculation of **embedded direct (Scope 1) and indirect (Scope 2) emissions** per tonne of product, expressed in tCO₂e. Default values are punitive; actual values protect margin.

### Phase 1: Baseline & System Architecture (Now – Q3 2025)
**Action Items**
- Deploy a **Supplier Sustainability Gateway**: a secure, cloud-based data platform with **in-region server residency** (Mumbai, Istanbul, Casablanca zones) to respect sovereignty. Suppliers upload facility-level energy and process data quarterly; the platform auto-generates CBAM-compliant XML reports. EU declarants receive only aggregated product-level embedded emission figures, not proprietary process yields.
- Issue **Embedded Emissions Templates** developed for:
  - **Steel:** Crude steel, hot-rolled coil, wire rod, bars. Account for precursor attribution (pig iron, DRI, ferroalloys) and complex goods system boundaries per Implementing Regulation 2023/1773.
  - **Aluminum:** Primary ingots, billets, slabs, extrusions, foil. Document electricity emissions factor for smelting. Create parallel PPA documentation to claim lower market-based Scope 2 where grid factors are punitive.
- Install **MRV Protocols** for Tier 2/3: sub-metering for EAF furnaces, fuel intake logs for reheat furnaces, alloy addition records for steel precursor tracking.

### Phase 2: Verification & Assurance (Q4 2025 – Q1 2026)
**Action Items**
- **Tier 1:** Obtain **reasonable assurance** under ISAE 3410 or ISO 14064-3 from EU-recognized verifiers.
- **Tier 2:** Obtain **limited assurance**; buyer co-funds 50% of first verification cycle.
- **Tier 3:** Execute buyer-funded “verification readiness” mock audits; target limited assurance by Q2 2026 to avoid default-value penalty at full phase entry.

### Phase 3: Continuous Improvement (2026+)
**Action Items**
- Roll out the **Renewable Energy for CBAM** program: facilitate group or virtual PPAs with green attributes tracked per GO/I-REC standards acceptable to EU CBAM.
- Co-develop steel decarbonization pathways: scrap optimization, transitional efficiency upgrades, and selective hydrogen DRI piloting where viable. Document marginal abatement cost curves jointly.

Cost distribution must align incentives, improve supplier margins, and avoid dependency.

**Pricing Architecture:** All long-term contracts executed from 2025 onward include a transparent **CBAM Pass-Through Clause**:
- Supplier invoices actual embedded emissions (tCO₂e/t) × EU ETS price (rolling 3-month average).
- Buyer pays this carbon line-item to the supplier, who remits it to EU authorities via the CBAM Registry, OR buyer retains and pays directly, with supplier providing verified emissions data.
- **No hidden markup.** Full invoice transparency on carbon line-item. A contractual cap prevents pass-through markup exploitation.

## 7. ADDRESSING FAIRNESS & LEGITIMATE ASYMMETRY: DIPLOMATIC ENGAGEMENT PROTOCOL

Many suppliers perceive CBAM as disguised protectionism or WTO-inconsistent unilateralism. Dismissing this view destroys trust. We adopt a **“Validate-Then-Reframe”** protocol with tangible buyer concessions.

### Step 1: Acknowledge the Legitimate Grievance
In executive dialogues, open with explicit validation:
> *“We recognize that CBAM imposes a unilateral carbon cost on your current production that no European competitor faced during their historical industrialization. The mechanism’s design does not account for the carbon space consumed by early-industrialized economies. That asymmetry is real. We also acknowledge that serious legal arguments exist regarding WTO consistency, particularly under the Enabling Clause and GATT Article XX. We do not treat these positions as mere obstruction.”*

This disarms hostility and signals partnership, not enforcement.

### Step 2: Offer Tangible Buyer Concessions
Validation without concession is empathy theater. We commit to:
- Absorbing defined non-CBAM cost inflation during supplier transition (Transition Cost Stabilization Commitment).
- Sharing joint IP and marketing revenue from co-developed low-carbon products.
- Providing floor-price protections that keep suppliers whole even if EU carbon prices spike.
- Never requiring political endorsement of CBAM as a condition of commercial support.

### Step 3: Reframe from Protectionism to “Market Access Insurance”
Pivot to commercial reality:
> *“Whether or not CBAM is fair in origin, it is the operative gate to our shared market. Our mutual competitors—Chinese, Russian, Middle Eastern—face identical barriers. If we collaborate to lower your verified embedded emissions below default values, your material becomes more competitive inside the EU than higher-carbon rivals. CBAM then becomes a moat around our joint business.”*

### Step 4: Joint Advocacy for Fairer Implementation
Create a **Supplier Council on Climate Trade** comprising 2–3 senior representatives from each region.
- Jointly petition the European Commission for:
  - Simplified MRV for SME exporters.
  - Use of CBAM revenues (per Regulation Article 30) to finance industrial decarbonization in emerging-market supply regions, not merely general EU budget contribution.
  - Faster recognition of credible non-EU ETS schemes (e.g., Turkey’s ETS, India’s Carbon Credit and Trading Scheme) to reduce double burden.
- Issue joint white papers positioning our supply chain as a model for “just transition in trade.”

### Step 5: Support Domestic Carbon Pricing
Encourage and technically support suppliers to advocate for domestic carbon pricing in their home countries. Revenue retained domestically is politically preferable to revenue transferred to the EU via CBAM certificates.

## 8. GOVERNANCE & RISK MITIGATION

| Risk | Mitigation Action |
|------|-----------------|
| **Supply Disruption from Tier 3 Exit** | Maintain a **Dual-Source Transition Map**. No single-source Tier 3 supplier is forced into compliance without a 12-month overlapping supply window. Use inventory pre-positioning for strategic raw materials. |
| **Regulatory/Political Retaliation** | Monitor India’s potential retaliatory measures and Turkey’s ETS linkage delays. Hedge by diversifying geographic exposure. Ensure all data collection is WTO-compliant to survive potential disputes. |
| **Data Sovereignty & Security** | Emissions data resides on neutral platforms with servers in-region. EU declarants receive only aggregated product-level embedded emission figures, not proprietary process yields. |
| **Greenwashing / Reputational Exposure** | No public “green partnership” claims until independent limited assurance is achieved. Marketing claims require pre-approval by buyer’s legal and sustainability teams. |
| **Competitive Poaching** | Protect buyer investment via **Right of First Refusal (ROFR)** clauses for incremental low-carbon capacity, balanced by **Joint IP agreements** that give suppliers ongoing revenue rights. |
| **Carbon Leakage via Shadow Sourcing** | Prohibit CBAM arbitrage where a supplier shifts high-carbon production to a non-tracked affiliate for domestic sale while exporting low-carbon product to EU. Insert contractual attestation of corporate-group MRV coverage (Alliance Integrity Attestation). |
| **Verifier Bottleneck** | Pre-contract EU-accredited verification bodies for 2025–2026 cycles. Support local audit body accreditation in Turkey and North Africa. |

## 10. CONCLUSION

CBAM is an extraterritorial shock to long-standing trade equilibria. If managed transactionally, it will fracture supplier relationships, inflate costs via default-value penalties, and drive supply
