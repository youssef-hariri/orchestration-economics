# 2026 Global Framework for Faith-Based and Cultural Seasonal Campaigns

**For global brand, regional marketing, agency, legal, communications, social-response, operations, and community-partnership leaders**

---

## 1. Executive summary

Seasonal campaigns connected to Ramadan, Lunar New Year, Diwali, and comparable occasions must be treated as **locally accountable cultural participation**, not as one global idea translated into multiple markets.

The brand should maintain global consistency in:

- Ethics, human rights, and inclusion.
- Food safety, certification, and claims substantiation.
- Accessibility, safeguarding, privacy, and copyright.
- Creator conduct and disclosure.
- AI and synthetic-media controls.
- Monitoring, escalation, documentation, and crisis response.

Markets must retain authority over:

- Cultural insight and audience definition.
- Language, dialect, tone, humor, imagery, and symbolism.
- Product role and local execution.
- Community, creator, and nonprofit partnerships.
- Local launch, pause, correction, or withdrawal.

A campaign may proceed only when:

1. Its premise is locally authored or materially reshaped.
2. The product has a credible role.
3. Relevant cultural, religious, language, food, legal, and regulatory specialists have reviewed it.
4. Consultation is meaningful, compensated, and documented.
5. Representation avoids tokenism, stereotyping, and false universality.
6. Claims and certifications are valid in the destination market.
7. Operations, availability, customer care, and partnerships support the message.
8. The brand can monitor, correct, pause, withdraw, and explain its decisions quickly.

**Core standard:** Global consistency belongs in governance and safeguards—not in identical creative, rituals, symbols, family structures, humor, or regional assumptions.

## 2. Principles

### 2.1 Respect before relevance

Every campaign brief must answer:

- Why is the brand participating?
- What legitimate value does it provide?
- Why does the product belong in the story?
- Who helped shape the work?
- Who benefits beyond the brand?
- What could be perceived as tokenistic, extractive, trivializing, or appropriative?
- What should the brand not depict or claim?

If the product has no credible role, the correct decision may be not to campaign.

### 2.2 Local authorship and accountability

Local or market teams own the cultural premise, audience interpretation, language, partnerships, and local launch decision.

A global asset must not become the default merely because it is cheaper, faster, or easier to distribute.

### 2.3 Authenticity is multidimensional

Authenticity requires a credible combination of:

- Local authorship or material local reshaping.
- Cultural and religious accuracy.
- A natural, honest product role.
- Representation with narrative agency.
- Appropriate language, imagery, and symbolism.
- Transparent commercial and community purpose.
- Accountability when concerns arise.

Local approval alone does not establish authenticity.

#### Material adaptation

An asset is not materially adapted merely because it has new subtitles, colors, names, voice-over, food substitutions, or a local celebrity.

Material adaptation requires documented changes to one or more of:

- Cultural insight.
- Narrative or product role.
- Language and register.
- Casting and representation.
- Setting and visual codes.
- Community context.
- Audience or distribution strategy.

### 2.4 Participation, not performance

Credible participation may include:

- Useful meal, hosting, gifting, or delivery solutions.
- Accurate product and certification information.
- Locally meaningful partnerships.
- Fairly paid creators and contributors.
- Transparent community investment.
- Accessible and practical seasonal experiences.

The brand must not imply:

- Membership in a faith or culture.
- Authority to define an observance.
- That purchasing or consuming the product demonstrates religious commitment.
- That one campaign represents every member of a community.

### 2.5 Specificity over generic symbolism

Lanterns, crescent moons, mosques, diyas, rangoli, dragons, zodiac animals, fireworks, traditional clothing, sacred spaces, and family feasts must not be used as generic seasonal decoration.

Every such element must be:

- Necessary or clearly relevant.
- Accurate for the intended market and observance.
- Appropriate to the audience.
- Reviewed by qualified specialists.
- Used without implying brand ownership of the tradition.

### 2.6 Sacred content is restricted

The following require enhanced review and should normally be avoided unless demonstrably necessary:

- Scripture or sacred quotations.
- Prayer or worship.
- Sacred figures or religious doctrine.
- Devotional gestures.
- Holy sites or religious objects.
- Ritual acts.
- Claims about religious obligation or suitability.

#### Sacred-content necessity test

Before use, the team must demonstrate:

- Why the element is necessary.
- Why the product does not appear to replace, improve, or authenticate a religious practice.
- That the use is non-humorous and non-decorative.
- That an appropriately qualified religious specialist approved it.
- That required rights and consent have been obtained.

If the creative works without the sacred element, remove it.

### 2.7 Inclusion within inclusion

No occasion should be treated as culturally uniform. Teams should consider relevant differences in:

- Region and nationality.
- Language and dialect.
- Religious interpretation and level of observance.
- Ethnicity and diaspora status.
- Age, gender, class, and disability.
- Household structure.
- Urban, rural, migrant, and nontraditional family experience.
- People who participate socially without formally observing.

Inclusive representation does not require every asset to depict every group. It requires that the intended audience is defined honestly and that the brand does not claim to represent people it has not meaningfully considered.

## 3. Roles and decision rights

| Area | Local market | Regional team | Global brand | Legal / compliance | Issues / communications |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cultural premise | Accountable | Consulted | Consulted | Informed | Informed |
| Local language and imagery | Accountable | Consulted | Informed | Consulted | Informed |
| Claims and certification | Consulted | Consulted | Informed | Accountable; veto authority | Informed |
| Local launch | Accountable | Consulted | Informed | Veto on legal or safety issues | Consulted |
| Global reuse | Consulted | Consulted | Accountable | Veto on legal or safety issues | Consulted |
| Local pause or withdrawal | Accountable | Consulted | Informed | Consulted | Consulted |
| Cross-border pause | Consulted | Consulted | Accountable | Consulted | Accountable |
| External crisis response | Consulted | Consulted | Consulted | Consulted | Accountable |

### Global brand team

Owns:

- Global brand, ethical, accessibility, and safety standards.
- Resources and specialist access.
- Common risk taxonomy.
- Cross-border distribution controls.
- Global listening infrastructure.
- Enterprise escalation and learning.

The global team may not override legitimate local cultural judgment solely for aesthetic consistency.

### Regional team

Owns:

- Regional coordination.
- Market-grouping rationale.
- Shared specialist resources.
- Cross-market conflicts.
- Regional operational and claims consistency.
- Escalation support.

### Local market lead

Owns:

- Market strategy and audience definition.
- Local cultural brief.
- Consultation and community participation.
- Local agency management.
- Local legal and regulatory clearance.
- Local launch, pause, correction, or withdrawal.
- Local-language external response.

A market may immediately pause its own assets.

### Agencies

Own:

- Native cultural and linguistic development.
- Documentation of assumptions and sources.
- Local casting, creators, production, and media.
- Version control.
- Early risk escalation.
- Moderation and listening support.

Agencies may not self-approve their own work or suppress dissent to protect schedule.

### Cultural and religious specialists

Provide advice within a defined scope of expertise, including:

- Cultural accuracy.
- Language and dialect.
- Religious doctrine, scripture, fasting, prayer, or sacred content where qualified.
- Halal, vegetarian, or other food-related religious requirements.
- Likely local audience interpretation.

Cultural expertise and religious expertise are not interchangeable.

### Community and nonprofit partners

Provide informed community perspective and advise on proposed benefits and representation. They do not automatically represent all community members.

A partnership must not be presented as community endorsement without explicit consent.

### Legal, regulatory, food, and certification teams

Own:

- Claims substantiation.
- Food safety and allergens.
- Halal, kosher, vegetarian, vegan, and related certification.
- Promotions, pricing, availability, and influencer disclosures.
- Privacy, copyright, safeguarding, and local advertising compliance.

Local legal or regulatory counsel is required for amber and red work where local exposure exists.

### Communications and issues team

Owns:

- Cross-border escalation.
- Crisis coordination.
- Holding statements.
- Media and stakeholder response.
- Remediation governance.
- Post-incident review.

### Operations, retail, customer care, and employee teams

Ensure:

- Inventory, store hours, delivery, and customer care align with the campaign.
- Retailers, franchisees, distributors, and delivery partners are briefed.
- Employees receive FAQs and protection from harassment.
- Seasonal accommodations and community commitments are deliverable.

## 4. Market adaptation guidance

Campaigns must be planned by market or by a clearly justified audience cluster. “Gulf,” “North Africa,” or “Southeast Asia” is not sufficient unless the team documents why markets can be grouped.

### 4.1 Ramadan

“Muslim audience” is not a sufficient market definition. Each brief must state the geography, audience, language, level of observance, product occasion, and whether the campaign depicts religious practice or broader cultural participation.

#### Gulf markets

Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman require separate country review for content involving religious references, Arabic copy, public spaces, influencers, charity, certification, or national identity.

Review:

- Modern Standard Arabic versus local dialect.
- English usage and audience role.
- Iftar, suhoor, fasting, prayer, charity, and hospitality references.
- Modesty, gender, family, and public-space representation.
- Halal certification and preparation.
- Local creator and disclosure requirements.
- Retail, delivery, and customer-service hours.
- National versus pan-Arab symbolism.

A Gulf execution must not automatically represent the wider Muslim world.

#### North Africa

Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, and Egypt must be assessed separately where relevant.

Review:

- Dialect, French, bilingual, Amazigh, and other language choices.
- Local culinary traditions and household routines.
- Humor, celebrity, class, and urban/rural cues.
- Whether Gulf-style luxury imagery appears imported or elitist.
- Levels of religious observance.
- Charity and community representation.
- Country-specific advertising, food, and creator rules.

#### Southeast Asia

Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei, and other relevant markets must not be treated as one audience.

Review:

- Indonesian, Malay, English, Chinese, Tamil, and other language needs.
- Local halal certification and ingredient disclosure.
- Preparation and cross-contact.
- Urban, rural, migrant, convert, mixed-faith, and single-person households.
- Homecoming, mosque, family, and community narratives without assuming they are universal.
- Local creator norms and language-capable moderation.

Halal certification approval, cultural narrative approval, and religious-content approval are separate controls.

### 4.2 Lunar New Year

The brief must identify whether it represents:

- Chinese New Year.
- Vietnamese Tết.
- Korean Seollal.
- Another local tradition.
- A deliberately broad Lunar New Year audience.

It must document:

- Why “Chinese New Year” or “Lunar New Year” is appropriate.
- Which community is intended.
- Language and script requirements.
- Whether zodiac content is applicable.
- Food symbolism and taboos.
- Gifting and family conventions.
- Diaspora and mixed-family representation.
- Symbols prohibited from transfer across traditions.

Chinese characters, calligraphy, zodiac references, food symbolism, and rituals require specialist review. They must not be transferred automatically between Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean, or other audiences.

All dates, zodiac references, scripts, packaging, and retail materials require final verification before release.

### 4.3 Diwali

The brief must specify the regional, religious, and audience context.

Review:

- Hindu, Jain, Sikh, Buddhist, secular, and diaspora participation.
- Distinction between Diwali and Bandi Chhor Divas.
- Regional terminology and festival sequence.
- Vegetarian and other dietary expectations.
- Use of diyas, rangoli, lamps, fire, clothing, music, devotional elements, and deities.
- Regional languages and foods.
- Firework safety, air quality, animal welfare, and local restrictions.
- Whether the work reduces Diwali to lights, shopping, sweets, or Bollywood imagery.

Do not use “every Indian family,” “all South Asians,” or equivalent universal claims.

### 4.4 Overlapping occasions in 2026

Lunar New Year and the expected Ramadan period occur close together in 2026. Markets with overlapping audiences require a dual-season plan covering:

- Media and inventory competition.
- Creator and production schedules.
- Retail and delivery capacity.
- Multilingual customer care.
- Households participating in both occasions.
- Avoidance of generic “diversity” content that merges distinct observances.
- Monitoring for perceived prioritization or cultural collision.

The occasions must not be combined into one narrative without separate community review.

## 5. Campaign development and consultation

### 5.1 Consultation stages

Consultation must occur at:

1. Brief development.
2. Early concept.
3. Script and storyboard.
4. Casting and production.
5. Final language and asset review.
6. Post-launch evaluation.

Final-artwork-only review is insufficient for sensitive work.

### 5.2 Advisory group

Each market must appoint an advisory group appropriate to the occasion and risk. Membership may include:

- Local cultural practitioner.
- Qualified religious specialist.
- Native-language and dialect expert.
- Local creative practitioner.
- Food, halal, vegetarian, or certification expert.
- Community or nonprofit representative.
- Intended-audience participants.
- Accessibility specialist.
- Legal, regulatory, and brand representatives as needed.

One advisor cannot represent an entire religion, ethnicity, region, generation, or class.

### 5.3 Advisor standards

The record must show:

- Qualifications and scope of expertise.
- Compensation.
- Conflicts of interest.
- Materials reviewed.
- Advice provided.
- Changes made.
- Unresolved concerns.
- Final decision and rationale.

Advisors must have sufficient time and context to provide candid feedback. For amber and red work, at least one reviewer must be independent of the originating creative agency.

### 5.4 Audience testing

Testing should be segmented by relevant:

- Geography.
- Language.
- Age.
- Observance.
- Gender.
- Socioeconomic group.
- Diaspora status.
- Disability.
- Religious or cultural tradition.

Ask:

- Does this feel respectful?
- Does it feel locally relevant?
- Does the product have a credible role?
- Who may feel excluded or stereotyped?
- What feels inaccurate or overly commercial?
- Would you share this with family or friends?
- What would make the campaign more trustworthy?

A positive average score does not override a serious specialist objection.

### 5.5 Representation and agency audit

Review who:

- Speaks.
- Makes decisions.
- Prepares, serves, and consumes the product.
- Has narrative and emotional agency.
- Appears only as background or symbolic representation.
- Is shown as wealthy, poor, traditional, modern, able-bodied, or family-based.
- Is included or excluded from the intended audience.

Do not use diverse casting as a substitute for meaningful participation.

## 6. Campaign development safeguards

### Red flags requiring redesign

- Sacred practices used as decoration or a punchline.
- Generic “exotic” settings, clothing, food, or music.
- One person used to represent an entire community.
- Imported regional aesthetics presented as universal.
- Poverty, piety, gender roles, or family duty used as shortcuts.
- A creator selected only for ethnicity, nationality, or religion.
- Charity beneficiaries used as emotional props.
- Product positioned as replacing or improving a religious practice.
- “Positive” intent used to excuse factual inaccuracy.
- A community partnership used as reputational cover.
- Combined religious or cultural symbols with no coherent local rationale.
- Claims that imply all members of a community observe an occasion identically.

### Respectful adaptation is more defensible when:

- The idea is locally authored or materially reshaped.
- Cultural elements are accurate and proportionate.
- The product has a credible role.
- The brand does not claim ownership or authority.
- Relevant communities influence decisions.
- Contributors and creators are fairly paid.
- Community benefit is transparent and meaningful.
- Sacred elements are avoided or carefully justified.
- The asset is not exported without destination-market review.

### Appropriation risk increases when the brand:

- Extracts a ritual, symbol, pattern, food, or story primarily for sales appeal.
- Treats sacred practice as a prop or conversion trigger.
- Uses a community’s cultural expression while excluding or underpaying community members.
- Claims the practice as a brand innovation.
- Sanitizes or stereotypes a tradition for global audiences.
- Uses diverse casting or charity to shield weak creative.
- Distributes a locally approved asset globally without review.
- Implies that purchase provides cultural legitimacy.

## 7. Claims, legal, and operational controls

### 7.1 Claims requiring evidence

All claims must be market-specific and current, including:

- Halal, kosher, vegetarian, vegan, allergen, alcohol, and pork statements.
- Fasting, iftar, suhoor, energy, hydration, health, or nutrition claims.
- “Traditional,” “authentic,” “inspired by,” or geographic-origin claims.
- Charity, donations, community impact, or beneficiary numbers.
- Sustainability claims.
- Price, discounts, availability, delivery, and operating-hour claims.
- Influencer or community endorsement.

Visual context must not imply certification or religious suitability that has not been substantiated.

### 7.2 Claims register

Record:

- Exact approved wording.
- Evidence source.
- Market and channel.
- Approver.
- Expiry or review date.
- Required disclosure.
- Associated asset versions.

### 7.3 Operational credibility

The campaign must not promise seasonal support that the business cannot deliver.

Confirm:

- Product availability.
- Inventory and distribution.
- Store and delivery hours.
- Customer-care language capability.
- Retailer and franchisee readiness.
- Product preparation and cross-contact controls.
- Donation and partnership funding.
- Seasonal employee support.

Operational failure can undermine cultural credibility even when the creative itself is accurate.

## 8. Risk classification and approval safeguards

### Green: routine

Examples:

- Product-led seasonal content without religious imagery.
- Locally reviewed greetings or gifting content.
- Standard creator content using approved claims.
- Low-sensitivity seasonal color or food references.

Approval:

- Local marketing lead.
- Local creative and language review.
- Legal or claims review where applicable.
- Automated preflight checks.

### Amber: sensitive

Examples:

- Iftar, suhoor, fasting, prayer-adjacent, or charity references.
- Religious symbols.
- Traditional-food or authenticity claims.
- Sensitive family, gender, child, or vulnerable-community depictions.
- Humor involving the occasion.
- Cross-market adaptation of culturally specific work.

Approval:

- Local market lead.
- Cultural advisor.
- Religious specialist where relevant.
- Native-language reviewer.
- Legal, food, or certification teams as applicable.
- Inclusion and accessibility review.
- Regional communications.
- Independent human reviewer.

### Red: high risk

Examples:

- Scripture, prayer, sacred figures, doctrine, or holy sites.
- Content that mocks, ranks, compares, or disparages religions or communities.
- Political, sectarian, geopolitical, or conflict-related references.
- Unsubstantiated religious, food-safety, health, certification, or charity claims.
- Serious allegations of racism, exploitation, religious disrespect, or harmful stereotyping.
- Global reuse without destination review.
- Regulator, certification-body, institutional, or major partner intervention.

Approval:

- Market director.
- Qualified cultural and religious specialists.
- Local legal or regulatory counsel.
- Global issues lead.
- Senior global brand leadership.
- Independent reviewer.

Unresolved red risk defaults to redesign, delay, geographic restriction, or cancellation.

### Separate pre-launch risk from live incident severity

A low-risk greeting may become a severe live incident if it triggers credible harm, misinformation about product safety, or cross-border escalation. Risk classification and incident severity must therefore be recorded separately.

## 9. Automated and independent human review

### 9.1 Automated preflight

Automated systems should check, where technically possible:

- Missing mandatory disclosures.
- Unapproved or expired claims.
- Incorrect dates, prices, products, markets, or certification references.
- Wrong language or script version.
- Asset-version mismatches.
- Restricted terms, symbols, or imagery.
- Missing captions, alt text, contrast, or audio description.
- Geographic distribution violations.
- Creator disclosure or rights omissions.
- Product availability conflicts.
- Duplicate or outdated assets.
- Potential synthetic or manipulated media.
- Required AI disclosures and provenance fields.

Automation may flag risk and enforce workflow. It may not determine cultural acceptability or replace human review.

### 9.2 Independent human review

Amber and red work requires a qualified reviewer who:

- Did not originate the concept.
- Is not evaluated solely on launch speed or approval.
- Has access to the full context and intended distribution.
- Can record dissent.
- Has relevant expertise for the issue under review.

For content involving doctrine, scripture, prayer, fasting, or sacred practice, a qualified religious specialist is required.

### 9.3 Approval outcomes

Each reviewer records one of:

- Approve.
- Approve with conditions.
- Revise and resubmit.
- Reject.
- Unable to assess within their expertise.

Conditions must identify the required change and verifier. A senior override of a rejection requires written rationale and may not bypass legal, safety, certification, or safeguarding controls.

## 10. Launch checklist

### Cultural and content readiness

- [ ] Market grouping is justified.
- [ ] Cultural and religious consultation is complete.
- [ ] Representation and narrative agency have been reviewed.
- [ ] Sacred-content necessity test is complete where relevant.
- [ ] All languages, dialects, scripts, subtitles, captions, and alt text are approved.
- [ ] Accessibility and safeguarding reviews are complete.
- [ ] Geographic restrictions are documented.
- [ ] Material dissent is resolved or escalated.

### Claims and legal readiness

- [ ] Claims register is complete.
- [ ] Certification is current and valid in the target market.
- [ ] Food, ingredient, allergen, and preparation details are verified.
- [ ] Promotion, price, availability, and delivery claims are confirmed.
- [ ] Influencer disclosures, rights, and conduct clauses are complete.
- [ ] Privacy, copyright, regulatory, and local counsel reviews are complete where required.
- [ ] AI or synthetic-content disclosures and provenance records are complete.

### Operational readiness

- [ ] Inventory and distribution are confirmed.
- [ ] Store and delivery hours align with the creative.
- [ ] Customer care can respond in relevant languages.
- [ ] Retailers, franchisees, distributors, and delivery partners are briefed.
- [ ] Community commitments and donations are funded, verifiable, and transparent.
- [ ] Employee FAQs and escalation routes are issued.

### Monitoring and response readiness

- [ ] Listening keywords include local languages, dialects, transliterations, misspellings, and hashtags.
- [ ] Monitoring coverage and fallback arrangements are documented.
- [ ] Moderators are trained in local context.
- [ ] Escalation contacts are active and tested.
- [ ] Holding statements and factual response materials are locally reviewed.
- [ ] Takedown, correction, replacement, withdrawal, and apology decision trees are ready.
- [ ] Cross-border distribution and pause controls have been tested.
- [ ] An amber or red incident drill has been completed where applicable.

No campaign may launch with a missing mandatory review, unresolved material specialist objection, incomplete claim, certification gap, unavailable product, or inadequate response capability.

## 11. Scrutiny monitoring and escalation model

### 11.1 Monitoring coverage

Each market must declare its actual coverage:

- **Tier 1:** Continuous human monitoring for high-risk markets and peak observance periods.
- **Tier 2:** Scheduled monitoring with on-call escalation.
- **Tier 3:** Agency-supported monitoring with predefined escalation and customer-care routes.

Plans must account for time zones, public holidays, peak iftar and shopping periods, and platform-specific limitations.

### 11.2 Pre-launch preparation

At least four weeks before launch:

- Establish market and language baselines.
- Create keyword lists covering dialects, transliterations, misspellings, and hashtags.
- Identify likely criticism themes and credible local voices.
- Confirm platform-specific pause procedures.
- Prepare locally reviewed factual responses.
- Brief customer care, retailers, creators, employees, and partners.
- Test cross-border asset restrictions.
- Define misinformation, impersonation, and manipulated-context procedures.
- Confirm evidence-preservation procedures.

### 11.3 Monitoring categories

Track by market, language, platform, creator, and asset version:

- Cultural or religious offense allegations.
- Tokenism, stereotyping, or appropriation criticism.
- Translation and terminology errors.
- Product, ingredient, allergen, or certification claims.
- Charity and partnership allegations.
- Creator misconduct.
- Cropped, edited, or AI-generated versions.
- Impersonation and coordinated manipulation.
- Media, regulator, retailer, employee, and partner reaction.
- Customer-service and operational complaints.
- Cross-border reposting.

Automated sentiment may flag issues but cannot resolve sarcasm, dialect, religious meaning, or context. Human validation is mandatory for amber and red decisions.

### 11.4 Escalation levels

#### Level 1: Routine concern

Examples:

- Isolated negative comments.
- Minor language questions.
- Product or certification inquiries.

Actions:

- Answer factually where useful.
- Log the issue.
- Consult the local team if a pattern develops.
- Do not amplify isolated criticism unnecessarily.

#### Level 2: Credible local criticism

Examples:

- Multiple credible local voices identify the same error.
- A local advisor or partner raises a material concern.
- Local media reports the issue.
- Culturally specific criticism materially exceeds baseline.

Actions:

- Pause scheduled amplification.
- Notify local, regional, legal, and global issues teams.
- Validate with qualified local specialists.
- Decide whether to correct, annotate, replace, restrict, or withdraw.
- Respond in the local language where appropriate.

#### Level 3: Severe or cross-border issue

Examples:

- Religious mockery, sacrilege, racism, hate speech, or serious stereotyping.
- Food-safety, allergen, or certification risk.
- Regulator, certification-body, institutional, or major partner intervention.
- Threats, doxxing, or targeted harassment.
- Spread across multiple markets.
- Major creator, retailer, or community-partner withdrawal.
- Materially false claims about charity, safety, or religious suitability.

Actions:

- Freeze paid amplification and new adaptations where technically feasible.
- Preserve all relevant evidence.
- Convene the cross-border response team.
- Assign one accountable coordinator and spokesperson.
- Distinguish legitimate criticism, factual error, misinformation, harassment, and manipulation.
- Coordinate market-specific corrections, apologies, withdrawals, or remediation.
- Protect staff, creators, advisors, and partners.

### 11.5 Initial response targets

Targets must reflect platform and channel realities. As a general standard for red incidents:

- Internal acknowledgment within 30 minutes.
- Escalation within 60 minutes.
- Paid amplification paused within 90 minutes where feasible.
- Documented decision within two hours.
- Public response within two to four hours once facts and local wording are confirmed.

## 12. Response playbooks

### 12.1 Praise

- Thank audiences without claiming universal cultural authority.
- Credit local creators, advisors, and partners where agreed.
- Continue monitoring for misunderstandings about product, certification, or cultural content.

Suggested wording:

> We’re grateful that this locally created work is resonating with audiences. The campaign was developed with market-based creators and advisors, and we appreciate the feedback shared by the community.

### 12.2 Good-faith criticism

- Listen before defending.
- Identify the specific concern.
- Consult relevant local and religious specialists.
- Acknowledge impact and correct factual errors.
- Explain what has changed.
- Do not argue that people should not be offended.

Suggested wording:

> We understand the concern about how this detail was represented. We are reviewing it with our local cultural and language advisors and have paused further promotion while we determine the appropriate correction.

### 12.3 Misinformation or manipulated context

Verify:

- The original asset.
- Version, date, and distribution.
- Whether the content was altered.
- Whether the underlying criticism remains legitimate.
- Whether the claim creates safety, certification, charity, or security risk.

Correct only where correction is likely to reduce harm. Do not label legitimate criticism as misinformation merely because it is unfavorable.

Suggested wording:

> The image circulating is an altered version of our approved content. The relevant product information is available through our official channels. We are also reviewing the broader concern raised about the original campaign with our local advisors.

### 12.4 Credible harm or serious error

Examples include incorrect religious text, unsafe food information, false certification, discriminatory representation, or serious cultural disrespect.

Actions:

- Stop paid and organic amplification where feasible.
- Remove or replace the asset.
- Notify retailers, partners, creators, and customer care.
- Issue a direct, locally reviewed acknowledgment.
- State what was wrong and what has changed.
- Consider apology, restitution, consultation, donation, or other remediation only where genuine.
- Publish verified remediation outcomes.
- Conduct an independent post-incident review.

Suggested wording:

> We made an error in this campaign and understand why it caused concern. The content has been withdrawn while we correct the issue. We are reviewing the work with qualified local specialists and will update our process before any replacement content is released.

Do not use “we only intended to celebrate” as a substitute for accountability.

## 13. Misinformation, AI, and evidence controls

For each material incident, preserve:

- Original asset and version ID.
- Publication time and targeting.
- Approvals and consultation records.
- Translation and claims files.
- Certification evidence.
- Creator agreements.
- Screenshots, URLs, and platform notices.
- Advisor assessments.
- Response and takedown logs.

Synthetic media requires additional review for:

- AI-generated faces, voices, translations, or scripts.
- Synthetic depictions of religious figures, sacred leaders, children, or identifiable community members.
- Likeness, consent, provenance, and disclosure.
- Manipulated cultural representation.

Synthetic depictions of sacred figures or identifiable community members are prohibited absent exceptional approval, documented consent, and specialist review.

## 14. Cross-border coordination

### 14.1 Content firewall

Content created for one market may not be exported through global social channels, paid media, influencer reposts, corporate websites, retailer networks, or employee advocacy tools without destination-market review.

The review must confirm:

- Language and terminology.
- Cultural and religious relevance.
- Symbol and imagery suitability.
- Product and claims validity.
- Certification and regulatory status.
- Creator rights and consent.
- Moderation and response capability.
- Whether geographic restriction is required.

### 14.2 Cross-border response team

For Level 3 incidents, convene:

- Affected market leads.
- Regional communications.
- Global brand.
- Global issues and corporate communications.
- Legal and compliance.
- Food and certification owners where relevant.
- Agency and platform representatives as needed.
- Qualified local advisors.

Markets retain authority over local language, local channels, and locally appropriate remedies. Global teams coordinate facts, enterprise risk, and cross-border action.

### 14.3 Coordinated but non-identical communications

Global teams provide:

- Verified facts.
- Agreed terminology.
- Claims evidence.
- Core action taken.
- Timing and escalation status.
- Questions that must not be answered speculatively.

Markets may adapt:

- Tone.
- Language.
- Cultural explanation.
- Spokesperson.
- Remedy.
- Channel.
- Timing.

Before posting a global statement, assess whether it will amplify a local controversy or cause the original content to reach new audiences.

## 15. Governance documentation

Maintain a central **Seasonal Campaign Register** containing:

- Market, season, audience, and grouping rationale.
- Campaign owner, agencies, creators, advisors, and partners.
- Cultural and religious brief.
- Consultation, compensation, qualifications, and conflicts.
- Representation, accessibility, and safeguarding reviews.
- Sacred-content assessment.
- Claims, certification, food, legal, and regulatory evidence.
- Asset versions, languages, scripts, and distribution restrictions.
- Automated and independent review results.
- Dissent and decision records.
- Launch-readiness status.
- Monitoring baselines and escalation contacts.
- Incidents, responses, remediation, and corrective actions.
- Agency training and performance records.

Records must be retained under applicable legal, privacy, contractual, and records-management requirements.

### Required disagreement record

For each material disagreement, record:

- Disputed element.
- Person or group raising it.
- Relevant expertise.
- Evidence.
- Affected audience.
- Whether the issue is factual, theological, cultural, legal, operational, or reputational.
- Severity and likelihood.
- Mitigations considered.
- Decision-maker.
- Final rationale.
- Conditions and follow-up date.

## 16. Metrics

Metrics must be segmented by market, language, audience, platform, and asset version. Engagement alone is not evidence of cultural success; controversy can increase engagement.

### 16.1 Cultural quality

- Percentage of campaigns with consultation before concept lock.
- Percentage of locally authored or materially adapted work.
- Native-language error rate.
- Respect, relevance, representation, and product-credibility scores.
- Number of material changes resulting from consultation.
- Advisor confidence in the final work.
- Reduction in repeated cultural errors.
- Percentage of mandatory reviews completed on time.

### 16.2 Representation and community accountability

- Representation and narrative-agency assessment.
- Participation of relevant less-visible groups.
- Advisor and community-partner satisfaction.
- Compensation and participation completion.
- Community commitments delivered.
- Verified donation amounts and beneficiaries.
- Accessibility completion.
- Number of partners or advisors whose concerns led to changes.

### 16.3 Brand and commercial performance

- Brand trust and consideration.
- Qualified engagement from intended audiences.
- Positive, neutral, negative, and ambiguous sentiment by market and language.
- Product credibility score.
- Incremental sales, visits, or conversion against a suitable local baseline.
- Customer-service satisfaction.
- Product availability and delivery performance.

### 16.4 Governance and resilience

- Claims and certification completion.
- Food-safety and regulatory incidents.
- Assets corrected before paid amplification.
- Detection-to-verification time.
- Verification-to-escalation time.
- Escalation-to-pause time.
- Decision-to-response time.
- Incident severity and cross-border spread.
- Percentage of incidents resolved with qualified local input.
- Completion of post-incident corrective actions.
- Agency training and performance compliance.
- Monitoring coverage achieved versus planned coverage.

No unresolved food-safety, certification, regulatory, or material misrepresentation issue may be treated as an acceptable campaign result.

## 17. Final accountability standard

The decisive question is not whether the creative visibly resembles Ramadan, Lunar New Year, or Diwali. It is whether the intended audience can reasonably conclude that:

- The brand understood the specific local occasion.
- The product has a credible role.
- People were represented as participants rather than symbols.
- Language, food, imagery, and religious references are accurate.
- Sacred practices were not used casually or commercially.
- The work was shaped by people with relevant knowledge and authority.
- Local teams had genuine power to change or reject the work.
- The brand listened when concerns were raised.
- Cross-border distribution was controlled.
- The brand can correct, withdraw, apologize, and remediate quickly.

The operating standard is therefore:

> **Globally governed, locally accountable, evidence-based participation—with the authority and capability to adapt when context, criticism, or harm requires it.**
