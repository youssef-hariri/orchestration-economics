# Executive Strategy: Global Rollout of Health Features

## Recommendation

Operate the portfolio as a shared sensing and software platform with **market-specific regulatory modes**. For each feature and country, independently control intended use, claims, evidence, UX, thresholds, privacy, distribution, support, and post-market monitoring.

A wellness classification in one market must never be used to justify medical claims elsewhere. Disease-specific outputs, clinical-referral recommendations, physician promotion, and diagnostic-looking UX may create medical-device obligations regardless of the label.

### Sequencing

1. Build the global quality, clinical, regulatory, privacy, and safety foundation.
2. Launch bounded wellness features in selected markets.
3. Select and launch the first regulated sleep-apnea market.
4. Expand to the second regulated market only after bridging analysis and initial post-market review.
5. Resolve US jurisdiction, classification, and pathway before medical commercialization.
6. Scale fall detection and AF detection next.
7. Keep blood-pressure and glucose capabilities in evidence-generation programs until safety and utility are demonstrated.
8. Expand emerging markets individually, only where registration, follow-up care, and post-market support are credible.

## 1. Portfolio and market architecture

### Feature tiers

| Tier | Features | Initial posture |
|---|---|---|
| **Wellness** | Sleep regularity, recovery, activity, general stress coaching | General-health claims only; no disease identification, diagnosis, treatment, or triage |
| **Screening/decision support** | Sleep apnea, AF, fall detection | Medical-device posture wherever outputs identify disease risk, support clinical decisions, or trigger emergency action |
| **Measurement/diagnosis/treatment** | Diagnostic BP, glucose measurement, medication guidance | Later-stage programs requiring the highest evidence and regulatory investment |

Each country-feature combination should be assigned one of three modes:

- **Wellness:** no named disease, disease score, clinical referral, or diagnostic implication.
- **Medical:** authorized disease-specific screening or decision support.
- **Restricted/disabled:** classification unresolved, evidence incomplete, follow-up unavailable, or local controls inadequate.

### Market-selection scorecard

Score candidate countries from 1–5 using:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Regulatory clarity and authorization probability | 20% |
| Evidence transferability and population fit | 15% |
| Clinical partners and investigators | 15% |
| Confirmatory-care availability and affordability | 15% |
| Time and cost to launch | 10% |
| Post-market and support capability | 10% |
| Strategic credibility and evidence reuse | 10% |
| Commercial opportunity | 5% |

Select the first regulated market by **31 December 2026**. Require a weighted score of at least **3.5/5**, with no score below **3/5** for regulatory clarity, clinical readiness, follow-up care, or post-market capability.

Automatic disqualifiers include unresolved material classification uncertainty, no realistic confirmatory care, inadequate local safety handling, or unfunded privacy, representation, language, or vigilance obligations.

## 2. Global foundation and governance

Establish a Health Feature Launch Board with authority to delay, restrict, or suspend launches. It should include regulatory, quality, clinical safety, engineering, product, privacy, cybersecurity, legal, marketing, support, regional, and patient-safety leaders.

Before the first regulated launch, implement:

- Medical-device quality and software lifecycle controls.
- Clinical evaluation, investigation, and research-ethics procedures.
- Cybersecurity and vulnerability management.
- Central claims registry linked to product-release systems.
- Algorithm versioning, model-lock, and change-impact controls.
- Regional feature flags and rollback capability.
- Complaint, vigilance, adverse-event, and safety-signal processes.
- Local-language and health-literacy validation.
- Privacy, consent, retention, deletion, and third-party-access policies.
- Correction, recall, discontinuation, and historical-result remediation playbooks.
- Market-specific customer-support and clinician-escalation procedures.

Each feature-market launch requires an approved dossier covering intended use, classification, evidence, claims, UX, limitations, privacy, cybersecurity, referral capacity, staffing, budget, and post-market controls.

No commercial or regional leader may override a clinical-safety or regulatory stop decision without documented executive, legal, and medical review.

## 3. Sleep-apnea screening program

### Intended use

Use a narrow, non-diagnostic claim:

> “The feature analyzes selected physiological signals to identify patterns that may be associated with sleep apnea and supports discussion with a qualified healthcare professional. It is not intended to diagnose or exclude sleep apnea.”

Before pivotal validation, select one clinical target—for example, moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea above a defined apnea-hypopnea index threshold. The target determines the reference standard, sample size, performance target, labeling, and regulatory pathway.

### Validation investment

Prioritize investment in four layers:

1. **Technical validation:** device fit, signal quality, firmware, missing data, movement, skin tone, body size, age, sex, comorbidities, medications, and sensor degradation.
2. **Clinical validation:** prospective, multicenter, home-use study using an accepted reference standard, with community or primary-care recruitment as well as specialist sites.
3. **Human factors:** comprehension of screening versus diagnosis, negative and inconclusive results, confirmatory testing, next steps, and data use.
4. **Clinical utility:** referral appropriateness, testing completion, time to consultation, anxiety, false reassurance, unnecessary testing, and clinician usefulness.

The clinical protocol must specify reference test, scoring manual, blinding, central adjudication, allowable testing interval, locked algorithm, subgroup enrollment, and statistical analysis.

Include or explicitly exclude minors, pregnancy, major cardiopulmonary disease, implanted devices, and conditions or medications affecting signals. No excluded or unvalidated population should be silently exposed through default distribution.

### Internal launch criteria

Final thresholds must be regulator- and risk-approved before enrollment. As provisional internal controls:

- At least **90% of representative users** correctly understand screening versus diagnosis, the limits of a negative result, the meaning of insufficient data, and the next step.
- Initial controlled-launch confirmatory-test completion target: **at least 60%**, unless local access constraints justify an alternative approved by clinical safety and ethics leadership.
- Target median time to confirmatory evaluation: **30 days or less**, subject to market-specific clinical norms.
- At least **80% of participating clinicians** rate the report useful or actionable.
- No unmitigated critical safety risk or clinically unacceptable signal of delayed care, false reassurance, anxiety, or unnecessary testing.

These are internal gates, not universal regulatory requirements.

### Algorithm and correction governance

Lock the model and thresholds before pivotal validation. Classify changes as:

- **Non-material:** no plausible effect on output.
- **Performance-affecting:** requires regression testing and review.
- **Material:** changes intended use, target population, model architecture, inputs, thresholds, or clinically relevant performance; requires regulatory assessment and potentially new validation.

If historical results change, determine whether to recalculate, withdraw, relabel, or notify users and clinicians. Preserve an auditable record of original and corrected outputs.

## 4. Regional rollout

### European Union

Treat sleep-apnea screening as regulated medical-device software where it identifies possible disease or provides information used for diagnostic decisions.

Document:

- Whether the wearable, app, and algorithm are one device, accessories, or separate products.
- The applicable MDR classification rule.
- Clinical-evaluation and investigation requirements.
- QMS, software, cybersecurity, technical-file, UDI, vigilance, and economic-operator obligations.
- GDPR health-data controls.
- Applicable EU artificial-intelligence requirements.
- Country-specific language, advertising, reimbursement, distribution, and clinical-workflow requirements.

Launch initially through academic sleep centers, hospitals, primary-care networks, or qualified testing partners. Do not assume EU conformity eliminates national implementation differences.

### Japan

Run a separate PMDA/MHLW program. Confirm:

- Device class and approval, certification, or notification route.
- Japanese Marketing Authorization Holder.
- Local QMS and post-market obligations.
- Acceptability of foreign clinical data.
- Need for Japanese bridging or local evidence.
- Japanese-language labeling, support, and consumer materials.

Use respected hospitals, universities, and sleep specialists to build credibility.

### United States

Make FDA jurisdiction, classification, and pathway resolution a hard gate. Seek feedback on:

- Whether the product is within FDA device jurisdiction.
- Whether a narrowly framed general-wellness version is viable.
- 510(k), De Novo, or other pathway.
- Reference standard, endpoints, performance, subgroups, human factors, cybersecurity, and software-change controls.

Do not release a US “wellness” version containing disease-specific language, a sleep-apnea risk score, clinical-referral instructions, or diagnostic-looking outputs while classification is pending.

Also address state privacy and consumer-health-data laws, advertising substantiation, product liability, employer/payer distribution, and litigation-discovery risk.

### Emerging markets

Assess each country individually. Launch medical screening only where the company can confirm registration, local representation, privacy compliance, language validation, vigilance, and affordable access to confirmatory care. Where care is inaccessible, offer general sleep-health tracking rather than disease screening.

## 5. Clinician and consumer credibility

### Clinicians

Create an independent advisory group comprising sleep physicians, primary-care clinicians, epidemiologists, nurses, patients, health-equity experts, privacy specialists, and ethicists.

Publish its charter, membership, expertise, conflicts, and dissenting views. Maintain independent publication rights and separate advisory input from commercial approval.

Publish or make available:

- Study protocols and populations.
- Reference standards.
- Subgroup performance and confidence intervals.
- Calibration and indeterminate-result rates.
- Failure modes and limitations.
- Algorithm-version history.
- Negative findings, corrections, discontinued features, and safety signals.

Provide non-diagnostic reports showing date range, repeated results, wear time, signal quality, uncertainty, and a clear separation between device output and clinical interpretation.

### Consumers

Use plain language and distinguish:

- No result.
- Insufficient data.
- No concerning pattern detected.
- Screening signal identified.

Example notification:

> “Your recent sleep data showed a pattern that may be associated with sleep apnea. This feature cannot diagnose sleep apnea. Consider discussing the result with a healthcare professional, particularly if you experience loud snoring, breathing pauses, or daytime sleepiness.”

For insufficient data:

> “We could not obtain enough reliable data to provide a result. This does not mean your sleep is normal.”

Prohibit disease-diagnostic wording, “medical-grade” claims without substantiation, vague “doctor approved” claims, fear-based alerts, green states implying disease exclusion, and immediate upselling after a concerning result.

Measure comprehension, anxiety, false reassurance, follow-up behavior, privacy confidence, and disparities across language, literacy, age, sex, ethnicity, skin tone, body size, disability, and healthcare access.

## 6. Additional-feature sequence

| Priority | Feature | Strategy |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Stress/recovery coaching | Broad wellness launch; no diagnosis of anxiety, depression, burnout, or other conditions |
| 2 | Fall detection | Validate falls, slow collapses, false negatives, battery, connectivity, location, consent, contacts, and emergency-service integration |
| 3 | AF detection | Validate against ECG; control false alerts, anxiety, inappropriate emergency use, and clinician workload |
| 4 | Blood-pressure trends | Distinguish trends from measurements; never imply cuff replacement without authorization |
| 5 | Glucose/metabolic insights | Separate lifestyle coaching from glucose measurement, diabetes management, hypoglycemia, or medication guidance |

AF and fall detection should not scale until safety and escalation infrastructure is mature. Blood pressure and glucose remain option-value programs until technical feasibility, clinical utility, and regulatory pathways are demonstrated.

## 7. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Misclassification or claim spillover | Country-specific intended use, claims registry, release-system enforcement, regulator consultation |
| False negatives | High-sensitivity design, clear limitations, repeat testing, referral guidance, subgroup monitoring |
| False positives and alert burden | Threshold optimization, clinical-utility studies, referral-capacity analysis, clinician workflow testing |
| Demographic performance gaps | Prespecified subgroup enrollment, disparity thresholds, mitigation or market restriction |
| Inadequate follow-up care | Minimum care-access criteria before launch; country-level referral partners |
| Algorithm drift or update harm | Model lock, version control, regression testing, change-impact assessment, historical-result remediation |
| Privacy or cybersecurity failure | Data minimization, consent controls, secure development, local compliance, incident response |
| Physician rejection | Independent evidence, transparent limitations, non-diagnostic reports, practical workflows |
| Consumer misunderstanding | Behavioral comprehension testing, restrained UX, no disease-specific wellness mode |
| Emergency escalation failure | Regional testing of connectivity, location, battery, contacts, service integration, and fallback procedures |
| Regulatory or legal exposure | Local counsel, advertising review, documentation discipline, product-liability and litigation controls |
| Reputational damage from correction | Predefined notification, withdrawal, recalculation, regulator, clinician, and user-remediation procedures |

## 8. Milestones and decision gates

### 2026: Foundation and classification

- Complete country-feature matrix and scorecard.
- Establish Launch Board, RACI, clinical advisory, ethics, and safety structures.
- Select the sleep-apnea target endpoint and intended use.
- Begin EU, Japan, and US consultations.
- Implement QMS, claims controls, privacy, cybersecurity, and post-market systems.
- Complete technical validation and initial human-factors testing.
- Launch only bounded wellness features.
- Select first regulated market by **31 December 2026**.
- Approve feature-market budgets, staffing, referral partners, and critical path.

### 2027: First regulated launch

- Complete locked, prospective sleep-apnea validation.
- Complete subgroup, human-factors, and clinical-utility analyses.
- Complete the first regulatory submission or conformity pathway.
- Launch through controlled clinical and referral partners.
- Monitor performance, comprehension, safety, referral completion, and support load.
- Advance AF and fall-detection programs.

### 2028: Expansion

- Expand sleep apnea to the second regulated market after formal bridging analysis and post-market review.
- Resolve the US pathway and complete required evidence.
- Launch AF or fall detection only where safety infrastructure and care pathways are operational.
- Continue BP and glucose feasibility programs.
- Publish the first transparency and performance report.

### 2029 and beyond: Scale

- Expand to additional cleared markets.
- Add clinician and health-system integrations selectively.
- Evaluate reimbursement and outcomes partnerships.
- Progress BP or glucose claims only after dedicated authorization.
- Conduct ongoing real-world evidence, independent audits, and post-market equity reviews.

---

## Final position

The company should optimize for **durable regulatory, clinical, and consumer credibility rather than maximum initial geographic coverage**.

The key investment is not only the algorithm. It is the reusable health-product operating system: intended-use discipline, clinical evidence, human factors, claims and release control, privacy, cybersecurity, clinician workflow, equitable access, post-market surveillance, and transparent correction.

Building that foundation in 2026 will make the sleep-apnea launch safer, create reusable regulatory capability, and reduce the cost and credibility risk of the remaining health features.
