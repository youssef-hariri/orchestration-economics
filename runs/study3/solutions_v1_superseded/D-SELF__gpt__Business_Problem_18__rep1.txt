# Repositioning Strategy: Keep the Standard. Remove the Friction.

## Executive summary

The brand should not attempt to become a “young” brand. That approach has already produced skepticism among consumers under 35 and risked alienating loyal customers. The stronger opportunity is to make the brand’s existing trust, quality, and expertise more useful in contemporary life.

The strategic idea is:

> **Keep the standard. Remove the friction.**

The quality and dependability customers have trusted for 70 years remain intact. The brand evolves how that value is accessed, understood, used, and experienced—through more relevant occasions, clearer design, greater convenience, improved digital and retail journeys, and credible contemporary partnerships.

The repositioning should focus initially on **one priority younger audience, one current consumer occasion, and one tangible proof point**. Heritage should be used as evidence of capability, not as the main reason to buy. Broad communications should follow demonstrated improvements in product or experience, not precede them.

Success requires three outcomes:

1. Consumers under 35 reinterpret the brand as relevant to a current need.
2. That changed perception produces incremental trial and repeat purchase.
3. Loyal customers retain trust in quality, continuity, and the customer relationship.

---

# 1. Strategic ambition and positioning

## The strategic choice

The brand’s challenge is not low awareness. It is a failure of current meaning. Younger consumers know the brand but do not see a compelling reason to choose it now.

The brand should therefore move from:

> “A respected brand from the past”

to:

> **“A proven brand that makes a contemporary need easier to solve with confidence.”**

This creates relevance without abandoning the heritage that built the business.

## Recommended positioning structure

The final positioning should be tailored to the category’s strongest opportunity, but should follow this structure:

> **For [priority younger segment] in [specific contemporary occasion], [Brand] provides [distinctive current benefit]. It combines the standards people have trusted for 70 years with [specific improvement that makes the benefit easier, faster, more relevant, or more accessible today].**

A working positioning statement is:

> **For people who want dependable quality that fits the way life works today, [Brand] is the trusted choice that combines proven expertise with practical, contemporary solutions. Unlike brands that chase trends or rely only on nostalgia, [Brand] keeps what has earned people’s trust and continually improves how that value is delivered.**

This should not be approved as final language until research identifies the specific occasion and benefit that make the brand distinctive.

## Supporting brand promise

> **The standard stays. The experience moves forward.**

Internally, this can be expressed even more operationally:

> **Keep the standard. Remove the friction.**

This gives the organization a clear test for every proposed change:

- Does it preserve the quality or trust customers value?
- Does it make the brand easier to discover, choose, use, access, or recommend?
- Is the improvement meaningful enough to justify changing behavior?

## Heritage narrative

The brand should explain its evolution simply:

> **The standards people trust are staying. We are updating how people experience them today.**

Heritage should prove:

- consistency;
- product expertise;
- quality discipline;
- resilience;
- customer understanding;
- an ability to adapt across previous generations.

The brand should not lead with “70 years” unless the anniversary directly supports a current benefit. Longevity is a reason to believe, not a complete value proposition.

# 2. Target audiences and consumer insight

## Primary growth audience: younger functional adopters

The first priority should be consumers under 35 who:

- recognize the brand;
- have a frequent or commercially meaningful category need;
- value reliability, convenience, quality, or confidence;
- currently choose newer alternatives because they appear easier, clearer, or more relevant;
- are open to established brands when those brands demonstrate present-day usefulness.

This group offers the most practical route to growth because the barrier is likely not rejection of the category, but lack of a current reason to choose this brand.

## Secondary audience: younger identity-seeking consumers

A second group uses brand choices to express taste, standards, values, or belonging. They are skeptical of manufactured heritage and will not be persuaded by a visual makeover alone.

The brand can earn this audience by demonstrating:

- a credible point of view;
- real standards rather than advertising claims;
- participation in relevant communities;
- useful expertise;
- authentic collaboration with practitioners and creators.

This audience should not determine the entire brand strategy in the first phase. It should be engaged through credible proof and participation, not through forced cultural language.

## Loyal heritage customers

Loyal customers are the commercial and reputational foundation of the transition. They value:

- dependable quality;
- familiarity;
- reduced purchase risk;
- continuity;
- family or personal rituals;
- being treated with respect.

They should be positioned as participants in the next chapter, not as obstacles to progress.

The reassurance should be explicit:

> **We are expanding the brand’s relevance, not replacing the people who built it. The products and standards you trust remain central, while new formats and experiences make the brand useful to more people.**

## Lapsed and rejecting consumers

These consumers should be a later recovery opportunity. They may associate the brand with:

- outdated design;
- limited choice;
- poor convenience;
- old-fashioned values;
- a lack of cultural relevance.

They will require evidence of substantive change. They should not be the first audience used to judge the strategy.

## Core consumer insight

Younger consumers do not necessarily reject established brands. They reject brands that ask them to respect history without showing how that history helps them now.

The opportunity is to make the brand’s accumulated expertise solve a contemporary problem better—more simply, conveniently, confidently, or credibly—than newer alternatives.

# 3. Preserve, evolve, retire

## Preserve: non-negotiable equities

### Product and trust

Preserve:

- core product quality and safety;
- proven performance;
- signature products and recognizable benefits;
- customer service standards;
- dependable availability;
- honest, evidence-based claims.

These are the elements that loyalists actually trust and younger consumers may be willing to value if presented in a contemporary context.

### Distinctive brand assets

Retain the name and the two or three strongest recognizable assets, such as:

- logo structure;
- signature color;
- packaging silhouette;
- symbol;
- sound;
- product shape;
- recognizable retail presence.

These assets should be modernized selectively, not erased. Recognition is a competitive advantage, especially in crowded retail and digital environments.

### Authentic heritage

Preserve stories that demonstrate:

- how quality was established;
- how the brand solved problems in earlier eras;
- how products or methods have evolved;
- how customers have used the brand across generations;
- how the organization has previously adapted successfully.

Avoid treating every historical detail as sacred. Preserve heritage that supports the new promise.

### Customer relationship

Preserve:

- respect for loyal customers;
- familiar hero products;
- established service commitments;
- transparent communication;
- continued access to the products customers rely on.

## Evolve: visible but controlled modernization

### Product and format

Develop one bridge offer connected to the priority occasion. It could be:

- a more convenient format;
- a starter or trial bundle;
- a contemporary size or configuration;
- a service enhancement;
- a personalization, refill, repair, or digital feature;
- a clearer solution for an emerging routine.

The offer must retain the brand’s core quality cues. It should make the brand easier to try, not signal that the original brand has been abandoned.

### Packaging

Evolve packaging through:

- clearer hierarchy;
- improved readability;
- easier navigation;
- contemporary photography or illustration;
- more useful product information;
- improved portability, accessibility, or sustainability where credible.

Maintain recognizable core assets during the first wave. Do not redesign every product simultaneously.

### Voice

Move from:

- formal to clear;
- institutional to human;
- nostalgic to useful;
- broad claims to specific proof;
- one-way declarations to invitations to participate.

Do not use slang, memes, or youth-coded language unless it occurs naturally through a credible partner or community.

### Digital and retail experience

Improve the customer journey at the largest points of friction:

- product discovery;
- search;
- comparison;
- checkout;
- delivery;
- customer support;
- product education;
- retail navigation;
- availability.

The brand’s strong distribution should become a contemporary advantage: easy to find, easy to understand, and easy to buy.

### Representation

Show the real breadth of people who use or could credibly benefit from the brand. Representation should reflect actual customers, practitioners, employees, and communities—not simply younger casting added to an unchanged story.

## Retire or reduce

Retire or significantly reduce:

- nostalgia without a current reason to buy;
- anniversary-led messaging as the dominant campaign idea;
- “young and cool” language;
- generic influencer activity;
- borrowed trend codes;
- unsupported innovation, purpose, inclusion, or sustainability claims;
- the visual and verbal devices associated with previous failed modernization;
- simultaneous changes to logo, packaging, product range, tone, and campaign world;
- communications that present younger consumers as replacing older ones;
- separate “old customer” and “young customer” versions of the brand.

# 4. Brand expression and experience guidance

## Brand character

The brand should feel:

- experienced, not old;
- confident, not defensive;
- useful, not institutional;
- warm, not sentimental;
- distinctive, not trendy;
- open, not performative.

## Visual expression

The visual system should retain recognition while improving relevance:

- preserve the most distinctive core asset;
- simplify secondary elements;
- improve digital responsiveness and accessibility;
- use contemporary compositions and real-world settings;
- show products in current routines rather than only in nostalgic contexts;
- balance archival material with present-day proof.

A useful rule is:

> **Do not hide the brand’s age; show what its experience enables now.**

## Verbal expression

Use specific, demonstrable language:

- “Designed to make…”
- “Tested for…”
- “Easier to…”
- “Trusted for…”
- “Improved based on…”
- “Built to…”

Avoid vague claims such as:

- “reimagined for a new generation”;
- “the future of…”;
- “young, bold, and different”;
- “a whole new brand.”

Those phrases announce a transformation without giving consumers a reason to believe it.

## Experience principles

Every touchpoint should reinforce four principles:

1. **Recognizable:** Customers can identify the brand quickly.
2. **Useful:** The experience makes a current task easier.
3. **Proven:** Claims are supported by evidence.
4. **Inclusive across generations:** Younger consumers can enter without making loyalists feel excluded.

# 5. Channel and content strategy

Channels should perform different jobs rather than repeat one broad campaign.

## Owned channels: reassure and explain

Use the website, email, CRM, packaging, customer service, and loyalty channels to:

- explain what is changing and what is not;
- demonstrate product improvements;
- provide usage guidance;
- share authentic heritage proof;
- invite feedback;
- support trial and repeat.

Existing customers should hear about significant changes directly before encountering them unexpectedly in stores.

## Social and creator channels: demonstrate contemporary usefulness

Prioritize:

- product demonstrations;
- real customer routines;
- practitioner advice;
- comparisons and problem solving;
- behind-the-scenes quality proof;
- responses to customer questions;
- credible community participation.

Creators should be selected for expertise, trust, or genuine category involvement—not only follower count.

## Search, e-commerce, and retail media: capture active demand

Use these channels to help consumers:

- understand product differences;
- find the right format;
- compare options;
- solve a specific need;
- move quickly from consideration to purchase.

The product page and shelf should deliver the positioning without requiring consumers to have seen the campaign.

## Retail: convert relevance into trial

Activate the distribution advantage through:

- clearer shelf navigation;
- occasion-based merchandising;
- trial sizes or introductory bundles;
- sampling and demonstrations;
- QR-enabled education;
- retailer media tied to the priority need;
- improved availability and replenishment.

## Experiential: provide useful participation

Prioritize experiences that deliver practical value:

- workshops;
- demonstrations;
- local community programs;
- skill-sharing;
- product testing;
- intergenerational learning;
- relevant cultural or category events.

The brand should contribute expertise, access, or utility—not simply display branding.

## Content pillars

### 1. Proven performance

Show what the product does and why it can be trusted.

### 2. Made for current routines

Demonstrate contemporary occasions without stereotyping younger consumers.

### 3. Expertise in action

Feature employees, makers, practitioners, and customers who make the brand’s standards tangible.

### 4. Heritage with a purpose

Use history only when it explains a present-day capability or benefit.

### 5. Shared knowledge

Pair experience and new perspectives naturally, without assigning older people to the past and younger people to the future.

# 6. Credible campaign and activation examples

These examples should be adapted to the category and tested before investment.

## Campaign: “Still Sets the Standard”

Show a current consumer problem, demonstrate the product solving it, and then reveal the heritage as proof of the standard behind the result.

Example structure:

1. A younger consumer faces a real, contemporary need.
2. A practitioner or customer demonstrates the solution.
3. The brand explains what makes the product reliable.
4. A concise heritage reference shows how that capability was earned.
5. The consumer buys or uses the product in a real channel.

The emphasis is on performance now, not nostalgia then.

## Content platform: “Passed On. Put to Work.”

Pair experienced customers or specialists with younger users who apply that knowledge in current contexts.

The older participant is not presented as a symbol of the past, and the younger participant is not used as a symbol of novelty. Both contribute expertise and perspective.

## Retail activation: “Find Your Fit”

Create an occasion-based retail and e-commerce program that helps consumers select the right product, format, or bundle for a current need.

Components could include:

- simple product comparison;
- starter options;
- in-store demonstrations;
- QR-linked how-to content;
- first-purchase incentives;
- follow-up CRM for repeat use.

This makes the brand’s distribution strength part of its modern relevance.

## Product or service pilot: “The Easier Way”

Launch one focused improvement that removes a known barrier for younger consumers—for example, ease of use, portability, accessibility, information, delivery, or customization.

The campaign should document the improvement and the customer feedback behind it. This is more credible than claiming the entire brand has been transformed.

## Community activation: “Standards in Practice”

Partner with credible local practitioners, educators, or community organizations to provide useful sessions related to the category. The brand contributes product, expertise, or access.

Success should be measured through participation, trial, repeat, and local consideration—not event attendance alone.

# 7. Phased transition sequence

## Phase 0: Choose the opportunity — Months 0–3

### Objectives

- Identify the specific younger consumer occasion to own.
- Define the enduring brand truth.
- Establish what loyalists consider non-negotiable.
- Audit gaps between the desired promise and the actual experience.

### Actions

- Research younger adopters, rejectors, lapsed customers, and loyalists.
- Map the category occasions where competitors are winning.
- Test three distinct positioning territories.
- Audit distinctive assets for recognition and datedness.
- Review product, packaging, retail, digital, and service friction.
- Establish the Brand Evolution Council.
- Select one audience, one occasion, and one proof point.

### Decision gate 1: strategic permission

Proceed only if the organization can answer:

- Why should a younger consumer choose the brand now?
- What does the brand do credibly better?
- What must remain unchanged?
- What tangible improvement will support the claim?

## Phase 1: Prove the experience — Months 4–9

### Objectives

- Deliver the minimum meaningful change.
- Test younger trial and acceptance.
- Involve loyal customers before broader rollout.
- Identify operational or reputational weaknesses.

### Actions

- Launch the bridge offer or experience in one or two markets or channels.
- Pilot updated packaging on selected products.
- Improve the relevant digital or retail journey.
- Use practitioner and customer demonstrations.
- Invite loyalists to previews, testing sessions, or advisory panels.
- Monitor complaints, sentiment, service contacts, trial, and repeat intent weekly.

### Decision gate 2: proof and protection

Scale only if the pilot demonstrates:

- improved younger consideration and trial;
- satisfactory product experience;
- credible repeat intent;
- stable or improving loyalist trust;
- no material deterioration in core sales;
- operational readiness across supply, retail, service, and digital.

## Phase 2: Reframe the brand — Months 10–18

### Objectives

- Introduce the new positioning publicly.
- Connect the campaign to proven improvements.
- Update expression without sacrificing recognition.

### Actions

- Launch the proof-led brand platform.
- Roll out the updated visual system first in priority products and channels.
- Expand creator, retail, and e-commerce content.
- Communicate continuity directly to loyal customers.
- Scale the strongest activation, not every pilot idea.
- Provide employees and retailers with clear explanations and execution guidance.

The broad campaign should not be the first evidence of change. Consumers should encounter the improved product or experience before or alongside the new message.

## Phase 3: Scale and institutionalize — Months 19–36

### Objectives

- Build repeat behavior and sustained relevance.
- Expand proven offers.
- Make the new positioning part of business operations.

### Actions

- Extend successful formats and services.
- Retire dated expressions gradually.
- Update product innovation briefs, agency scopes, training, and incentives.
- Continue a balanced mix of contemporary proof and selective heritage.
- Review loyalist trust and younger brand equity annually.
- Reallocate funding from low-performing modernization activity to proven growth levers.

### Decision gate 3: portfolio and identity scale

Scale further only when growth is incremental, repeatable, and not dependent on discounting, novelty, or one campaign burst.

# 8. Involving and reassuring the loyal base

Loyalists should be engaged early and treated as a source of credibility.

## Recommended actions

- Establish a standing loyal-customer advisory group.
- Invite long-standing customers to preview product and packaging changes.
- Feature real loyal customers in stories about current use, not only historical memories.
- Communicate directly through CRM, packaging, retail, and customer service.
- Preserve hero products and familiar quality cues.
- Explain changes in terms of improved choice and convenience.
- Offer early access or trial of new formats without forcing migration.
- Provide customer-service scripts that clarify continuity.

The message should be:

> **Nothing essential about the quality you trust is being discarded. We are making that quality easier to use and opening the brand to more contemporary needs.**

Loyalist feedback should inform the transition but should not create a veto over every visual or verbal change. The goal is reassurance, not paralysis.

# 9. Authenticity and backlash safeguards

## Proof before promise

No major communication should claim modernization without a corresponding improvement in:

- product;
- service;
- packaging;
- convenience;
- accessibility;
- distribution;
- digital experience; or
- a demonstrable current use case.

## Heritage accuracy

Review all historical claims and archive use for accuracy. Do not romanticize the past or use heritage to avoid discussing current weaknesses.

## Cultural credibility

Before launch, assess:

- whether the language sounds natural;
- whether the partnership has a genuine category connection;
- whether communities have meaningful input;
- whether representation reflects real users;
- whether the work appears opportunistic;
- whether the brand can sustain the claimed behavior after the campaign ends.

## Controlled migration

Change recognizable elements in stages. Avoid simultaneous changes to the name, logo, packaging system, product portfolio, tone, media strategy, and retail presence.

## Backlash response

If criticism emerges:

1. Acknowledge it quickly.
2. Clarify what is changing and what remains protected.
3. Provide evidence of the improvement.
4. Correct factual or executional problems.
5. Do not dismiss loyalists as resistant or younger critics as uninformed.
6. Pause or revise work if criticism reveals a real experience or credibility gap.

## Stop conditions

Pause a rollout if:

- loyalist trust declines materially for two consecutive periods;
- core product sales fall beyond an agreed threshold;
- complaints suggest perceived quality reduction;
- younger consideration rises without trial;
- trial fails to convert into repeat;
- partners or communities identify the work as performative or exploitative;
- retail and service teams cannot deliver the promise consistently.

# 10. Organizational governance

## Brand Evolution Council

Create a senior cross-functional council chaired by the business or brand leader, with representation from:

- marketing;
- product and innovation;
- operations;
- sales and retail;
- e-commerce and digital;
- customer service;
- finance;
- legal and compliance;
- human resources;
- regional teams.

The council should own the transition, not just approve advertising.

## Decision rights

The council should approve:

- the enduring brand promise;
- non-negotiable brand equities;
- the priority younger occasion;
- bridge products and experience changes;
- identity migration;
- major partnerships;
- pilot expansion;
- backlash responses.

## Initiative scorecard

Every major initiative must answer:

1. What current consumer problem does it solve?
2. What is the brand’s right to play?
3. What operational proof supports the claim?
4. Which recognizable assets remain?
5. How will loyalists experience the change?
6. What will be stopped or simplified to support it?
7. How will it be piloted?
8. What result would cause the organization to stop?

## Employee activation

Employees need a concise explanation:

> **The quality and standards people know are staying. We are improving how the brand fits current needs and making it easier for more people to use.**

Train customer-facing teams on:

- the new promise;
- protected elements;
- product changes;
- customer questions;
- feedback escalation;
- claims substantiation;
- how to explain continuity without sounding defensive.

# 11. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Likely consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic modernization | Younger consumers identify the work as inauthentic | Fix one meaningful experience or product issue before broad communication |
| Heritage dilution | Loss of recognition and loyalist trust | Preserve name, hero products, and strongest distinctive assets; migrate gradually |
| Generic positioning | No clear reason to choose the brand | Focus on one priority occasion and one differentiated benefit |
| Over-targeting youth | Existing customers feel rejected | Use a shared human benefit and communicate continuity explicitly |
| Tokenistic creators or representation | Cultural backlash and low credibility | Select partners for genuine category relevance and give them substantive roles |
| Too many initiatives | Inconsistent execution and weak impact | Fund one proof point first; scale only what works |
| Experience gap | Campaign promise exceeds delivery | Require operational sign-off before launch |
| Younger trial without repeat | Short-term volume but no sustainable growth | Measure cohort repeat, satisfaction, and full-price behavior |
| Loyalist overprotection | Brand remains stagnant | Treat quality and trust as non-negotiable, but test expression and experience |
| Internal conflict | Delays and inconsistent decisions | Use a senior council, one-page guardrails, and formal decision gates |
| Trend dependence | Relevance disappears when the trend fades | Build platform around enduring consumer needs and category truth |
| Discount-led growth | Margin erosion and weak brand equity | Track full-price conversion and incremental demand |

# 12. Measurement framework

Measurement must distinguish attention from genuine repositioning and incremental growth.

## Leading indicators

Track monthly or quarterly by audience:

- perceived contemporary relevance;
- consideration;
- perceived fit with current lifestyles;
- understanding of the priority use case;
- perceived quality and trust;
- brand distinctiveness;
- recognition of core assets;
- campaign credibility;
- creator or partner credibility;
- product-page engagement;
- retail consideration;
- customer-service sentiment;
- employee understanding of the positioning.

## Behavioral indicators

Track:

- under-35 trial rate;
- trial-to-repeat conversion;
- repeat purchase within a defined period;
- full-price conversion;
- incremental revenue from younger cohorts;
- bridge-product velocity;
- availability and distribution;
- e-commerce conversion;
- category share in the priority occasion;
- customer lifetime value by cohort;
- cannibalization versus incremental sales;
- sampling-to-purchase conversion.

## Loyalist protection indicators

Track:

- loyalist retention;
- core-product sales;
- trust and quality perceptions;
- purchase frequency;
- complaints linked to perceived abandonment;
- complaints linked to product quality or packaging;
- customer-service resolution time;
- sentiment regarding respect for heritage;
- migration from legacy products to new formats.

## Execution indicators

Track:

- packaging and retail compliance;
- product availability;
- service-team readiness;
- digital task completion;
- claim substantiation;
- partner adherence;
- feedback-to-improvement cycle time;
- employee confidence in explaining the transition.

## Test design

Where possible, use matched-market or geo-testing:

- pilot markets receive the new offer and communications;
- comparable control markets retain existing activity;
- compare younger trial, repeat, core sales, loyalist retention, and brand measures;
- control pricing, distribution, and promotional differences as far as possible.

Set baselines before launch and define success thresholds at each decision gate. Social impressions, views, likes, and earned reach should be treated as diagnostic indicators—not as proof of repositioning success.

## Balanced success scorecard

A practical executive scorecard should include:

### Growth
- under-35 consideration and trial;
- repeat purchase;
- incremental revenue and margin;
- share in the priority occasion.

### Continuity
- loyalist retention;
- core-product sales;
- trust and quality perceptions;
- recognition of distinctive assets.

### Relevance
- contemporary fit;
- message association;
- product and experience satisfaction;
- earned credibility.

### Execution
- availability;
- retail compliance;
- digital conversion;
- employee readiness;
- customer-service performance.

---

# Conclusion

The brand does not need to erase its 70-year history to become relevant to consumers under 35. It needs to convert that history into a contemporary advantage.

The winning strategy is to preserve the standards, trust, recognition, and genuine customer relationships that built the brand while removing the practical and symbolic friction that makes it feel outdated. That means selecting one current occasion, delivering one meaningful proof point, involving loyalists early, and only then scaling the message and visual expression.

Younger consumers should encounter a brand that is easier to choose, more useful in present-day life, and credible in its standards. Loyal customers should encounter the same trusted quality, expressed with greater convenience and choice. Relevance will come not from pretending the brand is young, but from proving that its experience still matters now.
