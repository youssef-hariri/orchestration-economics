# Operations Improvement Plan: Reduce Quoted Lead Time Without Sacrificing Certified Quality

## 1. Executive recommendation and target state

### Recommendation

Do not attempt to reduce every order from 14 weeks to 6 weeks immediately. Instead, establish a **segmented, evidence-based promise model** and reduce lead time by removing waiting, queueing, rework, and uncertainty—not by shortening or bypassing certified inspections.

The recommended approach is to:

1. **Measure actual elapsed time** from order acceptance through shipment, separating touch time from waiting, supplier, approval, inspection, and rework time.
2. **Pilot improvements on one repeatable product family** with stable demand and established certification requirements.
3. **Create a standard/configured product segment** capable of a materially shorter promise.
4. **Overlap engineering, procurement, inspection preparation, and documentation activities** where technically and contractually permissible.
5. **Use controlled postponement and selective inventory** for common, low-risk components and modules.
6. **Implement finite-capacity scheduling and WIP controls** around the true bottlenecks.
7. **Make quality gates earlier, better prepared, and more visible—not weaker.**
8. **Align Sales, Production, Quality, Engineering, and Supply Chain around one shared outcome:** on-time delivery of a conforming, fully documented product.

### Target operating state

| Order segment | Initial validated quote target | Longer-term target |
|---|---:|---:|
| Standard/configured-to-order | 8–10 weeks | 6–8 weeks |
| Controlled variant | 10–12 weeks | 8–10 weeks |
| Complex engineered-to-order | Case-specific, initially 12–14 weeks | Reduce only after separate analysis |

The six-week competitor quote should be treated as a **benchmark and hypothesis**, not an immediate universal commitment. The company should first determine whether the competitor is quoting a comparable configuration, inspection scope, documentation package, and delivery point.

### Initial program targets

Subject to baseline validation, the company should target:

- Reduce median order-to-shipment time for qualifying standard products from approximately 14 weeks to **8–10 weeks within six months**.
- Demonstrate **6–8-week performance** for a controlled pilot segment before commercializing a six-week promise.
- Achieve at least **95% on-time shipment against the validated promise date**.
- Maintain or improve first-pass yield and rolled throughput yield.
- No increase in warranty claims, customer escapes, certification breaches, or traceability failures.
- Reduce rework days, inspection queue time, and nonconformance closure time.
- Eliminate undocumented expedites and informal quality or engineering approvals.

These are targets, not established facts. They should be reset after the first four to six weeks of timestamped analysis.

# 2. Current-state diagnosis and how to measure actual time loss

## Facts currently known

- The company quotes approximately 14 weeks.
- The fastest competitor quotes approximately 6 weeks.
- Sales reports losing roughly one in three deals on delivery speed alone.
- The product requires certified inspections at multiple stages.
- A prior compression attempt increased warranty claims and damaged customer trust for approximately two years.
- Production, Quality, and Sales disagree about the causes of delay.

## Hypotheses requiring validation

The likely causes are:

- Incomplete order information and repeated engineering clarification.
- Sequential engineering and procurement.
- Long or unreliable supplier lead times.
- High WIP and functional production queues.
- Jobs released before all material, information, tooling, or inspection capacity is available.
- Inspection and certificate-review queues.
- Late detection of defects and slow nonconformance disposition.
- Frequent schedule changes caused by aggressive sales commitments.
- Final products waiting for documentation, release, packaging, or shipping.
- The six-week competitor quote may apply only to standard configurations or may use different commercial assumptions.

None of these hypotheses should be accepted as fact until verified with order-level data.

## Measure the complete order timeline

For at least the previous 12–24 months, reconstruct the timeline for each order, segmented by product family and configuration complexity.

### Required timestamps

**Commercial and engineering**

- Quote acceptance.
- Order entry.
- Order completeness.
- Contract and technical review.
- Engineering start.
- First release.
- Final engineering release.
- Customer approval.
- Engineering change dates.

**Procurement and materials**

- Purchase requisition.
- Purchase order.
- Supplier acknowledgment.
- Supplier promised date.
- Actual receipt.
- Incoming inspection request.
- Incoming inspection release.
- Material shortage start and resolution.

**Production**

- Work-order release.
- Operation start and finish.
- Queue entry and exit.
- Setup start and finish.
- WIP moves.
- Rework start and completion.
- Final assembly completion.

**Quality and certification**

- Inspection request.
- Inspector assigned.
- Inspection start.
- Inspection result.
- Hold or nonconformance creation.
- Engineering disposition.
- Retest or reinspection.
- Final inspection.
- Certificate-package completion.
- Quality release.

**Shipping**

- Packaging start and finish.
- Commercial/export release.
- Carrier booking.
- Shipment.

## Quantify touch time versus waiting time

For each order, calculate:

\[
\text{Order-to-ship lead time}
=
\text{touch time}
+
\text{queue time}
+
\text{supplier time}
+
\text{approval time}
+
\text{rework time}
+
\text{administrative and transport time}
\]

Report both median and P90 performance. Averages alone will hide variability and exceptional delays.

### Recommended lead-time-loss categories

| Category | Example losses |
|---|---|
| Customer/commercial | Missing technical data, late approvals, contract review |
| Engineering | Queue, clarification loops, revisions, release batching |
| Procurement | PO processing, supplier acknowledgment, supplier production |
| Material | Shortage, shipment, incoming inspection, missing certificates |
| Production | Work-center queue, setup, priority changes, material moves |
| Quality | Inspector availability, test-equipment queue, documentation review |
| Rework | Defect repair, MRB disposition, retest, repeat inspection |
| Final release | Certificate reconciliation, packaging, export documentation |
| Shipping | Carrier availability, customer release, transport |

### Hold-code discipline

Every blocked order must have one visible status:

- Ready.
- In process.
- Awaiting customer.
- Awaiting engineering.
- Awaiting supplier.
- Awaiting material.
- Awaiting production capacity.
- Awaiting quality.
- Awaiting disposition.
- Awaiting documentation.
- Released.

Each hold requires:

- Named owner.
- Cause code.
- Next action.
- Due date.
- Aging.
- Escalation threshold.

This will prevent cross-functional arguments from replacing evidence.

# 3. Prioritized improvement initiatives

## Priority 1: Stabilize the process and define a “complete order”

**Owner:** COO or General Manager, with Commercial Operations  
**Timing:** First 30 days

Create a single definition of an order that is ready to enter the committed lead-time clock. It should include:

- Approved configuration.
- Complete customer technical information.
- Contract and commercial review.
- Applicable codes and standards.
- Defined inspection and certification scope.
- Customer witness requirements.
- Credit and export approvals.
- No unresolved technical clarification.

Sales may quote an accelerated class only when the order fits an approved configuration and a capacity window.

### Expected benefit

Removes hidden waiting and prevents Production and Engineering from receiving unstable work. The actual benefit must be measured rather than assumed.

---

## Priority 2: Establish finite-capacity scheduling and WIP control

**Owner:** Operations  
**Timing:** Days 30–90

Replace broad batch scheduling with a rolling, constraint-based schedule that considers:

- Released engineering status.
- Material availability.
- Qualified personnel.
- Tooling and equipment.
- Inspection and test capacity.
- Setup sequence.
- Customer witness points.
- Shipment date.

### Operating rules

1. Do not release a job without complete information, required material status, tooling, and planned quality capacity.
2. Do not start work solely to improve utilization.
3. Limit WIP before constrained resources.
4. Freeze the near-term schedule, initially for approximately five working days.
5. Require cross-functional approval for changes that displace another order.
6. Reserve a small, controlled capacity buffer for genuine emergencies.
7. Recalculate the complete critical path after any major material, engineering, or quality event.

### Priority

First address the work center or inspection resource that contributes most to order-level delay—not simply the department with the highest utilization.

---

## Priority 3: Overlap engineering, procurement, inspection preparation, and documentation

**Owners:** Engineering, Supply Chain, Quality  
**Timing:** Days 30–120

Safe overlap can include:

- Purchasing approved common components before final order-specific engineering is complete.
- Releasing standard engineering modules earlier where interfaces are controlled.
- Preparing inspection plans, gauges, fixtures, and inspectors before production completion.
- Reviewing supplier certificates before material arrives.
- Preparing the quality dossier while physical production is underway.
- Booking customer or third-party witnesses when the operation is scheduled.
- Performing independent document reviews in parallel with physical work.

The principle is:

> Compress preparation and waiting; do not compress required acceptance activities.

No activity may proceed from provisional drawings, verbal approvals, or incomplete certification requirements.

---

## Priority 4: Standardize the product and postpone final customization

**Owners:** Engineering and Operations  
**Timing:** Months 2–6

Classify product content into:

1. Common, forecastable components.
2. Approved configurable modules.
3. Repeatable order-specific elements.
4. New or technically high-risk elements.

Develop standard bills of material, routings, inspection plans, drawings, and work instructions for categories 1 and 2.

### Controlled postponement

Prebuild only components or modules that are:

- Common across multiple orders.
- Low risk of engineering change.
- Stable in demand.
- Easily serialized or lot-traced.
- Covered by an approved quality plan.

Final customer-specific work—such as drilling, fitting, software, labeling, final assembly, or performance configuration—should remain downstream after order confirmation.

Do not prebuild safety-critical, pressure-boundary, customer-specific, or certificate-dependent items unless Engineering, Quality, and Regulatory have approved the approach.

---

## Priority 5: Reduce supplier-driven delay

**Owner:** Supply Chain/Procurement  
**Partners:** Quality and Engineering  
**Timing:** Months 2–6

Segment suppliers by:

- Criticality.
- Lead-time contribution.
- Lead-time variability.
- Defect rate.
- Documentation performance.
- Capacity and continuity risk.

For critical suppliers, establish:

- Confirmed lead-time and capacity commitments.
- Required acknowledgment times.
- Quality agreements.
- Certificate requirements.
- Corrective-action response times.
- Change-notification obligations.
- Escalation contacts.
- Dual-source or contingency plans where technically and regulatorily feasible.

A second source is not considered available until it is technically qualified, commercially viable, and approved under applicable certification requirements.

---

## Priority 6: Reduce rework and accelerate nonconformance disposition

**Owners:** Quality Manager and Engineering Manager  
**Timing:** First 60 days onward

Set response standards such as:

- Containment of critical defects during the same shift.
- Technical triage within one business day.
- Defined disposition times by severity.
- Documented repair, retest, and reinspection requirements.
- Weekly Pareto review of recurring defects.

Use first-piece approval, mistake-proofing, standard work, calibrated tooling, operator qualification, preventive maintenance, and supplier corrective actions to prevent defects rather than relying on final inspection.

Rework must remain visible. No order should be reported complete before all required rework, retesting, records, and approvals are complete.

---

## Priority 7: Digitize the quality and manufacturing record

**Owners:** Quality Systems and IT  
**Timing:** Months 2–9

Implement, initially for the pilot product family:

- Electronic travelers.
- Barcode or serial-number scanning.
- Automatic linkage of order, serial number, lot, drawing revision, operator, inspector, and equipment.
- Required-field validation.
- Role-based approvals.
- Audit trails.
- Alerts for missing certificates or inspection records.
- Digital certificate packages.

Digital records should prevent omission and make status visible. They must not turn into automatic or unreviewed approvals.

# 4. Protection of certified quality gates

## Non-negotiable policy

No lead-time initiative may:

- Remove a mandatory inspection.
- Reduce required testing or sampling without formal technical justification.
- Use unqualified personnel, suppliers, materials, or processes.
- Permit production from unreleased engineering data.
- Use verbal or retrospective approvals.
- Ship with an open critical nonconformance.
- Ship without the required certificate package.
- Remove required inspector independence or segregation of duties.
- Break serial, lot, material, or configuration traceability.

Quality and Regulatory/Certification retain independent release and stop-work authority. Sales and Production cannot override a certified gate to meet a customer date.

## Gate-by-gate operating model

| Gate | Improvement allowed | Control that remains |
|---|---|---|
| Contract/configuration review | Standard templates and early review | Requirements must be complete and unambiguous |
| Design and quality-plan release | Modular release with defined interfaces | No build from provisional or verbal data |
| Incoming material verification | Advance certificate review and scheduled inspection | No use of quarantined or uncertified material |
| First-piece/in-process inspection | Inspection at source and advance preparation | Required characteristics and acceptance criteria remain |
| Special-process verification | Prequalified suppliers and early capacity booking | Qualified process, personnel, and records remain mandatory |
| Functional/performance test | Prepare fixtures, software, and test plans in advance | Required test must still be performed and passed |
| Final inspection/configuration audit | Parallel document review and dossier prebuild | Full final acceptance and configuration verification remain |
| Certificate and shipment release | Digital checklists and early record assembly | Authorized release only after complete objective evidence |

Any proposed change to inspection frequency, scope, or acceptance criteria requires:

- Formal change control.
- Risk assessment.
- Technical justification.
- Quality and Regulatory approval.
- Customer or third-party approval where required.
- Defined monitoring after implementation.

## Quality stop criteria

Stop expansion of the shorter promise if there is:

- A safety-critical or regulatory escape.
- Missing, altered, or retrospective certified records.
- Lost traceability.
- Use of unreleased engineering data.
- Use of unapproved material or supplier.
- A critical nonconformance shipped or inadequately contained.
- Statistically meaningful deterioration in critical-characteristic capability.
- Warranty or customer-escape trends above the agreed control limit.
- Materially worse quality performance on expedited orders.

# 5. Inventory, supplier, capacity, and scheduling changes

## Inventory policy

Use a selective inventory strategy:

| Material type | Recommended policy |
|---|---|
| Common, stable-demand, long-lead | Safety stock, kanban, or supplier-held stock |
| Common, short-lead | Min/max replenishment |
| High-value, low-volume | Firm-order purchase or reserved supplier capacity |
| Customer-specific | Purchase after approved order and design release |
| Safety-critical | Qualified source, complete traceability, no uncontrolled substitution |
| Change-sensitive or obsolete-prone | Minimal or no prebuild |

Before approving inventory, compare:

\[
\text{Expected value of delay avoided}
>
\text{carrying cost}+\text{obsolescence exposure}+\text{quality exposure}
\]

For expensive or engineering-sensitive items, favor consignment, call-off agreements, or reserved supplier capacity.

All stocked material must remain subject to:

- Quarantine and release status.
- Lot and certificate traceability.
- Shelf-life and preservation controls.
- FIFO or expiry control.
- Periodic quality and inventory audits.

## Supplier changes

Measure suppliers using both delivery and quality:

- P50 and P90 actual lead time.
- On-time delivery.
- Acknowledgment reliability.
- Defect rate.
- Certificate accuracy.
- Corrective-action response.
- Sub-tier risk.
- Capacity during demand peaks.

Critical suppliers should have capacity reservations and escalation triggers. For example, a critical supplier miss of two business days should trigger a path review—not an automatic customer promise.

## Capacity changes

Use capacity additions only after confirming the constraint with data. Options include:

- Cross-training qualified employees.
- Adding inspectors or test capacity.
- Adding a second shift.
- Purchasing or leasing constrained equipment.
- Outsourcing qualified operations.
- Improving setup and campaign sequencing.
- Protecting maintenance and calibration time.

Overtime alone is not a sustainable compression strategy and may recreate the prior warranty problem.

## Scheduling changes

Implement a daily exception review and weekly capacity review. The daily review should address blocked orders and actions; it should not become a forum for repeatedly rewriting the whole schedule.

Create a controlled expedite process:

- Limit expedited work initially to no more than approximately 5% of constrained capacity.
- Require cross-functional approval.
- Identify displaced orders and incremental costs.
- Confirm engineering, material, production, inspection, test, and shipment capacity.
- Track expedited orders separately for quality and warranty performance.
- Suspend the program if expedited work performs materially worse.

# 6. Phased implementation roadmap and owners

## Phase 0: Stabilize and measure — Weeks 0–4

**Executive owner:** COO/GM  
**Program owner:** Operations Excellence or Value Stream Manager

Actions:

- Freeze informal quality and documentation shortcuts.
- Map mandatory quality and certification gates.
- Establish the order-level timestamp baseline.
- Segment product families and configurations.
- Define “order complete.”
- Launch hold codes and owner-based escalation.
- Identify top warranty failure modes.
- Start daily cross-functional order review.

**Exit criteria:**

- Baseline dashboard approved.
- Gate-control matrix approved by Quality and Regulatory.
- Pilot product family selected.
- No accelerated quote issued without feasibility review.

## Phase 1: Remove administrative and queue waste — Weeks 5–10

**Owners:** Operations, Engineering, Quality, Sales Operations

Actions:

- Introduce standard, controlled-variant, and complex quote classes.
- Implement finite-capacity scheduling on the primary constraint.
- Book inspections and tests earlier.
- Launch rapid nonconformance triage.
- Prebuild documentation templates.
- Establish a schedule freeze.
- Require formal expedite approval.
- Begin approved engineering/procurement overlap.

**Expected result:** Initial reduction primarily from administrative and queue time. Quantify after implementation rather than assuming a fixed number of weeks.

## Phase 2: Modularize and decouple — Months 3–5

**Owners:** Engineering, Supply Chain, Operations, Quality Systems

Actions:

- Standardize high-volume modules.
- Establish controlled common-component inventory.
- Introduce supplier-held or consignment stock where appropriate.
- Release standard engineering packages earlier.
- Implement electronic travelers and traceability.
- Cross-train qualified production and inspection personnel.
- Establish supplier capacity agreements.

**Exit criteria:**

- Traceability demonstrated through a complete pilot order.
- Prebuilt inventory remains within approved financial and engineering-change limits.
- First-pass yield is no worse than baseline.
- No certification or record-integrity findings.

## Phase 3: Stabilize constraints and expand — Months 6–9

**Owners:** Operations, Supply Chain, Quality, Engineering

Actions:

- Add measured bottleneck capacity.
- Qualify alternate suppliers where justified.
- Expand finite-capacity scheduling across the value stream.
- Use capability-based optimization only after formal approval.
- Extend the model to controlled variants.

**Target:** Standard products demonstrate 6–8-week actual performance in validated capacity windows; controlled variants move toward 8–10 weeks.

## Phase 4: Commercial rollout — Months 9–12

**Owners:** Sales, COO, Operations, Quality

Actions:

- Commercialize the shorter promise only for validated configurations.
- Publish a lead-time matrix by configuration and capacity band.
- Include customer witnesses, special materials, and certification scope in the quote logic.
- Compare competitor offers on a like-for-like basis.
- Continue warranty surveillance by build cohort.

# 7. Cross-functional governance and incentives

## Governance structure

Establish a **Lead-Time and Quality Steering Committee** chaired by the COO or GM.

| Role | Accountability |
|---|---|
| COO/GM | Enterprise trade-offs, target approval, escalation |
| Program leader | Integrated plan, data, benefits, issue resolution |
| Sales/Commercial | Customer requirements and achievable commercial commitments |
| Engineering | Configuration, design release, technical changes |
| Operations | Capacity, schedule, WIP, execution |
| Quality/Regulatory | Gate criteria, certification, independent release, stop-work authority |
| Supply Chain | Supplier capacity, material availability, inventory |
| Finance | Inventory, expedite economics, business case |
| IT/Systems | ERP, PLM, QMS, MES data integrity |

### Meeting cadence

- **Daily:** Blocked-order and constraint review.
- **Weekly:** Lead-time, quality, supplier, and capacity review.
- **Monthly:** Steering Committee review of targets, investments, risks, and stop/go decisions.

## Shared scorecard

Use common metrics rather than conflicting local goals:

- Median and P90 order-to-ship lead time.
- On-time shipment against validated promise.
- First-pass yield.
- Rolled throughput yield.
- Customer escapes and warranty claims by build cohort.
- Open nonconformance age.
- Certificate-right-first-time rate.
- Engineering release-right-first-time rate.
- Supplier on-time delivery and defect rate.
- WIP age.
- Schedule adherence.
- Expedite rate and cost.
- Cost of poor quality.

### Incentive changes

- **Sales:** Reward orders won with achievable dates and profitable, conforming delivery—not simply the shortest quote.
- **Production:** Reward completed, conforming orders and schedule reliability—not utilization alone.
- **Quality:** Reward prevention, right-first-time release, and escape avoidance—not merely the number of holds.
- **Engineering:** Reward complete, right-first-time release and controlled reuse.
- **Supply Chain:** Reward reliable delivery and quality-adjusted supplier performance—not purchase price alone.
- **Executives:** Reward end-to-end revenue, customer retention, quality, and working-capital performance.

No function should be rewarded for improving its local metric by transferring delay or risk to another function.

# 9. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Warranty or quality escape | Pilot first, preserve all gates, monitor build cohorts, define stop criteria |
| Regulatory or customer certification breach | Gate-by-gate compliance matrix and Quality/Regulatory approval of all changes |
| Inventory obsolescence | Stock only stable common items; use consignment; set financial and revision-risk limits |
| Supplier cannot support increased demand | Validate demonstrated capacity, P90 performance, sub-tier risk, and alternate-source qualification |
| Bottleneck shifts downstream | Recalculate the full critical path after every major change |
| Demand spike | Use capacity bands; suspend short promises above validated capacity |
| Late engineering change | Define configuration freeze; require impact assessment and re-quote |
| Failed inspection | Containment, MRB, documented repair/retest, and no automatic preservation of the original promise |
| Metric gaming | Immutable timestamps, transparent exclusions, matched pilot comparison, and quality-adjusted measures |
| Overtime-driven process instability | Add capacity only with qualification, standard work, maintenance, and first-piece verification |
| Functional conflict persists | Shared scorecard, explicit decision rights, daily exception governance, and executive ownership |
| Sales overpromises | System-controlled quote classes and mandatory capacity/quality feasibility approval |

# 10. First 30/60/90-day action plan

## First 30 days: establish facts and stabilize controls

1. Appoint the COO-sponsored program leader.
2. Select one representative pilot product family.
3. Extract and analyze 12–24 months of order, production, supplier, quality, and warranty data.
4. Map actual critical paths and separate touch from waiting time.
5. Define mandatory certified gates and prohibited shortcuts.
6. Create the single “order complete” checklist.
7. Implement hold codes, owners, due dates, and aging.
8. Start daily Sales–Engineering–Operations–Quality–Supply Chain review.
9. Identify the top five delay causes and top five warranty failure modes.
10. Stop informal expedites, provisional production, and retrospective documentation.

**30-day deliverables:**

- Baseline lead-time analysis.
- Pilot charter.
- Gate-control matrix.
- Initial KPI dashboard.
- Approved quote segmentation.
- Initial bottleneck and supplier-risk list.

## By 60 days: execute flow improvements

1. Start finite-capacity scheduling at the principal constraint.
2. Introduce schedule freeze and formal priority-change approval.
3. Book inspection and test capacity at work-order release.
4. Launch rapid nonconformance triage.
5. Begin engineering/procurement overlap for approved common items.
6. Prebuild digital quality dossiers and document templates.
7. Establish supplier acknowledgment and escalation rules.
8. Create the qualification matrix for production and inspection personnel.
9. Run the first controlled pilot orders.
10. Compare results with historical or concurrent matched orders.

**60-day deliverables:**

- Measured reduction in queue and hold time.
- Pilot performance report.
- Supplier capacity commitments.
- Verified inspection-resource capacity.
- No-gate-bypass audit results.

## By 90 days: validate and prepare controlled scale-up

1. Introduce limited common-component inventory or supplier-held stock where approved.
2. Standardize the first set of repeatable product modules.
3. Implement electronic travelers or an interim controlled traceability process.
4. Review pilot performance against promise, first-pass yield, rework, and quality outcomes.
5. Confirm whether the standard segment can support an 8–10-week commercial quote.
6. Identify the capacity, supplier, and systems investments required to reach 6–8 weeks.
7. Establish pilot stop/go criteria and warranty surveillance.
8. Publish the first capacity-based customer lead-time matrix.
9. Reset targets using P50 and P90 evidence.
10. Present a scale-up recommendation to the Steering Committee.

## Final recommendation

The company should pursue a substantial lead-time reduction, but it should earn the shorter promise through controlled flow and demonstrated capability. The safest and most credible path is:

> **Measure the real delay, standardize what can be standardized, buy or prebuild only what is stable and traceable, schedule finite capacity, prepare inspections and documentation earlier, prevent defects at source, and retain every mandatory certified quality gate.**

A validated 8–10-week promise for standard products is a reasonable initial objective to test. A six-week promise should be offered only after a representative pilot proves that the company can achieve it consistently, at the required quality, certification, warranty, and profitability levels.
