# Building an Innovative International Company

## 1. Diagnosis

Uneven innovation across countries is unlikely to be a simple creativity problem. It usually reflects differences in:

- **Psychological safety:** whether employees can challenge decisions or propose unconventional ideas without career consequences.
- **Leadership behavior:** whether local managers reward curiosity or only error-free execution.
- **Decision rights:** whether offices can run small tests without lengthy headquarters approval.
- **Resources:** protected time, funding, data, technology, skills, and specialist support.
- **Cultural expectations:** attitudes toward hierarchy, public disagreement, individual recognition, and failure.
- **Regulation and market conditions:** privacy, labor, safety, financial, intellectual-property, and sector-specific requirements.
- **Cross-border access:** whether local teams can learn from other offices or obtain headquarters support.

The company should not impose one “correct” innovation culture. It should establish **global minimum conditions**—safety, transparency, responsible experimentation, equitable access, and compliance—while allowing each office to adapt communication, incentives, participation methods, and pilot design to local circumstances.

The central objective is not to maximize ideas or tolerate careless failure. It is to make **safe, disciplined experimentation accessible in every location**.

---

## 2. Global principles and local adaptation

| Standardize globally | Adapt locally |
|---|---|
| Definition of innovation, including products, services, processes, customer experience, technology, and business models | Local customer problems and innovation priorities |
| Non-retaliation for good-faith proposals and respectful challenge | How employees raise concerns: anonymous channels, facilitated meetings, one-to-one discussions, or digital tools |
| Transparent feedback on ideas | Language, communication style, and evaluation forums |
| Minimum access to time, small-scale funding, tools, and expertise | How protected time is scheduled around shifts, local workloads, or customer demands |
| Common experiment stages and evidence standards | Research methods, pilot locations, and customer engagement practices |
| Proportionate legal, privacy, safety, security, and labor review | The precise compliance checkpoints required by each jurisdiction |
| Leadership accountability for openness and inclusion | The form of local leadership sponsorship |
| Core performance measures and equity principles | Local targets adjusted for branch size, maturity, regulation, and market conditions |
| Shared knowledge standards and collaboration infrastructure | Regional communities, meeting times, translation, and peer-learning formats |

Equal treatment does not always produce equitable opportunity. A small or highly regulated office may need additional legal support, translation, analysts, technology, or funding to have a fair chance to innovate.

---

## 3. Specific recommendations

### A. Create psychological safety and responsible risk-taking

Psychological safety must be demonstrated through daily management behavior, not only through corporate messaging.

**Actions**

1. Establish a global non-retaliation standard for good-faith ideas, dissent, and reporting of experiment results.
2. Train managers to:
   - Ask exploratory questions before judging ideas.
   - Invite dissenting and minority views.
   - Admit uncertainty and mistakes.
   - Separate negligence from a well-designed experiment that produced an unfavorable result.
   - Give credit to employees and teams for their contributions.
3. Add questions on managerial openness, fear of consequences, and ability to challenge decisions to regular pulse surveys.
4. Require teams to conduct short retrospectives after experiments:
   - What did we expect?
   - What happened?
   - What did we learn?
   - What should change?
5. Use culturally appropriate channels. In a high-hierarchy office, an anonymous or confidential route may be essential. In another location, a facilitated team discussion may generate more participation.
6. Avoid requiring employees to publicly “celebrate failure.” In some cultures that language may be counterproductive. Use more precise terms such as **responsible testing, evidence-based learning, or assumption review**.

A failed experiment should be treated positively only when the team defined a meaningful question, assessed risks, used resources responsibly, and reported the result honestly.

---

### B. Give employees time, funding, and decision rights

Creativity will remain symbolic if employees have no capacity or authority to act.

**Actions**

- Give each office a modest, rapidly accessible experiment budget.
- Establish funding tiers:
  - **Local grants:** small, low-risk tests approved locally.
  - **Regional funds:** pilots involving multiple teams or customers.
  - **Global scale-up funds:** initiatives with evidence of value and cross-border potential.
- Protect time through a locally workable model: a fixed monthly allocation, scheduled innovation sessions, periodic sprints, or time embedded in improvement teams.
- Provide shared access to data analysts, designers, engineers, legal advisers, privacy specialists, and security experts.
- Define local spending and experimentation authority clearly. A low-risk process test should not require headquarters approval.
- Adjust targets and workloads so innovation time is not simply added to an already full schedule.

For example, a branch might be authorized to spend up to a defined amount on a six-week customer-service pilot, while a test involving personal data, safety risks, regulated products, or material financial exposure would require specialist review.

---

### C. Establish a simple idea-to-experiment process

The company should use one common process, but keep early-stage requirements lightweight.

#### Stage 1: Define the problem

Employees submit a short description of a customer, employee, operational, or market problem. The submission should identify who is affected and why the problem matters.

#### Stage 2: Screen and respond

A local innovation council reviews the proposal against transparent criteria:

- Strategic relevance
- Customer or operational value
- Feasibility
- Potential risks
- Distinctiveness from existing work

Every proposer receives a decision or status update within a defined period, even when the idea is declined.

#### Stage 3: Design a test

The team specifies:

- A testable hypothesis
- Target users or process
- Proposed intervention or prototype
- Measures of success
- Budget and duration
- Compliance requirements
- Stop, revise, or scale criteria

#### Stage 4: Run a controlled experiment

The team conducts the smallest useful test, rather than preparing a large business case prematurely.

#### Stage 5: Review evidence

A review determines whether to:

- Stop because the hypothesis was not supported
- Revise and test again
- Continue with a larger pilot
- Scale more broadly
- Transfer the learning to another office

Every experiment should produce a short learning record, including negative results and unexpected consequences.

#### Risk tiers

- **Tier 1: Low risk:** internal process changes, non-sensitive prototypes, limited customer-service tests. Local approval.
- **Tier 2: Moderate risk:** broader customer pilots, material operational change, new technology, or cross-border involvement. Regional or specialist review.
- **Tier 3: High risk:** personal data, safety, regulated products, employment impacts, cybersecurity, significant financial exposure, or external legal obligations. Formal compliance and executive review.

“Agile” or “experimental” language must never be used to bypass legal, privacy, safety, security, or labor obligations.

---

### D. Redesign incentives and recognition

Reward the quality of innovation behavior, not the volume of ideas or failure itself.

**Reward**

- Clear problem definition
- Customer or employee evidence
- Appropriate risk assessment
- Efficient testing
- Honest reporting of negative results
- Useful learning
- Collaboration across functions and countries
- Reuse, adoption, and responsible scaling
- Helping another office implement or adapt an innovation

**Do not reward**

- Number of ideas submitted without regard to quality
- Spending a budget without evidence
- Rule-breaking
- Failed outcomes that produced no useful learning
- Individual ownership that undermines teamwork
- Labeling routine work as “innovation” merely to obtain recognition

Use a mix of:

- Team recognition
- Development opportunities
- Promotion and career consideration
- Protected time
- Public or private acknowledgment, depending on local norms
- Modest financial awards where legally and culturally appropriate

Include an **innovation contribution** category in performance discussions, but do not make uncertain commercial outcomes the sole basis for individual performance ratings. Review incentives periodically for gaming, unhealthy competition, or bias toward headquarters-based employees.

---

### E. Make participation inclusive and accessible

Idea systems often favor fluent, senior, office-based employees. Design for broader participation.

**Actions**

- Offer multilingual idea submission and evaluation.
- Provide several channels:
  - Digital platform
  - Facilitated workshops
  - Anonymous submissions
  - Frontline team meetings
  - One-to-one manager or champion discussions
  - Customer and supplier feedback
- Include shift workers, field staff, contractors where appropriate, remote employees, and employees with limited digital access.
- Rotate meeting times so global forums do not consistently favor headquarters.
- Use translation, written pre-reads, and facilitation so fluent English speakers do not dominate.
- Track participation and idea outcomes by location, function, seniority, gender, employment type, and language where lawful.
- Ensure local adaptation does not weaken protections against retaliation, discrimination, or exclusion.

Public individual awards may work in one country, while team or community recognition may be more inclusive in another.

---

### F. Build cross-border knowledge sharing without excessive bureaucracy

Knowledge sharing should help offices adapt proven approaches, not force them to copy solutions designed for another market.

**Actions**

- Create global communities of practice around themes such as customer experience, operational efficiency, sustainability, digital services, or new business models.
- Establish an internal expertise marketplace where offices can request coaches, analysts, technical specialists, or pilot partners.
- Fund cross-border experiment teams and paired-office projects.
- Maintain a searchable library containing:
  - Problem addressed
  - Local context
  - Experiment design
  - Results
  - Compliance considerations
  - Implementation requirements
  - What did not work
- Recognize employees who transfer and adapt another office’s innovation.
- Use rotating showcases and short demonstrations rather than large, presentation-heavy events.
- Pair resource-constrained or newer offices with more experienced locations, while avoiding a one-way “headquarters teaches everyone” model.

A solution proven in one market should be treated as a hypothesis elsewhere. Local teams should be authorized to redesign it for local customers, laws, infrastructure, and cultural expectations.

## 4. Roles and governance

### Headquarters and global innovation council

Headquarters should:

- Set the global principles, strategic themes, and minimum protections.
- Provide shared platforms, baseline funding, specialist resources, and training.
- Define risk tiers, data standards, and required compliance controls.
- Monitor equity of access and prevent headquarters bias in funding and attention.
- Sponsor cross-border learning and scale innovations with evidence.
- Remove systemic barriers in procurement, budgeting, data access, and approval processes.
- Measure conditions and outcomes, not merely activity.

The global council should not approve every local experiment. Its role is to set boundaries, allocate larger resources, and resolve cross-border issues.

### Regional or local leaders

Local leaders should:

- Select relevant local problems within global priorities.
- Establish an innovation council or equivalent forum.
- Allocate small grants and protected time.
- Adapt participation and recognition methods to local culture.
- Ensure legal, privacy, labor, safety, and security requirements are met.
- Model openness and protect employees who raise good-faith challenges.
- Review experiments quarterly, emphasizing learning and barriers.
- Share local successes and failures with other offices.

### Managers

Managers are responsible for the day-to-day climate. They should:

- Ask for alternatives and evidence.
- Make time available.
- Give prompt feedback.
- Avoid taking credit for employee ideas.
- Explain why proposals are declined.
- Support responsible tests while stopping unsafe or noncompliant activity.
- Include innovation contributions in regular development conversations.

### Employees and experiment teams

Employees should:

- Identify meaningful problems rather than generate ideas for their own sake.
- Use the experiment process and disclose risks.
- Seek appropriate compliance advice.
- Track evidence and report results honestly.
- Share learning, including negative results.
- Respect local laws, customers, colleagues, and data rights.
- Collaborate across functions and countries.

Decision rights should be documented in a simple approval matrix or RACI, so employees know what they can decide independently and when escalation is required.

## 5. Phased implementation roadmap

### Phase 1: Diagnose and design — 0–3 months

- Survey psychological safety, managerial support, time, autonomy, and resource access.
- Interview employees, managers, local leaders, compliance teams, and employee representatives.
- Map current idea processes, approval times, funding, decision rights, and experiment outcomes.
- Segment offices by size, maturity, market conditions, regulation, and resource constraints.
- Identify existing practices worth learning from, without assuming they transfer unchanged.
- Define global minimum standards and the initial approval matrix.

### Phase 2: Establish foundations — 3–6 months

- Appoint a global sponsor, global council, and local or regional champions.
- Publish non-retaliation, transparency, and responsible-experimentation standards.
- Train managers in psychological safety, inclusive participation, and evidence-based experimentation.
- Launch small grants, protected-time arrangements, and shared specialist support.
- Create multilingual idea channels and a simple feedback commitment.
- Select pilot offices deliberately: include large and small, highly innovative and less active, headquarters and remote, and culturally contrasting locations.

### Phase 3: Pilot and learn — 6–12 months

- Run the common idea-to-experiment process in pilot offices.
- Test different participation formats, incentives, time-allocation models, and cross-border partnerships.
- Use risk-tiered compliance review.
- Conduct monthly operational reviews and quarterly learning reviews.
- Collect employee feedback on bureaucracy, fairness, safety, and usefulness.
- Stop practices that create idea volume without experimentation or learning.

### Phase 4: Scale and connect — 12–18 months

- Refine the framework based on pilot evidence.
- Expand the process to additional offices, with local redesign where necessary.
- Establish global communities of practice and an internal expertise marketplace.
- Increase funding for experiments with validated customer, operational, sustainability, or financial value.
- Integrate successful practices into normal operating processes rather than leaving them as special projects.

### Phase 5: Institutionalize — 18 months and beyond

- Include innovation-supportive leadership behavior in promotion and performance systems.
- Build innovation time and funding into annual planning.
- Review procurement, data access, compliance, and budgeting rules for unintended barriers.
- Repeat innovation-health assessments annually or semiannually.
- Refresh global priorities based on market evidence.
- Continue to compare offices on conditions, learning quality, equity, and impact—not raw idea counts.

## 6. Balanced scorecard

Use the scorecard for diagnosis and improvement, not as a simplistic league table. Compare offices with appropriate context and avoid penalizing regulated branches for running fewer, higher-quality tests.

| Dimension | Leading measures | Lagging measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Psychological safety and leadership** | Survey scores on speaking up, manager openness, and non-retaliation; proportion of managers trained and behavior-assessed; employee reports of constructive feedback | Retention of innovative talent; number and severity of retaliation or conduct cases; sustained improvement in engagement and trust |
| **Access and equity** | Protected innovation hours; grant and specialist access by office; participation by location, function, seniority, gender, language, and employment type; proportion of ideas receiving feedback on time | Distribution of adopted innovations and benefits; representation of smaller or less-visible offices among scaled initiatives |
| **Idea flow** | Number of active contributors; diversity of contributors; time from submission to decision; percentage of proposals receiving clear feedback | Percentage of ideas converted into validated experiments; quality and relevance of ideas adopted |
| **Experiment quality** | Time from approval to first test; percentage with a hypothesis, success measures, stop criteria, and risk review; experiment completion and learning-review rates | Customer adoption, operational improvement, employee impact, sustainability benefits, revenue, cost reduction, or risk reduction |
| **Learning and failure discipline** | Percentage of experiments documenting negative results and changed assumptions; repeat-test rate; evidence of stopping weak initiatives promptly | Reduction in repeated mistakes; value generated from discontinued experiments; speed and quality of later decisions |
| **Cross-border collaboration** | Number of cross-border teams, expertise exchanges, and reused/adapted practices; participation across time zones and languages | Innovations adopted or adapted in multiple markets; benefits from knowledge transfer; reduced duplication |
| **Compliance and responsible risk** | Completion of proportionate privacy, legal, safety, security, and labor reviews; avoidable rework; clarity of risk-tier decisions | Compliance breaches, customer harm, safety incidents, data incidents, or regulatory sanctions linked to experiments |
| **Business and organizational outcomes** | Portfolio balance across product, service, process, and business-model innovation; funding allocation by strategic theme | New revenue, cost savings, customer satisfaction, cycle-time improvements, productivity, resilience, and return on innovation investment |

Set a baseline before launch, report trends rather than isolated numbers, and supplement metrics with confidential interviews and case reviews. A high number of ideas with low experimentation may indicate bureaucracy; a high number of experiments with poor risk controls may indicate reckless incentives.

---

## 7. Key trade-offs and safeguards

- **Consistency versus autonomy:** Standardize principles and risk boundaries, not every meeting or tool.
- **Speed versus compliance:** Use lightweight review for low-risk tests and rigorous review for material risks.
- **Failure tolerance versus accountability:** Reward disciplined learning, not carelessness.
- **Global scale versus local relevance:** Transfer principles and evidence, not unmodified solutions.
- **Competition versus collaboration:** Use team and cross-border recognition to limit knowledge hoarding.
- **Measurement versus bureaucracy:** Maintain a small global core dataset and allow limited local measures.
- **Short-term delivery versus exploration:** Protect explicit time and recognize innovation work in performance systems.
- **Visibility versus inclusion:** Do not equate confident public participation with creativity; provide private, facilitated, and asynchronous alternatives.

## Immediate next steps

1. Appoint a global sponsor and create a cross-functional innovation council.
2. Run a 60-day diagnostic covering safety, leadership, decision rights, resources, regulation, and participation by office.
3. Publish global minimum standards for non-retaliation, feedback, responsible experimentation, and equitable access.
4. Define risk tiers and local approval limits so low-risk tests can proceed quickly.
5. Select a diverse pilot group and provide each location with protected time, small grants, manager training, and specialist support.
6. Launch a simple multilingual idea-to-experiment process and baseline scorecard.
7. Review pilot evidence after six months, remove barriers, and scale only practices that demonstrate learning, inclusion, compliance, and meaningful impact.
