# Japan Campaign: 「毎日に、確かな一歩。」  
### “An informed step in every day”

This campaign is designed for the Japanese market, initially focusing on adults aged 30–59 in Tokyo, Osaka, and other major metropolitan areas. It assumes the product is an orally consumed dietary supplement. The product’s exact ingredients, dosage, intended benefit, evidence, classification, and labeling must be validated in Japan before any public claim is approved.

The campaign’s governing principle is simple:

> Help consumers make an informed choice—not a pressured or medically misleading one.

---

## 1. Market rationale and cultural context

Japan is an attractive market for a carefully managed wellness supplement because consumers commonly value:

- Practical health-maintenance routines.
- Product safety, quality, and ingredient transparency.
- Reputable companies and credible information sources.
- Convenient products that fit work and family life.
- Sustainable habits rather than dramatic short-term transformations.
- Clear instructions and responsible customer support.

These are strategic hypotheses, not assumptions about every Japanese consumer. Consumer research should validate differences by age, region, income, health literacy, product category, and lifestyle.

The campaign should therefore avoid aggressive “miracle” positioning and instead present the supplement as one optional component of a balanced routine involving food, sleep, movement, and professional healthcare when needed.

The product must never be presented as a substitute for:

- A balanced diet.
- Sleep or exercise.
- Prescribed medication.
- A doctor, pharmacist, dietitian, or other qualified healthcare professional.
- Diagnosis or treatment of a medical condition.

---

## 2. Audience

### Primary audience: routine-oriented urban adults aged 30–49

People managing demanding work, commuting, household, or caregiving responsibilities who may value convenient and clearly explained wellness products.

**Needs:**

- Simple usage instructions.
- Confidence in ingredients and quality.
- A product that fits an existing routine.
- Transparent price and purchase terms.

### Secondary audience: adults aged 35–59 balancing work and family

Consumers who may place particular importance on safety, value, responsible use, and information that can be discussed with a pharmacist or healthcare professional.

### Secondary audience: adults aged 50–59 interested in healthy maintenance

Communications should focus on maintaining everyday wellbeing and independence, never on fear of ageing, decline, or becoming a burden.

### Protected or excluded audiences

The campaign should not intentionally target:

- Children or adolescents unless the product is specifically approved for them.
- Pregnant or breastfeeding people unless appropriate safety evidence exists.
- People inferred to have a disease, mental-health condition, fertility concern, or medication use.
- People seeking urgent treatment or a cure.
- Consumers likely to interpret the product as an alternative to professional medical care.

Targeting should use broad geography, contextual content, age ranges where appropriate, and declared wellness interests. It should not rely on inferred sensitive health conditions or medical browsing behavior.

---

## 3. Campaign objectives and KPIs

### Business objectives

During the first six months:

1. Build awareness of the product in selected metropolitan markets.
2. Explain what the supplement contains and how it should be used.
3. Establish credibility through accurate, appropriately modest communication.
4. Generate qualified visits and first purchases.
5. Encourage informed repeat purchase based on product satisfaction, not fear or subscription pressure.
6. Maintain strong consumer-safety, privacy, and advertising-compliance performance.

### Illustrative KPIs

Final targets should be established using Japanese category benchmarks and pilot results.

#### Awareness and consideration

- Aided brand awareness: 10–15% improvement among the exposed test audience.
- Product-category comprehension: at least 90% correctly identify the product as a supplement rather than a medicine.
- Benefit comprehension: at least 70% correctly understand the approved product role.
- Trust and transparency scores: positive movement from baseline.
- Qualified landing-page visits and engagement with ingredient, safety, and usage information.

#### Commercial performance

- Conversion rate by channel and audience.
- Cost per qualified visit and first purchase.
- Checkout abandonment.
- One-time versus recurring-order selection.
- Repeat purchase and customer satisfaction.
- Refund, cancellation, and complaint rates.

#### Consumer welfare and compliance

- Zero substantiated medical, disease-treatment, or unsupported superiority claims.
- 100% of paid creator content with prominent commercial disclosure.
- Percentage of assets mapped to approved claim IDs.
- Time to correct prohibited content.
- Adverse-event reports per 1,000 orders.
- Customer-service response and escalation time.
- No material privacy incidents or health-targeting complaints.

The campaign should not optimize solely for clicks, low acquisition cost, or subscriptions if those outcomes result from consumer misunderstanding.

## 4. Positioning

### Recommended positioning

> For Japanese adults seeking a practical way to support everyday wellness, [Product Name] is a clearly explained supplement that fits into a balanced daily routine, with transparent ingredients, responsible usage guidance, and only evidence-supported benefits.

### Brand promise

> Clear information for a considered everyday choice.

### Positioning boundaries

The product is:

- A supplement, not a medicine.
- An optional addition to a healthy routine.
- A product whose benefits depend on its verified ingredients, dose, formulation, and lawful Japanese claim status.
- Not guaranteed to work for everyone.
- Not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease.

---

## 5. Message architecture

All communications must use the approved Japanese product classification and claim wording. The examples below are strategic drafts, not legal approvals.

### Pillar 1: What it contains

Explain the product factually:

- Ingredient names.
- Quantity per serving.
- Source, where relevant.
- Serving size and recommended use.
- Allergens and suitability information.
- Manufacturing and testing information that can be documented.

**Example copy:**

> 成分・含有量・お召し上がり方をわかりやすくご紹介します。  
> “We clearly explain the ingredients, amounts, and directions for use.”

Do not use “purest,” “safest,” or “highest quality” without appropriate independent substantiation.

### Pillar 2: What it may support

Use only a benefit authorized for the product’s Japanese classification and supported by evidence for the exact formulation and dosage.

**Conditional example:**

> [Approved Japanese functional or nutrient claim.]  
> “[Approved English interpretation.]”

If no specific functional claim has been approved, use factual, non-efficacy language such as:

> 毎日の健康習慣に取り入れやすいサプリメント。  
> “A supplement designed to fit easily into an everyday wellness routine.”

This wording must still be reviewed for its overall implication.

Avoid:

- “Treats,” “cures,” or “prevents” disease.
- “Boosts immunity” unless specifically permitted and substantiated.
- “Detoxifies.”
- “Burns fat.”
- “Anti-ageing.”
- “Clinically proven” without evidence meeting the relevant standard.
- Guaranteed, rapid, permanent, or universal results.
- “Natural, therefore safe.”
- Claims that the product replaces medical treatment.

### Pillar 3: Fits into balanced habits

**Example copy:**

> 食事・睡眠・運動を大切にしながら、毎日の習慣に。  
> “Alongside food, sleep, and movement, make it part of your daily routine.”

This must not imply that the supplement compensates for poor lifestyle habits.

### Pillar 4: Informed choice

**Example copy:**

> ご使用前に、成分・用法・注意事項をご確認ください。  
> “Before use, please review the ingredients, directions, and precautions.”

### Pillar 5: Appropriate limitations

Where relevant to the formula and label:

> 妊娠中・授乳中の方、服薬中の方、治療中の方は、ご使用前に医師または薬剤師にご相談ください。  
> “If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication, or receiving treatment, consult a doctor or pharmacist before use.”

The final Japanese wording must be determined by the product’s safety assessment and legal review.

### Campaign sign-off

The proposed territory is:

> 毎日に、確かな一歩。  
> “An informed step in every day.”

Because **確かな** may imply certainty or proven efficacy, it should be tested with Japanese consumers and reviewed by regulatory counsel. If testing suggests an efficacy implication, use a more descriptive alternative.

## 6. Creative direction

### Creative concept: “One informed step, every day”

Show ordinary people incorporating the supplement into realistic routines:

- Reviewing the label before use.
- Taking the product according to the directions.
- Preparing a balanced meal.
- Walking or commuting.
- Organizing a busy household or workday.
- Choosing to ask a pharmacist or doctor a question when appropriate.

The creative should show continuity of healthy habits, not dramatic transformation.

### Visual style

Use:

- Natural lighting.
- Calm, uncluttered layouts.
- Realistic urban Japanese settings.
- Diverse ages, genders, body types, family structures, and abilities.
- Clear pack shots and readable instructions.
- Japanese captions and plain-language explanations.

Avoid:

- Before-and-after bodies.
- Symptom-disappearance animations.
- Images of organs becoming “clean,” “strong,” or “protected.”
- Medical coats or clinical settings that imply endorsement.
- People discarding medication.
- Fear-based imagery involving illness, ageing, weight, or family failure.
- Claims communicated only through visual implication.

### Example 20-second video

**Scene 1:** A commuter checks the serving instructions.  
**On-screen text:** “Know what it contains.”

**Scene 2:** A parent reviews ingredient and allergen information.  
**On-screen text:** “Use it as directed.”

**Scene 3:** An older adult takes the product alongside a meal and a walk.  
**On-screen text:** “Make informed choices within a balanced routine.”

**End frame:**  
> 毎日に、確かな一歩。  
> “Learn what it contains. Use it as directed. Make it part of a balanced routine.”

Any benefit statement in the video must be replaced with the exact approved wording.

## 7. Channel and content plan

### Owned channels

#### Japanese website and product page

The website should be the definitive source of product information and include:

- Product category and intended use.
- Exact ingredients and quantities.
- Approved benefit wording.
- Directions, storage, and duration of use.
- Allergens, contraindications, and suitability information.
- Evidence summary and limitations.
- Manufacturing and quality details that can be substantiated.
- Frequently asked questions.
- Price, shipping, subscription, refund, and cancellation terms.
- Customer-service details.
- Safety and adverse-event reporting route.
- Notice that the product is not a substitute for professional medical advice.

#### LINE and email

Use only with appropriate consent for:

- Educational content.
- Order and delivery updates.
- Label-based usage reminders.
- Replenishment notices.
- Customer support.

Do not personalize messages based on inferred diseases, medications, fertility, mental-health status, or other sensitive information.

### Paid channels

#### Search

Use general category, ingredient, and routine-related searches that do not imply diagnosis or treatment. Maintain negative keyword lists for:

- Disease treatment.
- Cure or prevention.
- Medication replacement.
- Urgent symptoms.
- Emergency medical advice.

#### YouTube and online video

Use educational videos on:

- Reading supplement labels.
- Understanding ingredients.
- Appropriate use.
- Evidence limitations.
- How supplements fit alongside healthy habits.

#### Instagram and TikTok

Use short-form content for:

- Routine demonstrations.
- Ingredient explainers.
- Packaging and usage education.
- Myth correction without repeating unsupported claims in a sensational way.

#### X

Use selectively for factual updates, FAQs, and customer support. Monitor replies and reposts closely.

#### Retailer, pharmacy, and drugstore media

Use shelf cards, QR codes, and retailer pages that direct consumers to complete Japanese information. Materials should support informed questions rather than imply pharmacist or government endorsement.

### Content cadence during the pilot

- Two educational short videos per week.
- One ingredient or usage explainer per week.
- One FAQ or safety-information post per week.
- One routine/lifestyle post per week.
- Monthly long-form evidence or label-literacy content.
- Weekly monitoring of comments, reviews, and search queries.

## 8. Influencer and partner guidance

### Suitable partners

- Registered dietitians or appropriately qualified nutrition professionals.
- Pharmacists explaining product information, not making unsupported endorsements.
- Label-literacy educators.
- Lifestyle creators known for calm, factual communication.
- Trusted retailer or pharmacy partners.

### Selection criteria

Partners must:

- Reach the intended audience.
- Communicate health information responsibly.
- Avoid extreme body transformation or illness content.
- Accept mandatory disclosure and content approval.
- Avoid claims outside the approved brief.
- Permit monitoring, correction, and removal.

### Disclosure requirements

Every paid, gifted, affiliate, or otherwise controlled post should clearly disclose the commercial relationship in Japanese, using prominent wording such as:

> 広告  
> PR

The disclosure must be visible or audible in every applicable format, including:

- Feed posts.
- Stories.
- Short videos.
- Livestreams.
- Affiliate pages.
- Reposts.
- Captions and spoken content where appropriate.

Do not hide the disclosure after “more,” among hashtags, or in unreadable text.

### Contract controls

Contracts should require:

- Pre-approval of scripts and visuals.
- No disease, cure, treatment, replacement, or guaranteed-result language.
- No claim that the product works for everyone.
- No fabricated or exaggerated personal experience.
- No medical advice.
- Correction and takedown within defined timeframes.
- Comment and reply monitoring.
- Record retention of final assets and approvals.

A testimonial is not evidence of typical results and must not imply that the audience should expect the same outcome.

## 9. Launch timeline

### Phase 0: Regulatory and strategic preparation — weeks 1–8

- Confirm Japanese regulatory classification.
- Complete ingredient legality, safety, interaction, and dosage review.
- Build the product regulatory dossier.
- Confirm importer, manufacturer, distributor, and responsible-business details.
- Map all claims and implications.
- Conduct consumer, cultural, accessibility, and language research.
- Audit competitors, retailers, pricing, and platform requirements.
- Establish customer-service, complaint, and adverse-event procedures.
- Prepare Japanese master product-information sheet.

### Phase 1: Controlled pilot — weeks 9–12

Launch with limited spend and selected metropolitan audiences.

Test:

- Routine positioning versus ingredient-transparency positioning.
- Video versus static formats.
- Search, social, LINE, and retailer media.
- Landing-page structure.
- One-time purchase versus subscription presentation.
- Warning visibility and comprehension.

Do not scale based on conversion alone.

### Phase 2: Optimization — weeks 13–18

- Remove misunderstood language or visuals.
- Improve FAQs and safety information.
- Reallocate spend toward channels delivering both responsible conversion and high comprehension.
- Audit creators, affiliates, retailers, and comments.
- Conduct brand-lift, trust, and comprehension measurement.

### Phase 3: Controlled expansion — months 5–6

Expand only if:

- No material prohibited-claim incidents have occurred.
- Consumers correctly understand the product’s role.
- Customer-service and safety capacity are adequate.
- Complaint, refund, cancellation, and adverse-event indicators remain within approved thresholds.
- Supply, packaging, and Japanese labeling are reliable.

## 10. Localization requirements

Localization must go beyond literal translation.

### Language

- Use native Japanese copywriters.
- Conduct regulatory review of Japanese wording.
- Avoid direct translation of terms such as “boost,” “detox,” “anti-ageing,” and “clinically proven.”
- Explain technical terms in plain Japanese.
- Keep warnings and instructions prominent and readable.

### Cultural and regional validation

Test content in Tokyo, Osaka, and at least one additional region for:

- Naturalness and formality.
- Perceived trustworthiness.
- Medical or government-endorsement implications.
- Age and family stereotypes.
- Pressure to conform to a “perfect routine.”
- Comfort with the tagline and imagery.

### Accessibility

- Add Japanese captions to all video.
- Use sufficient contrast and readable typography.
- Provide alt text.
- Do not convey safety information by color alone.
- Make critical information available in accessible web formats, not only PDFs.
- Test with older adults, screen-reader users, and people with different levels of digital confidence.
- Consider additional language support for residents with limited Japanese literacy where commercially and operationally feasible.

---

## 11. Ethical safeguards

### Consumer autonomy

- Present one-time purchase and subscription options equally.
- Obtain clear affirmative consent for recurring orders.
- Show total cost, renewal date, delivery frequency, shipping, and cancellation terms before payment.
- Make cancellation as easy as enrollment.
- Avoid artificial countdowns, false scarcity, or fear-based retargeting.
- Do not imply consumers must continue taking the product to avoid health deterioration.

### Health safety

- Display relevant warnings before purchase and on the product page.
- Advise consumers taking medication or receiving treatment to seek appropriate professional advice where relevant.
- Do not provide individualized medical advice through marketing staff.
- Stop or escalate communications if repeated safety concerns appear.
- Maintain Japanese-language customer support and an adverse-event reporting route.

### Privacy

- Use contextual and broad audience targeting where possible.
- Do not infer or target diseases, medication use, fertility status, mental-health conditions, or other sensitive health characteristics.
- Minimize health-related data collection.
- Separate safety-reporting data from marketing databases.
- Explain data use, retention, vendors, and opt-out choices in Japanese.
- Review cross-border transfers and platform matching before launch.

### Inclusion

- Use diverse, non-stereotyped representation.
- Do not shame consumers about age, weight, appearance, productivity, or family performance.
- Show that choosing not to use the product can also be a reasonable decision.
- Avoid portraying the product as necessary for social acceptance or employability.

## 12. Regulatory and substantiation checklist

This checklist is strategic and operational guidance, not legal advice. Japanese legal and regulatory professionals must confirm the requirements for the specific product and claims.

### Product and classification

- [ ] Complete formula, dosage, specifications, and certificates of analysis.
- [ ] Ingredient legality and safety assessed for Japan.
- [ ] Interactions, contraindications, allergens, and vulnerable populations assessed.
- [ ] Product classification confirmed.
- [ ] Importer, manufacturer, distributor, and responsible-business obligations confirmed.
- [ ] Japanese label requirements identified.

### Claims

- [ ] Claim matrix completed for every consumer-facing statement and implication.
- [ ] Evidence matches the exact product, formulation, dose, population, and outcome.
- [ ] Ingredient evidence is not improperly presented as finished-product proof.
- [ ] Functional, nutrient, quality, comparative, ranking, and superiority claims reviewed.
- [ ] Headlines, visuals, testimonials, metadata, keywords, and comments included in review.
- [ ] “Clinically proven,” “No. 1,” “safe,” “natural,” “detox,” and similar terms excluded unless specifically substantiated and permitted.
- [ ] Disclaimers do not serve as a substitute for compliant claims.

### Consumer protection and commerce

- [ ] Pricing, discounts, scarcity, trial offers, and subscriptions substantiated and transparent.
- [ ] Cancellation and refund journey tested.
- [ ] No preselected recurring purchase.
- [ ] Review and testimonial practices are representative and non-manipulative.
- [ ] Retailer, affiliate, and marketplace copy is controlled.

### Influencers and platforms

- [ ] Commercial relationship disclosed prominently in Japanese.
- [ ] Creator scripts and final assets approved.
- [ ] Platform supplement and health-advertising policies checked.
- [ ] Age, targeting, landing-page, and disclosure restrictions reviewed.
- [ ] Comment and repost monitoring operational.

### Privacy

- [ ] Data inventory and flow map completed.
- [ ] APPI review completed for collection, use, sharing, retention, and transfers.
- [ ] Consent and privacy notices localized.
- [ ] Sensitive health data minimized and segregated.
- [ ] Vendor and platform contracts reviewed.
- [ ] Deletion, access, correction, and opt-out processes operational.

### Safety

- [ ] Japanese customer-service team trained.
- [ ] Adverse-event reporting and escalation procedures tested.
- [ ] Batch and purchase information can be captured where necessary.
- [ ] Serious-event pause and notification criteria approved.
- [ ] Complaint and safety trends reviewed weekly during the pilot.

## 13. Measurement and optimization

Use a balanced dashboard covering performance, comprehension, trust, safety, and compliance.

### Pre-launch baseline

Measure:

- Brand and category awareness.
- Consumer understanding of supplements versus medicines.
- Trust in the company and category.
- Perceived pressure and anxiety.
- Existing purchase and usage habits.
- Understanding of warnings and recurring-purchase terms.

### Test design

Run controlled tests by:

- Audience segment.
- Region.
- Channel.
- Creative format.
- Routine versus ingredient-transparency message.
- Landing-page layout.
- Warning placement.
- One-time purchase versus subscription presentation.

### Optimization rules

- Pause any creative that creates material medical misunderstanding.
- Replace language interpreted more broadly than intended.
- Increase education when safety questions recur.
- Reduce frequency if retargeting feels pressuring.
- Reallocate spend based on responsible conversion, trust, and comprehension—not simply cost per order.
- Re-test all revised Japanese copy.
- Audit the full customer journey, including retailer and creator content.

### Reporting cadence

- Daily monitoring during launch for safety and major compliance issues.
- Weekly review of complaints, comments, adverse events, claims, and conversion quality.
- Monthly review of trust, comprehension, accessibility, privacy, and subgroup outcomes.
- Formal scale/no-scale decision at the end of the pilot.

## 14. Key risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Product is incorrectly classified in Japan | Obtain local classification and import review before public claims or sale |
| A modest phrase implies medical efficacy | Conduct overall-impression and consumer-comprehension testing |
| Ingredient evidence is mistaken for product evidence | Maintain an exact-formula claim matrix and substantiation dossier |
| Influencer makes an unsupported health claim | Contractual controls, pre-approval, monitoring, and rapid takedown |
| Consumers interpret pharmacy or expert imagery as endorsement | Use genuine, limited educational roles and avoid implied approval |
| Subscription creates financial or ethical harm | Equal one-time option, clear renewal terms, easy cancellation, no false urgency |
| Adverse events or interactions | Ingredient-specific safety review, trained support, escalation and pause criteria |
| Sensitive health data is used for targeting | Contextual targeting, data minimization, APPI review, no health-condition inference |
| Older or vulnerable consumers feel pressured | Frequency caps, clear terms, no fear-based ageing messages |
| Retailers or affiliates introduce prohibited claims | Master information sheet, contracts, asset audits, correction obligations |
| Translation changes the claim meaning | Native Japanese regulatory and cultural review |
| Accessibility barriers hide safety information | Readable web content, captions, alt text, mobile and assistive-technology testing |
| Campaign is culturally stereotyped | Segment-specific research and testing across regions and life situations |
| Platform policies reject or restrict ads | Pre-clear category eligibility and maintain current platform checklists |

## 15. Assumptions and items requiring local validation

The campaign depends on confirming:

1. The exact product ingredients, formulation, dosage, and intended benefit.
2. Japanese legal classification and importability.
3. Whether the product qualifies for any recognized Japanese functional-claim framework.
4. The evidence available for the finished product rather than individual ingredients.
5. Mandatory label and advertising statements.
6. Relevant safety warnings, interactions, and population restrictions.
7. Appropriate Japanese claim wording.
8. Whether the 30–59 metropolitan audience is commercially optimal.
9. Category demand, competitor pricing, and retailer expectations.
10. Platform eligibility and targeting restrictions.
11. APPI implications of analytics, CRM, LINE, social, retailer, and cross-border data flows.
12. Subscription, refund, and distance-selling requirements.
13. Cultural interpretation of the tagline, imagery, expert content, and routine framing.
14. Accessibility needs and language support for the intended audience.
15. Operational readiness of Japanese customer service, complaint handling, and adverse-event reporting.

These validations should be completed before national-scale media investment.

---

## Final recommendation

Launch a controlled, evidence-led pilot in Japan under the territory:

> **毎日に、確かな一歩。**  
> **“An informed step in every day.”**

The campaign should win trust through clear information, modest and substantiated claims, realistic routines, transparent purchasing, visible safety guidance, and respectful representation. It should make clear that a supplement is not a substitute for professional medical advice or treatment.

The tagline, claim language, disclaimers, labeling, influencer disclosures, targeting practices, subscriptions, and platform executions all require confirmation by qualified Japanese regulatory, legal, scientific, privacy, and native-language specialists. Those reviews are essential safeguards, not merely administrative approvals.
