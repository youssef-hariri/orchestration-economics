# FOSTERING INNOVATION CULTURE IN AN INTERNATIONAL COMPANY: STRATEGIC FRAMEWORK & ACTION PLAN

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This company's innovation variance across locations is not a national-culture problem to accept—it is a **systemic problem to diagnose and solve**. High variance (e.g., 2–3× difference in idea-submission or pilot rates between sites) signals broken feedback loops, weak psychological safety, gatekeeping by middle management, misaligned incentives, and/or unclear decision rights, not immutable cultural traits. The strategy prioritises:

- **Build psychological safety first**: Visible failure amnesty, blameless post-mortems, and executive vulnerability are the foundation. Without them, risk-taking will not emerge, regardless of process.
- **Decentralise idea-to-pilot pathways**: Replace HQ-centric approval with tiered micro-budgeting, branch councils, and clear gate criteria. Speed and transparency kill the "ideas graveyard."
- **Make innovation a manager accountability, not an HR programme**: Tie local leader bonuses directly to pilot-launch rate, pilot-quality metrics, and team psychological-safety scores. Coaching support (not blame) for managers who lag.
- **Protect experimentation time and budget explicitly**: Without carved-out time and ring-fenced budget, innovation will be crowded out by delivery urgency. Make both inviolable.
- **Measure learning velocity, not just idea volume**: 100 submitted ideas are vanity if only 2 reach pilot and 0 generate insight. Prioritise pilot quality and post-mortem rigour.
- **Adapt structure and language by geography, not culture stereotypes**: High-uncertainty-avoidance sites need tighter gate criteria and more structured methods, not fewer pilots. Non-English branches need translation support and local champions, not different innovation rules.
- **Phased rollout with ruthless sequencing**: Kill low-ROI ideas (hackathons as theatre, premature communities of practice). Sequence Phase 1 (safety + process, 6 months) before Phase 2 (scaling + capability, 6 months).

Success requires visible executive sponsorship, manager accountability, transparent decision-making, and willingness to explicitly protect time and budget. Without these, initiatives become corporate theatre and erode trust.

## 1. DIAGNOSIS BEFORE DESIGN: HOW TO ESTABLISH BASELINE & IDENTIFY ROOT CAUSES

**Do not design intervention without first establishing where variance comes from.** Generic solutions fail because they treat symptoms, not causes.

### 1.1 Rapid Diagnostic Approach (Weeks 1–4)

**Core Questions to Answer:**

| **Question** | **Evidence to Collect** | **Timeline** | **Owner** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Which sites actively suppress ideas vs. simply don't surface them? | (a) Idea-submission logs by geography (last 12 months); (b) Exit interview themes from high-performers; (c) Manager sentiment survey (anonymised): "My team feels safe suggesting new ideas" (1–5 Likert). | Weeks 1–2 | Innovation lead + HR |
| What are *perceived* vs. *actual* barriers? | (a) Focus groups: 5–7 frontline staff + their manager separately in 3 high-variance sites; ask open-ended "What stops you from experimenting?"; (b) Legal/compliance audit of real regulatory constraints by country; (c) Budget authority audit: what approval thresholds do branch managers actually have? | Weeks 2–4 | Innovation lead + Compliance + Finance |
| Do employees see innovation as *strategic priority* or *rhetoric*? | Pulse survey (5 questions): "I am encouraged to experiment," "I know what happens to my ideas," "Innovation is a stated company priority," "I have time for exploration," "Failure is treated as learning." Target sample: 500–1,000 employees across all geographies. Analyse variance by site, level, function. | Weeks 1–3 | HR / Survey vendor |
| Which manager cohorts champion ideas? What do they do differently? | (a) Behaviour deep-dive: interview 5–10 high-performing innovation managers in top-5 idea-generating sites. Probe: 1:1 cadence, time spent on idea coaching, how they frame failure, bonus motivation; (b) Competency gap vs. low-performing managers. | Weeks 2–4 | Innovation lead + talent team |
| Is idea feedback transparent or black-box? | Process audit: select 10 random ideas from platform (if exists) or suggestion log. For each: What was submission date? When was feedback given? What was decision (approval, rejection, hold, rework)? Was submitter told? Was decision logic explained? Measure % with feedback ≤14 days; % with decision clarity. | Weeks 1–3 | Innovation lead |

### 1.2 Baseline Metrics (Establish by Week 4)

Record these before any intervention:

- **Idea velocity**: # ideas submitted per 100 employees, *by site*. Variance across sites (ratio of highest to lowest).
- **Idea-to-pilot conversion**: % of submitted ideas that advance to 4-week structured pilot.
- **Psychological safety index**: % of employees scoring ≥4/5 on "I can speak up without fear of retaliation," by site.
- **Manager innovation engagement**: % of managers reporting ≥5 hours/month spent coaching direct reports on experimentation, by site.
- **Decision transparency**: % of submitted ideas receiving visible feedback (approval, rejection, hold) within 14 days, by site.
- **Variance summary**: Identify sites that are 2–3× above or below mean on any metric. These are diagnostic opportunities, not cultural facts.

### 1.3 Likely Root Causes (Diagnostic Hypotheses)

**Hypothesis A: Leadership & Incentive Problem**
- *Signal*: High-variance sites show low manager engagement on innovation; pulse survey shows low trust in manager; exit interviews cite "not encouraged to try things."
- *Intervention needed*: Manager KPI redesign + coaching (Section 3.2).

**Hypothesis B: Systems & Decision Bottleneck**
- *Signal*: Long feedback loops (>4 weeks to hear back on ideas); ideas get stuck in approval limbo; no transparency on why ideas are rejected.
- *Intervention needed*: Idea platform + tiered budgeting + clear gates (Section 3).

**Hypothesis C: Psychological Safety & History**
- *Signal*: Pulse survey shows low "fear of retaliation" scores; employees cite past failed experiments as negative precedent; low idea-submission in risk-averse sites.
- *Intervention needed*: Failure amnesty mechanisms + executive storytelling + blameless post-mortems (Section 2).

**Hypothesis D: Resource & Time Constraint**
- *Signal*: Smaller, under-resourced branches show lower idea velocity despite manager willingness; pulse survey shows "no time for exploration."
- *Intervention needed*: Protected time guarantee + micro-budgeting (Section 3.6).

**Hypothesis E: Language & Distance Barriers**
- *Signal*: Non-English-primary sites underrepresent in global idea platform; geographically isolated branches report slow feedback loops.
- *Intervention needed*: Translation support + asynchronous processes + local decision authority (Section 5.5, 3.8, 3.6).

**Do NOT assume culture.** Use diagnostic evidence to confirm which hypotheses apply to which sites. A Japanese site may have low ideas due to manager gatekeeping (Hypothesis A), not uncertainty avoidance. A remote site may have low ideas due to time-zone delays (Hypothesis E), not hierarchical culture.

## 2. THE STRATEGY: CORE PILLARS & ACTIONABLE INITIATIVES

Innovation culture rests on six pillars, each with specific initiatives. Each initiative specifies *why it works*, *how to adapt it* for high-power-distance, high-uncertainty-avoidance, or under-resourced contexts, and *what global standards* are non-negotiable versus *what can be locally flavoured*.

### PILLAR A: LEADERSHIP & PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY

**Objective**: Make it visibly safe to take intelligent risks; signal that failure is learning, not shame.

#### A1. Executive Failure Storytelling (Non-Negotiable; Start Immediately)

**What it is**: CEO and 2–3 senior leaders each share a genuine, sanitised story of an intelligent failure: what they tried, why it didn't work, what they learned, what consequences they faced (time lost, budget burned, career stall, difficult conversation), and how they recovered.

**Why it works**:
- Normalises failure as status-neutral; signals that speaking candidly about setbacks is *high-status*, not shameful.
- Counter-acts implicit messaging that leaders don't fail (which implies non-leaders shouldn't risk failure either).
- Works in all cultures, especially high-uncertainty-avoidance and face-conscious cultures (Japan, Germany, France, Middle East, S. Korea) where failure carries social cost.

**Adaptation**:
- **High-UA, face-conscious cultures**: Focus storytelling on *how you recovered*—the narrative arc from failure to insight to success. Emphasise structured reflection, not just luck. Frame as "intelligent risk-taking," not recklessness.
- **High-PD cultures**: Have senior leaders acknowledge the failure *to their own boss* or board (if safe). Reverse-mention the hierarchy: "My boss supported me through this." Removes perception that only safe people survive.
- **All sites**: Make stories *specific* (numbers, names, timelines). Vague stories ("I once failed, now I'm CEO") feel performative. Real stories ("Q3 2022, I greenlit a £500k product pivot without sufficient customer validation; we burned £300k in 8 weeks before killing it; I lost a promotion that year but learned signal-before-commitment") are credible.

**Implementation**:
- Month 1: CEO selects 1 story; records 5-minute video or writes 1-page narrative.
- Monthly: Share 1 story via all-hands, internal newsletter, or innovation platform landing page.
- Quarter 1: 3–4 stories published across senior team. Refresh annually.

**Non-negotiable global standard**: Stories must acknowledge real consequences (time/money/career). Sanitised feel-good versions erode trust.

---

#### A2. Blameless Post-Mortem Ritual (Non-Negotiable; Essential for Trust)

**What it is**: After every pilot >£2k or >4 weeks, conduct a 90-minute retrospective. Facilitator (trained HR, external, or peer) ensures focus on *how decisions were made* and *what we learned*, not *who to blame*. Output: decision log, learning brief shared with similar teams. Participation is confidential (discussed in post-mortem; not fed to performance reviews).

**Why it works**:
- Separates outcome review from performance review; creates psychological safety signal (leadership explicitly protects participants from retaliation).
- Forces learning extraction before pilots are forgotten; embeds insight into next cycle.
- Works across all cultures when facilitation is structured (high-UA cultures value process; high-PD cultures respect the formal ritual).

**Adaptation**:
- **High-UA, high-PD sites (Japan, Germany, France, parts of Asia)**: Use *structured* facilitation with clear agenda, time-boxed discussions, and pre-printed decision checklist. Cultures with high error-cost sensitivity prefer rigour and clarity over free-form group discussion.
- **Individualist, low-PD sites (Nordic, Anglo)**: Can be less scripted; peer-led works; emphasis on "what would you do differently?"
- **Non-English sites**: Provide facilitator notes in local language; allow discussion in local language with post-mortem summary in English.
- **Remote/distributed teams**: Conduct hybrid; one sync call, plus asynchronous input option (written reflection submitted before meeting).

**Implementation**:
- Month 1: Identify & train 10–15 facilitators (HR, innovation team, external consultant). 2-day training + facilitator guide.
- Month 2: Pilot post-mortems with 3–5 live projects; refine process; publish case study of 1 post-mortem (anonymised) showing what good looks like.
- Ongoing: Schedule post-mortem within 2 weeks of pilot completion. Track compliance (100% target).

**Non-negotiable global standard**: (a) Facilitator is *not* the pilot sponsor or direct manager (independence). (b) Post-mortem findings are *confidential*; not fed to individual performance reviews. (c) Decisions and learnings are *documented* and *shared* (at least to similar teams; ideally, to Learning Lab, Section 3.8).

#### A3. Intelligent Failure Scoring Framework

**What it is**: Any pilot failure is classified as either (a) **intelligent failure** (well-designed hypothesis, clear success criteria before launch, good-faith execution, learned something material that informs next decision), or (b) **negligent failure** (poor hypothesis design, corner-cutting, execution shortcuts, avoidable error). Only (a) results in amnesia and peer recognition; (b) triggers accountability conversation with manager.

**Why it works**:
- Divorces failure from shame; clarifies that bad luck ≠ bad judgment.
- Shows that learning is the measure of success, not outcome.
- Essential in high-uncertainty-avoidance and face-conscious cultures (where binary "success/failure" triggers deep stigma).

**Adaptation**:
- **High-UA, face-conscious cultures**: Make scoring *transparent and pre-defined* (not subjective). Frame intelligent failure as "rigorous method executed honestly," which is high-status and intellectually honourable.
- **All sites**: Publish the scoring rubric (1 page; 5 criteria). Train all pilot leads on it *before* they launch (not after failure).

**Implementation**:
- Week 3: Design 1-page rubric. Example:

| **Criterion** | **Intelligent Failure** | **Negligent Failure** |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypothesis** | Clear, testable assumption stated before launch | Vague or changed mid-way |
| **Success Criteria** | Pre-defined learning metrics (e.g., "customer feedback on 20 users") | No clear definition of success |
| **Execution** | Good-faith effort; constraints documented | Shortcuts taken; effort half-hearted |
| **Learning** | Specific insight gained (e.g., "customers value X over Y") | No insight extracted; just "it didn't work" |
| **Decision Rigour** | Decision to stop made at agreed gate | Dragged on or killed abruptly without review |

- Month 2: Train all pilot leads & innovators on scoring (1-hour workshop). Embed in design-thinking bootcamp (Section 5.1).
- Ongoing: Use in post-mortems; score every failure within 2 weeks. Publish monthly score card (# intelligent failures, # negligent, trend).

**Non-negotiable global standard**: Scoring is *applied consistently* across all sites. No double-standard where HQ failures are always "intelligent" and branch failures are always "negligent."

#### A4. "Legally Protected Experimentation Zones" (Especially for Regulated Industries)

**What it is**: Define 2–3 business areas per geography where regulatory/compliance barriers are *explicitly suspended* for time-limited trials (e.g., 12 weeks). Example: "In the UK, pilots in the SME customer segment are approved to test 3 new pricing models without prior FCA approval, as long as no consumer harm risk exceeds threshold X." Written protocol signed by General Counsel, published to all teams.

**Why it works**:
- Removes perceived compliance risk as blocker; demonstrates that senior leadership + legal have pre-cleared the boundary.
- Branch staff feel *safe to experiment without second-guessing*; no "should we really do this?" anxiety.
- Essential in Highly Regulated sites (financial, healthcare, telecom).

**Adaptation**:
- **High-uncertainty-avoidance sites**: Make sandbox rules *crystal-clear in writing*. Vague rules trigger anxiety. Uncertainty-averse cultures need certainty on what's approved and what isn't.
- **Different regulatory regimes**: Sandboxes will vary by country (EU GDPR, UK FCA, Japan FSA rules differ). Don't force one-size-fits-all; publish country-specific sandboxes.

**Implementation**:
- Weeks 1–4: Legal/compliance audit per geography. For each site, identify 2–3 low-risk business areas where trials could run with minimal approval friction. Example: "Lower-risk customer segment," "Non-material revenue impact," "Process variant (not product change)."
- Week 4–6: Legal drafts sandbox charter per geography (1–2 pages, plain English). Map approved experiment types, time limits, approval thresholds, kill criteria.
- Week 6: Publish sandbox charters to all teams. Conduct 1-hour Q&A with local legal/compliance and innovation teams.
- Ongoing: Review sandbox effectiveness quarterly. Expand or tighten based on experience.

**Non-negotiable global standard**: Sandbox rules are *written and legally reviewed*. No "it's probably okay" handwaves. If you can't write it down, it's not approved.

#### A5. Protected Experimentation Time Guarantee (Non-Negotiable; Often Missing)

**What it is**: All teams commit to protecting **5% of employee time (~2.5 hours/week)** for exploration, learning, or small pilots. Time is explicitly carved out from BAU delivery KPIs; no project codes required; manager cannot reclaim it during normal delivery crises. Enforced via quarterly audit.

**Why it works**:
- Removes time-scarcity as excuse for inaction; signals permission to think differently.
- Protects innovation work from being crowded out (happens in every organisation: delivery urgency always wins).
- Essential in Resource-Starved and under-resourced sites.

**Adaptation**:
- **Highly pressured, delivery-focused sites**: Make the commitment at director level, not just team level. Director is held accountable if time gets reclaimed. Back-fill for critical roles if necessary.
- **Small teams**: Allow 5% to be pooled (e.g., 2 people per team per week, rotated) rather than fractionalised across everyone.
- **Regulatory/compliance-heavy sites**: Clarify that 5% includes compliance-reading, process-auditing, customer-conversation-to-understand-regulation—not just "new idea piloting." This aligns with risk management.

**Implementation**:
- Month 1: Policy published: "5% exploration time is protected. Manager accountability: report quarterly on time use."
- Month 1: Add to manager KPIs: "Team allocated 5% exploration time; tracked and reported."
- Months 3, 6, 9, 12: Audit via survey ("How much exploration time did you actually get?"). If >20% variance from 5% allocation, escalate to director.
- Public example: If a team runs strong pilots and shows productivity isn't harmed, feature them in comms ("Team X protected 5% time; launched 3 pilots; delivered on BAU on-time").

**Non-negotiable global standard**: This is inviolable. If a delivery crisis overrides it, the crisis was mis-planned, not innovation's fault.

### PILLAR B: STRUCTURES, PROCESSES & DECISION RIGHTS

**Objective**: Make idea-to-impact pipeline clear, fast, and transparent. Remove HQ bottlenecks and bureaucracy. Distribute decision-making.

#### B1. Global Idea Platform with Transparent Feedback (Non-Negotiable; The Spine)

**What it is**: Central online repository (e.g., Ideawake, Spigit, or bespoke build on Sharepoint) with two submission channels:
- **(a) Anonymous pitch**: Submitter provides 10-line problem statement, proposed test, success metric. Submitter unknown initially.
- **(b) Named idea with team**: Submitter identified; option to call for co-creators or volunteers.

**All ideas receive public feedback within 14 days** (Tier 1: auto-reject if out-of-scope; Tier 2: council reviews; Tier 3: senior approval). Decision is visible: "Approved," "Rejected—reason," or "Hold—pending X."

**Top 10% by quality (not volume) are automatically fast-tracked to 4-week pilots** with £2–10k seed budget.

**Why it works**:
- Centralises idea capture; kills "ideas scattered across email/Slack" problem.
- Makes feedback visible (signals listening); reduces fear that ideas vanish into black hole.
- Creates meritocratic pipeline; reduces gatekeeping; bridges distance for remote/isolated branches.
- Transparency (decision logic explained) builds trust, even when ideas are rejected.

**Adaptation**:
- **Non-English sites**: Support submission in local language; auto-translate to English for routing. Feedback provided in English; summarise in local language if needed.
- **High-uncertainty-avoidance cultures**: Publish decision criteria *before* opening submissions (e.g., "We're looking for ideas that: (1) address customer pain in X area, (2) can be tested in 4 weeks, (3) have <£50k upside if successful."). Clear criteria reduce anxiety about what's acceptable.
- **Geographically distant sites**: Ensure async—no sync meetings required to submit or get feedback.
- **Low digital literacy sites**: Offer paper submission (physical form) as fallback; admin scans and enters.

**Implementation**:
- Weeks 1–4: Platform selection & config. Define 5–10 submission categories (by business area, geography, or problem type). Design feedback workflow.
- Week 6: Launch. Public communication: "All ideas receive feedback within 14 days. Decision logic will be explained."
- Ongoing: 1 FTE triage/admin (monitor SLA, route ideas, track decisions, communicate feedback). Target: <2 business-day response to submitter notifications.

**Success metrics**:
- Ideas submitted per 100 employees, by site (track variance).
- % of ideas receiving feedback ≤14 days (target: 95%+).
- Decision clarity (% of rejections that explain *why*, not just "rejected").

**Non-negotiable global standard**: 
- (a) **14-day feedback SLA is sacred.** If slipping, hire/reassign capacity *immediately*. Ideas graveyard kills trust permanently.
- (b) Decision logic is *always explained*, even for rejections. "Rejected because out-of-scope, not aligned with strategy" is better than silence.
- (c) Submitter is *notified personally* (not buried in platform digest). Personal acknowledgment > platform post.

#### B2. Stage-Gated Micro-Budgeting (Non-Negotiable; Decentralises Approval)

**What it is**: Establish branch-level budget authority for innovation pilots in three tiers:

| **Tier** | **Budget Limit** | **Approval** | **Gate Criteria** | **Review Time** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1** | Up to £2k | Manager + Ambassador | Problem statement; success metric; 2-week timeline | None (approve same day) | "Test new customer onboarding flow with 5 users; success = <5 min to complete." |
| **Tier 2** | £2k–20k | Branch Innovation Council | Hypothesis; customer insight; 4–8 week timeline; learning plan | 1 week | "Pilot new product feature in UK market segment; success = >30% customer interest from 50 interviews." |
| **Tier 3** | £20k–100k | Regional Director + CFO | Business case; market validation; scaled pilot plan; expected ROI | 2 weeks | "Scale successful Tier 2 pilot to 3 regions; expected £500k uplift." |

**Money releases in tranches**: 50% at gate approval, 50% at mid-point review (learn-or-kill decision).

**Why it works**:
- **Decentralises approval**: Removes HQ bottleneck; branches decide on their own pilots (within authority).
- **Speed**: Tier 1 approvals same-day; Tier 2 in 1 week (vs. traditional 4–6 week approval cycles).
- **Clarity**: Gate criteria are explicit; managers know what's expected.
- **Prevents scope creep**: Funding tied to gate criteria; can't stretch budget without new approval.

**Adaptation**:
- **High-power-distance cultures**: Frame Tier 1/2 authority as "delegated responsibility from leadership," not abdication. Publish decision guidelines so managers feel empowered, not abandoned.
- **Under-resourced sites**: Lower Tier 1 threshold (e.g., £500 instead of £2k) to enable micro-pilots. Ring-fence £5–10k annual Tier 1 budget per small site so they're not starved.
- **Highly regulated sites**: Tier criteria should include compliance sign-off for financial/health pilots. Gate approval includes legal/compliance sign-off, not just business sign-off.

**Implementation**:
- Weeks 1–3: Finance + Innovation design tier framework and gate criteria. Align with CFO on budget lines (e.g., "£200k annual innovation pilot spend, distributed to branches by headcount").
- Week 4: Manager training: 1-hour workshop on gate criteria, approval authority, common mistakes. Repeat for new managers quarterly.
- Month 2: Publish gate criteria via email, intranet, and idea platform. Make it visible.
- Ongoing: Track approval times (dashboard). If Tier 2 approvals are >1 week, escalate delay reason and unblock.

**Success metrics**:
- Approval time: Tier 1 (target: <1 day), Tier 2 (target: <1 week), Tier 3 (target: <2 weeks).
- % of pilot ideas approved within tier authority (target: 80%+, indicating good filtering).
- Budget utilisation by tier and site.

**Non-negotiable global standard**: 
- (a) Gate criteria are *transparent and pre-published*. No surprises at approval time.
- (b) Tier authority is *enforced*. If a manager tries to escalate a Tier 1 decision to Tier 3, push back and remind them of delegation.
- (c) Kill decisions are *distributed*: branch can kill Tier 1 pilots; council can kill Tier 2; director can kill Tier 3. Killing doesn't escalate to HQ (avoids HQ gatekeeping).

#### B3. Branch-Level Innovation Councils (Distributed Decision-Making)

**What it is**: Each site establishes a 5–7 person council meeting monthly: mix of levels, functions, and tenure. Role: review incoming ideas, prioritise which get Tier 2 funding, unblock barriers, share learnings. Council has small discretionary budget (£5–20k/year depending on site size) to fund micro-pilots or remove obstacles.

**Composition**: E.g., Product Manager, Ops Manager, frontline staff (1–2), Ambassador, Finance admin, 1 manager from outside Ops (e.g., HR or Sales).

**Why it works**:
- **Distributes decision-making**: Branches decide locally; don't wait for HQ.
- **Gives staff agency**: Non-managers are in the room; ideas aren't gatekept by hierarchy.
- **Builds peer accountability**: Council members are invested in success of ideas they approved.
- **Surfaces barriers**: Council becomes a problem-solving body, not just approval body.
- **Accelerates feedback loops**: No time-zone delays waiting for HQ.

**Adaptation**:
- **High-power-distance cultures (Japan, India, Middle East)**: Ensure council chair (usually manager) explicitly solicits input from junior members ("Sarah, you heard 3 customer complaints this week about X; what would you test?"). Don't let hierarchy silence junior voices.
- **Small sites**: Council can be 3 people (Manager, Ambassador, 1 frontline staff).
- **Rotating facilitation**: Each month, rotate who chairs the meeting. Normalises power-sharing.
- **Remote participation**: If team is distributed across time zones, hybrid meeting (some in-person, some virtual) or async option (written proposals, e-vote).

**Implementation**:
- Month 1: Identify council members per site. Brief them on role & cadence.
- Month 2: First council meeting. Set norms: "No idea is dumb; all voices heard; we decide within 1 hour of discussion."
- Monthly thereafter: 90-min council meeting, same time each month (block calendar).

**Logistics**:
- Agenda template: (1) Review new ideas submitted (15 min), (2) prioritise Tier 2 pilots (30 min), (3) review active pilots—any blockers? (30 min), (4) share learnings with neighbouring sites (10 min), (5) next month preview (5 min).
- Output: Decision log emailed to all staff. "3 ideas approved for Tier 2 funding; 2 rejected (reasons); 1 hold pending customer research."

**Success metrics**:
- Council meeting attendance (target: 100%; if <80%, investigate why members aren't showing).
- Decision turnaround: Tier 2 ideas approved within 1 week of council review.
- Variety of pilots: Are ideas from all levels/functions getting funded? Or only senior/technical ideas?

**Non-negotiable global standard**: 
- (a) Council *has real authority* to approve Tier 2 pilots up to £20k. If HQ over-rules decisions regularly, council becomes pointless and demotivates.
- (b) Decisions are *documented and communicated to all staff*. Transparency builds trust and shows ideas matter.

#### B4. "Idea Ambassadors" Network (Local Champions)

**What it is**: Designate 1 energetic, credible mid-level employee per site (~1 per 300–500 employees) as "innovation ambassador." Role: 20% time (one day per week). Responsibilities:
- Evangelises innovation locally (coffee chats, brown-bags).
- Coaches idea submitters (helps frame ideas clearly before platform submission).
- Interfaces with central innovation team (attends monthly ambassador sync call).
- Represents branch in councils and cross-site forums.
- Returns learnings to site (shares case studies, lessons from other sites).

**Why it works**:
- Creates local proxy for central innovation function; removes "HQ edict" perception.
- Builds peer champions; embeds innovation in branch culture rather than treating it as external programme.
- Ambassadors are credible peers—junior staff more likely to approach ambassador than HQ innovation team.
- Essential in high-power-distance and geographically isolated sites (local voice reduces fear).

**Adaptation**:
- **High-power-distance sites**: Ambassador can be senior (not necessarily junior), but must be genuinely approachable (e.g., known for being open-minded, not political).
- **Smaller sites**: One ambassador per 200 employees (higher ratio) to ensure capacity.
- **Remote sites**: Ambassador gets £1–2k annual budget for occasional travel to HQ or neighbouring site (for peer learning and morale).

**Implementation**:
- Month 1: Identify & brief ambassadors. 2-day onboarding (in-person or virtual): role clarity, idea-coaching techniques, how to escalate blockers, platform orientation. Empower them: "You're the innovation leader of your site."
- Monthly: 1-hour virtual sync call (all ambassadors). Share top ideas submitted, blockers by site, peer learning, celebrate wins.
- Quarterly: Rotate 20–30% of ambassadors (refresh energy; prevent burnout). Outgoing ambassadors become "alumni mentors" to incoming cohort.
- Budget: ~£50k/year all-in for 20–30 ambassadors (salaries absorbed by branches; central cost: 0.5 FTE coordinator + travel for peer learning).

**Success metrics**:
- # of ideas coached/submitted per ambassador (target: 10–15/quarter).
- Ambassador attendance at monthly sync (target: 95%+).
- Ambassador burnout (pulse survey: "I have capacity for role"). If <60% agree, scale back roles or hire support.

**Non-negotiable global standard**: 
- (a) Ambassador has *real budget authority* or link to decision-maker (e.g., direct line to regional director for unblocking). If ambassador can't move blockers, role is toothless.
- (b) Ambassador role is *valued*: visible in internal comms, included in performance review (as positive), has direct line to senior sponsor.

#### B5. Rapid Learning Lab (Weekly Asynchronous Sharing)

**What it is**: Dedicated Slack channel (or async forum if no Slack) where pilot leads post **1-slide weekly learnings**:
- **What we tested**: 1-line hypothesis.
- **What we found**: Key data point or customer insight.
- **What we're trying next**: Next decision.

Posts are lightweight, real-time (not polished reports); no formality. Peer comments and questions in-thread. Monthly, curate top 3–5 learnings into digestible "Learning Digest" (1-page synthesis) shared enterprise-wide.

**Why it works**:
- Normalises learning-in-progress; reduces perfectionism (encourages real-time sharing vs. polished reports).
- Enables peer learning across time zones; captures tacit knowledge that would otherwise be lost.
- Feeds innovation pipeline: peers see what's being tested and can propose adjacent ideas.
- Asynchronous: works for distributed, remote teams with no sync-meeting burden.

**Adaptation**:
- **Non-English sites**: Allow posts in local language with English summary. English-speakers can ask clarifying questions; translations provided as needed.
- **Low digital-adoption sites**: Offer weekly "learning digest" email or printed summary (for staff without always-on Slack access).
- **Remote/high-context sites**: Provide post template to structure thinking; reduces ambiguity.

**Implementation**:
- Month 2: Create Slack channel. Invite all pilot leads + interested staff. Post house rules: "Keep posts to 2 min read. Questions/comments welcome. No politics or complaints; learning only."
- Weekly: Pilot leads post progress (even if "we hit a blocker
