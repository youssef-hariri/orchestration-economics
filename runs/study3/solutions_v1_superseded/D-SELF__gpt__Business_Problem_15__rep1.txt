# Global Future-of-Work Framework

## Overarching Objective

Design a work environment that improves business performance, employee experience, inclusion, resilience, and long-term capability—regardless of country, role, or work location.

The guiding philosophy is:

> **Design work around outcomes and activities, not assumptions about presence; set global standards for fairness, safety, security, and inclusion; and adapt implementation to legitimate local legal, cultural, operational, and employee needs.**

The company should not choose between “remote” and “office” as a single enterprise-wide answer. It should establish a portfolio of work models, selected through evidence and applied consistently within clearly defined boundaries.

---

# 1. Core Design Principles

## 1.1 Manage outcomes, not visibility

Performance should be assessed through quality, delivery, customer impact, collaboration, learning, and sustainable performance—not office attendance, online activity, or perceived visibility.

Managers should set clear objectives, expected outputs, decision rights, and service standards. Activity monitoring such as keystroke tracking or screen surveillance should not substitute for effective management and should be avoided except where narrowly justified, lawful, and proportionate.

## 1.2 Provide choice within clear boundaries

Employees should have meaningful flexibility where work permits, subject to:

- Customer and service requirements
- Team dependencies and time-zone coverage
- Security and data restrictions
- Health and safety
- Legal, tax, immigration, and employment requirements
- Need for specialized equipment or facilities

Flexibility should include more than location. Options may include adjusted hours, shift exchanges, compressed schedules, part-time work, job sharing, phased returns, predictable scheduling, and temporary arrangements.

## 1.3 Pursue equity, not identical treatment

A laboratory technician, field engineer, call-center employee, and software developer will not have the same work model. Fairness means providing comparable access to respect, career opportunity, information, tools, development, and appropriate flexibility—not imposing identical rules.

Differences in flexibility must be based on transparent, work-related criteria rather than manager preference, hierarchy, geography, or cultural stereotypes.

## 1.4 Make collaboration deliberate

Co-location should have a clear purpose. Offices and in-person gatherings should support activities such as:

- Onboarding and learning
- Team planning and problem-solving
- Innovation and relationship-building
- Customer engagement
- Mentoring and leadership development
- Work requiring specialized facilities

The company should not assume that more attendance automatically produces stronger culture or collaboration.

## 1.5 Build inclusion and accessibility into the default experience

Hybrid work should not create “first-class” office participants and “second-class” remote participants. Meetings, systems, offices, and career processes should be accessible by design, including captions, transcripts, accessible documents, screen-reader compatibility, suitable room layouts, quiet spaces, assistive technology, and equitable participation norms.

## 1.6 Protect security and privacy proportionately

Security controls should reflect the sensitivity of the work and data. The objective is secure enablement, not unnecessary friction or surveillance.

Core controls should include identity management, multi-factor authentication, managed devices, secure access, data classification, incident response, employee education, and clear rules governing approved locations and networks.

## 1.7 Treat wellbeing as a business requirement

A sustainable model must address workload, availability expectations, ergonomic safety, isolation, rest, caregiving, mental health, and the right to disconnect where applicable. Flexibility should not simply transfer equipment, connectivity, commuting, or unpaid overtime costs to employees.

---

# 2. A Hierarchy of Work-Model Decisions

Decisions should be made at the appropriate level rather than through one universal policy.

1. **Enterprise level:** Establish global principles, minimum standards, and governance.
2. **Country or regional level:** Apply legal, tax, labor, cultural, infrastructure, and consultation requirements.
3. **Role or activity level:** Determine what work requires physical presence, secure facilities, real-time interaction, or individual focus.
4. **Team level:** Set collaboration windows, meeting norms, office purposes, and response expectations.
5. **Individual level:** Address accommodations, caregiving needs, health requirements, and approved exceptions.

This hierarchy prevents both excessive centralization and fragmented local practices.

---

# 3. Work-Model Flexibility by Role and Activity

## 3.1 Classify work by its characteristics

Job titles alone are insufficient. Each role or activity should be assessed against:

- Need for equipment, laboratories, facilities, or physical materials
- Customer, public, or site interaction
- Security and confidentiality requirements
- Regulatory or licensing restrictions
- Interdependence with other roles
- Required time-zone coverage
- Need for rapid escalation
- Creativity, learning, and relationship-building requirements
- Ability to measure outputs and quality
- Health and safety conditions
- Availability of suitable home or local infrastructure

## 3.2 Use a portfolio of work models

Possible categories include:

| Work model | Typical application |
|---|---|
| **Site-based** | Manufacturing, laboratories, logistics, clinical, or equipment-dependent work |
| **Primarily on-site with schedule flexibility** | Customer operations, service centers, or roles requiring regular presence but allowing shift or hour flexibility |
| **Team-hybrid** | Roles requiring periodic collaboration, learning, or customer interaction |
| **Primarily remote** | Individually focused digital work with measurable outputs |
| **Globally distributed** | Teams operating across countries and time zones, using asynchronous methods |
| **Controlled-location** | Sensitive or regulated work requiring approved facilities or devices |
| **Mobile or field-based** | Sales, service, inspection, or customer-site roles |

A role may have different requirements for different activities. For example, a researcher may perform data analysis remotely but require periodic laboratory access.

## 3.3 Offer flexibility to non-remote roles

Where location flexibility is limited, the company should consider:

- Predictable schedules
- Shift swaps
- Compressed workweeks
- Part-time or phased arrangements
- Additional leave options
- Local work hubs
- Transport support
- Advance notice of schedule changes

This avoids making “future of work” synonymous with remote work for only a portion of the workforce.

---

# 4. Team Operating Agreements

Every hybrid or distributed team should document a practical operating agreement covering:

- Which activities are performed in person, synchronously, or asynchronously
- Core collaboration windows
- Expected response times
- Meeting-free periods
- Office attendance purposes, if any
- Decision-making and escalation channels
- Documentation and knowledge-sharing standards
- Time-zone rotation principles
- Onboarding, mentoring, and development practices
- Boundaries around working hours and availability

For example, a global team might use written decision logs, recorded briefings, and two rotating live collaboration windows so the same region is not always required to work inconvenient hours.

Managers should explain the business rationale for team arrangements and apply them consistently.

---

# 5. Offices and Workplace Experience

## 5.1 Reposition offices around activities

Offices should be designed as purposeful destinations rather than collections of assigned desks. Relevant space types include:

- Team collaboration areas
- Quiet and concentration rooms
- Training and onboarding facilities
- Client and customer spaces
- Innovation and workshop areas
- Social and community spaces
- Accessible workstations
- Private rooms for health, caregiving, prayer, or reflection where locally appropriate

## 5.2 Use evidence before changing the footprint

Real-estate decisions should consider:

- Utilization by space type and day
- Employee and customer needs
- Commuting conditions and safety
- Local housing and connectivity
- Future workforce scenarios
- Cost of travel and alternative workspaces
- Resilience and business continuity

Current attendance snapshots should not alone determine long-term property commitments. Pilot redesigned spaces and measure their effectiveness before making irreversible investments.

## 5.3 Make in-person time valuable

Team gatherings should have defined objectives, preparation, and outcomes. Requiring employees to travel merely to attend virtual meetings undermines trust and increases cost and environmental impact.

---

# 6. Collaboration Technology and Digital Infrastructure

The company should provide a secure, interoperable, accessible, and reliable technology foundation globally, while allowing limited local adaptation where infrastructure or regulation requires it.

Minimum capabilities should include:

- Secure identity and access management
- Company-managed devices appropriate to role risk
- Reliable video and audio conferencing
- Shared digital workspaces
- Searchable documentation and decision records
- Real-time captions and transcripts
- Low-bandwidth and mobile-access options
- Translation or multilingual support where needed
- Backup communication channels
- Service support across relevant time zones

Hybrid meetings should be designed to give all participants equivalent access to materials, discussion, decisions, and follow-up. “Providing a video link” is not sufficient.

Technology choices should also account for data residency, privacy, export controls, local availability, accessibility, and cybersecurity requirements.

---

# 7. Inclusion, Wellbeing, and Career Equity

The company should monitor whether work-model differences affect:

- Promotion and pay
- Access to senior leaders
- High-visibility assignments
- Mentoring and sponsorship
- Onboarding and informal learning
- Retention and regrettable attrition
- Belonging and participation
- Workload and burnout

Safeguards should include:

- Structured onboarding for remote and on-site employees
- Transparent promotion and performance criteria
- Deliberate mentoring and sponsorship
- Regular leadership access across locations
- Equitable allocation of development opportunities
- Ergonomic and equipment support
- Confidential accessibility and accommodation processes
- Support for caregiving and medical needs
- Manager training in distributed leadership and bias

Employees who lack a suitable home workspace, reliable connectivity, privacy, or safety should have practical alternatives, such as supported offices, local hubs, or appropriate allowances.

---

# 8. Global Consistency with Local Adaptation

## 8.1 Global non-negotiables

The enterprise should establish common standards for:

- Non-discrimination and fair access
- Outcome-based performance management
- Accessibility and inclusion
- Health and safety
- Security and privacy
- Minimum technology standards
- Employee participation
- Cross-border work controls
- Manager accountability
- Measurement and review

## 8.2 Local decision rights

Countries and regions should determine, within those guardrails:

- Working-time arrangements
- Office attendance practices
- Expense reimbursement and equipment support
- Benefits and leave practices
- Employee consultation processes
- Local workspace design
- Public-holiday and cultural considerations
- Approved local work hubs
- Country-specific communication norms

Each country should maintain a playbook identifying employment, tax, immigration, data-protection, safety, infrastructure, and consultation requirements.

Cross-border remote work should be governed separately, with approved countries, duration limits, advance review, and clear employee and manager responsibilities. Working temporarily from another country may create immigration, payroll, tax, social-insurance, data, or corporate-presence risks.

## 8.3 Explicit trade-off

Identical rules can appear simple but create unequal outcomes or legal violations. Excessive local autonomy can create fragmentation and favoritism. The appropriate balance is **global principles, transparent local variation, and central oversight of material exceptions**.

---

# 9. Governance and Decision Rights

A global future-of-work council should include HR, legal, tax, security, technology, finance, real estate, operations, and regional representatives.

### Global leaders and council

- Set principles and minimum standards
- Approve the measurement framework
- Resolve cross-border or enterprise-wide issues
- Review major exceptions and risks
- Sponsor pilots and annual reviews

### Country and regional leaders

- Confirm legal and regulatory compliance
- Engage works councils, unions, and employee representatives where required
- Adapt implementation to local conditions
- Manage local offices, benefits, and support

### Functions and managers

- Classify work using approved criteria
- Establish team operating agreements
- Manage through outcomes
- Apply flexibility decisions consistently
- Support inclusion, development, and wellbeing
- Document business reasons for exceptions or denials

### Employees

- Participate in co-design
- Follow agreed security and collaboration practices
- Communicate constraints and accommodation needs
- Provide feedback on effectiveness and unintended consequences

An appeal and accommodation process should be available, with documented decisions, service-level expectations, and periodic review for patterns of inconsistency.

# 10. Implementation Roadmap

## Phase 1: Diagnose

Combine evidence from:

- Role and activity analysis
- Operational and customer data
- Employee listening
- Office utilization
- Technology performance
- Legal, tax, immigration, and safety reviews
- Infrastructure and commuting conditions
- Equity and accessibility assessments

Employee preference is important, but it should be considered alongside business outcomes and operational evidence.

## Phase 2: Co-design

Develop:

- Global principles
- Work-model categories
- Country playbooks
- Team agreement templates
- Technology and security requirements
- Office scenarios
- Manager training
- Employee-support provisions
- Metrics and review triggers

Co-design should include employees, local leaders, occupational-health specialists, works councils, unions, accessibility experts, and other local stakeholders as appropriate.

## Phase 3: Pilot

Test different models across representative countries, functions, career stages, work types, and employee groups. Each pilot should define:

- The business problem being addressed
- The intended outcomes
- Costs and risks
- Measures of success
- Review dates
- Conditions for scaling, changing, or stopping

## Phase 4: Scale

Roll out with:

- Plain-language global and local guidance
- Local consultation and legal sign-off
- Manager enablement
- Technology and workspace readiness
- Equipment and support arrangements
- Clear escalation routes
- Consistent communications

## Phase 5: Review and optimize

Review policies at least annually and after significant changes in law, technology, business strategy, employee outcomes, or operating conditions. Policies should be evidence-based, periodically reviewed, and revised when results or circumstances warrant.

---

# 11. Measurement Framework

A balanced scorecard should measure outcomes rather than attendance alone.

## Business and customer outcomes

- Quality and timeliness of delivery
- Customer satisfaction
- Service-level achievement
- Error and rework rates
- Project delivery and decision speed
- Innovation and improvement activity

## Employee experience and wellbeing

- Engagement and belonging
- Ability to work effectively
- Workload and burnout indicators
- Manager effectiveness
- Retention and absence
- Perceived autonomy and fairness

## Inclusion and equity

Where lawful and privacy-preserving, analyze results by country, role, level, work model, gender, disability, career stage, and other relevant characteristics:

- Access to flexibility
- Promotion and pay outcomes
- Development and high-visibility work
- Attrition
- Meeting participation
- Accessibility experience

## Collaboration and workplace

- Team effectiveness
- Meeting volume and usefulness
- Documentation and knowledge-sharing quality
- Time-zone burden
- Office utilization by activity
- Workspace satisfaction
- Travel and commuting impacts

## Risk, compliance, and sustainability

- Security incidents
- Device and policy compliance
- Privacy or audit findings
- Safety incidents
- Cross-border work exceptions
- Business-continuity performance
- Real-estate and technology cost
- Travel and office energy impacts

Metrics must be interpreted together. Lower office occupancy is not a success if service quality, development, inclusion, or collaboration deteriorates. Similarly, high employee satisfaction is not sufficient if customer outcomes or compliance weaken.

---

# Conclusion

A successful global future-of-work model should be:

- **Outcome-led**, rather than presence-led
- **Flexible**, but bounded by business, legal, security, and team needs
- **Role- and activity-based**, rather than universally uniform
- **Globally coherent**, with legitimate local adaptation
- **Inclusive and accessible** by design
- **Purposeful about offices and collaboration**
- **Protective of privacy, wellbeing, and career equity**
- **Governed transparently**, with clear decision rights
- **Evidence-based and reversible**, with employee and local-stakeholder participation

The company should treat the model as an evolving operating system—not a one-time policy. Its continued success depends on testing assumptions, listening to employees, measuring outcomes across countries and employee groups, and adjusting decisions as evidence and circumstances change.
