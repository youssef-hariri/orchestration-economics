# Global Inclusion, Localization, Accessibility, and Responsible Product Guidance

## Purpose and operating model

Design the product to be usable, safe, respectful, and adaptable across languages, cultures, identities, abilities, ages, economic circumstances, and connectivity conditions.

Do not create one supposedly universal experience. Separate requirements into:

1. **Global baseline:** Minimum expectations for dignity, non-discrimination, privacy, safety, accessibility, user control, and meaningful recourse.
2. **Regional configuration:** Language, formats, imagery, holidays, communication styles, payment methods, and support channels requiring local research and testing.
3. **Legal or policy requirements:** Privacy, accessibility, child-safety, consumer-protection, identity-verification, data-transfer, and moderation obligations requiring qualified local review.

Global principles establish minimum protections; they do not require identical workflows everywhere. Local adaptation must not excuse discrimination, targeted abuse, unsafe surveillance, or denial of basic accessibility.

Involve people with relevant lived experience throughout discovery, design, testing, launch, and monitoring. Compensate participants and advisors fairly, protect their privacy, and do not treat one participant, translator, employee, or local expert as representative of an entire group.

---

## 1. Product strategy, research, and accountability

### Define measurable inclusion requirements

Include inclusion requirements alongside performance, security, and business goals. At minimum:

- Core workflows work with keyboards, screen readers, zoom, increased text spacing, voice control, and alternative input.
- Important information does not rely only on color, sound, gesture, location, literacy, or cultural knowledge.
- Users can change language, region, time zone, notification, privacy, and accessibility settings.
- Names, scripts, addresses, dates, currencies, phone numbers, calendars, and units are not restricted to one regional model.
- Safety, consent, recovery, billing, support, moderation, and appeal content is available in supported languages.
- Low-bandwidth, older-device, shared-device, and interrupted-connection scenarios are addressed where relevant.
- Sensitive data collection is minimized, justified, protected, and governed through retention and deletion rules.
- High-impact automated decisions have appropriate human oversight and meaningful appeal.

Define acceptance criteria for terms such as:

- **Critical content:** Safety, consent, privacy, recovery, billing, support, moderation, appeals, and crisis communications.
- **High-impact decision:** Account suspension, identity or age-verification failure, denial of funds or benefits, safety-report action, or sensitive-data disclosure.
- **Supported platform:** A documented device, operating system, browser, and assistive-technology combination with a defined support period.
- **Supported language:** A language meeting documented minimum coverage and quality requirements for critical workflows—not merely one with translated navigation labels.
- **Meaningful appeal:** An accessible, understandable, language-appropriate process capable of changing the original decision within a stated timeframe.
- **Safe fallback:** A reviewed alternative that does not create greater privacy, safety, accessibility, or legal risk than the unsupported experience.

### Conduct representative and safe research

Recruit according to product-relevant differences rather than demographic coverage alone. Depending on the product, include:

- Multiple regions, languages, dialects, and scripts
- Rural, urban, low-connectivity, and shared-device users
- Children, teenagers, older adults, caregivers, and support workers
- People with permanent, temporary, situational, or fluctuating disabilities
- Screen-reader, keyboard, switch, voice-control, magnification, and alternative-input users
- Deaf, hard-of-hearing, Deafblind, and sign-language users
- People with low literacy or limited digital experience
- Migrants, refugees, people without stable addresses, and people with documents issued elsewhere
- LGBTQ+ users and people with varied family, caregiving, and relationship structures
- Intersex users and people who may not safely disclose sex characteristics or gender
- People who cannot safely disclose identity, location, household, or immigration information
- Users with intersecting identities, such as disabled people using minority languages

Provide accessible materials, interpreters, fair compensation, safe withdrawal procedures, and privacy-preserving recruitment. Research involving children requires appropriate assent, guardian procedures where applicable, safeguarding, and secure data handling.

### Maintain evidence and risk records

For each market or language, record:

1. What was observed.
2. Which communities participated.
3. What remains uncertain.
4. Whether a finding is a preference, accessibility need, safety concern, or legal requirement.
5. Which product decisions changed.
6. Who reviewed sensitive content.
7. What must be retested after launch.
8. What evidence would trigger reconsideration.

Maintain:

- An inclusion risk register
- An assumption and decision log
- A language coverage matrix
- A jurisdiction and legal-requirements matrix
- A vendor and model-risk register
- Named owners, review dates, escalation paths, and pause criteria

Document whether community input is advisory or decision-making, how disagreements are handled, and how contributors are protected, credited, and compensated.

## 2. Product features and user control

Where relevant, allow users to control:

- Interface language and content language separately
- Region, time zone, calendar, and observance preferences
- Date, time, currency, and measurement formats
- Text size, contrast, motion, and visual density
- Notification channels, frequency, quiet hours, and preview visibility
- Display name, pronouns, relationship information, and profile visibility
- Accessibility preferences
- Location, contacts, personalization, and data-sharing permissions
- Sessions, devices, backups, linked accounts, and account recovery

Users should be able to override location-based defaults. IP address, device settings, phone prefixes, and geolocation do not establish someone’s identity, culture, religion, language, or residence.

Do not require disclosure of gender, sex characteristics, religion, ethnicity, nationality, disability, sexual orientation, relationship status, immigration status, or other sensitive characteristics unless clearly necessary, justified, and appropriately protected.

Avoid assuming:

- Heterosexuality, marriage, monogamy, or binary family structures
- A private device, personal phone number, email address, or bank account
- A permanent address or government document
- A credit card or biometric-capable device
- That caregivers should see all information belonging to the person they assist
- That users can safely disclose their identity or preferences

Separate legal names and identity markers from chosen or display information where possible. Prevent identity, relationship, gender, sex-characteristic, or orientation data from being exposed through notifications, search, support tools, exports, recommendations, billing, or account synchronization.

## 3. Connectivity, affordability, and payment access

For slow or expensive networks, older devices, and shared devices:

- Provide lightweight pages and compressed media.
- Avoid autoplay and large downloads by default.
- Support offline or intermittent workflows where feasible.
- Save progress and allow interrupted actions to resume.
- Clearly distinguish completed, pending, and failed actions.
- Support small screens, low memory, and older browsers.
- Consider text, email, SMS, USSD, or agent-assisted alternatives only after assessing cost, accessibility, privacy, availability, and safety.
- Provide offline access to critical instructions where practical.

After a dropped connection, a form should state whether submission succeeded, preserve entered data, and provide a safe retry or escalation option. Repeated retries must not unnecessarily cause lockouts, duplicate payments, fraud flags, or privacy exposure.

Where relevant, support:

- Local cards and currencies
- Bank transfers
- Mobile money
- Prepaid balances
- Cash or agent-assisted payments
- Alternative delivery, pickup, or identification methods

Clearly display:

- Total price
- Taxes and fees
- Currency and conversion basis
- Renewal date
- Cancellation method
- Refund terms
- Minimum transaction requirements
- Material data or service costs

Review local rules governing taxes, recurring payments, refunds, cooling-off periods, cancellation, and consumer protection.

**Measure:**

- Data transferred for core workflows
- Completion and abandonment on low-end devices
- Failure and retry rates under poor connectivity
- Payment failure and refund rates
- Support cost
- Minimum device and browser requirements
- Access without a private phone, bank account, or card

## 4. Interaction, visual design, and accessibility

### Flexible layouts

Design and test for:

- Right-to-left and bidirectional text
- Long and short translations
- Mixed scripts
- Increased text size and spacing
- Different font metrics
- Small screens and landscape orientation
- Vertical or complex writing systems where relevant
- Screen readers, keyboards, voice control, switch access, and magnification
- Local assistive-technology voices and pronunciation

Avoid fixed-width controls, text embedded in images, and layouts that fail when translated strings expand or contract.

Do not require drag-and-drop, swiping, hovering, precise tapping, complex gestures, or time-limited interaction when an accessible alternative is possible.

### Color, symbols, and gestures

- Never communicate important information through color alone.
- Pair color with text, labels, icons, patterns, or status descriptions.
- Check contrast for text, icons, controls, charts, and focus states.
- Test light mode, dark mode, color-vision differences, and high-contrast settings.
- Review gestures, hand signs, arrows, animals, clothing, religious symbols, and body language with people familiar with the relevant context.

**Do:** Display “Payment failed” alongside a red status indicator.  
**Don’t:** Use only a red dot to communicate an error.

Do not assume a color, symbol, gesture, direction, animal, or image has one universal meaning.

### Cognitive, motor, sensory, and speech access

Provide:

- Predictable navigation and consistent controls
- Plain-language instructions
- Adequate touch targets
- Error prevention and recovery
- Review-before-submit steps
- Progress indicators
- User-controlled time limits
- Reduced-motion settings
- Alternatives to precise motor actions
- Text-based alternatives to voice interaction
- Clear handling of interruptions and state changes
- No content exceeding applicable flashing thresholds

Consider cognitive disabilities, neurodivergence, memory impairments, fatigue, photosensitivity, vestibular disorders, speech disabilities, communication-device users, and fluctuating conditions. Accessibility needs may be permanent, temporary, or situational.

## 5. Accessibility and age inclusion

Meet applicable accessibility laws and recognized standards in each market. Use WCAG 2.2 AA as a baseline where appropriate, but do not treat compliance as proof of usability. Validate the experience with disabled people and assistive-technology users.

Support:

- Keyboard-only navigation
- Screen readers on supported platforms
- Magnification and high zoom
- Voice control
- Switch access
- Alternative input devices
- Captions and transcripts
- Audio description where needed
- Accessible PDFs and downloadable files
- Text-based alternatives to maps
- Charts presented as data tables or text summaries
- Relay, text, captioned, or signed support channels where relevant

Ensure:

- Controls have accessible names, roles, and states.
- Focus order is logical and visible.
- Form errors are associated with fields and announced appropriately.
- Dynamic updates are exposed to assistive technology.
- Text remains usable with increased size and spacing.
- Accessibility preferences persist where appropriate.
- Support staff can use accessible communication tools.

### Authentication and forms

- Do not make CAPTCHA the only verification method.
- Do not require facial, voice, or biometric verification unless documented as necessary, proportionate, legally reviewed, and accompanied by accessible alternatives.
- Allow password managers, pasting, keyboard navigation, and screen readers.
- Avoid forced timed checks.
- Accept realistic local names, addresses, phone numbers, and postal codes.
- Explain formatting requirements with examples.
- Do not reject valid input because it contains diacritics, non-Latin characters, one name, multiple family names, or an unfamiliar address structure.

### Children, teenagers, and older adults

Where relevant:

- Apply child-safety, age-assurance, and parental-consent requirements.
- Use privacy-preserving age assurance.
- Prefer self-declaration or lower-data methods where legally permissible and appropriate.
- Provide correction and appeal paths for false age-assurance results.
- Do not default to facial age estimation, biometric checks, or document checks where less intrusive methods are adequate.
- Design age-appropriate messaging, advertising, data collection, reporting, and contact controls.
- Avoid parental controls that unnecessarily expose a young person’s sensitive information.
- Test with children only under appropriate safeguarding and consent procedures.
- Include older adults without assuming low literacy, low technical ability, or uniform preferences.
- Consider age-related changes in vision, hearing, memory, motor ability, and cognition.
- Support caregiver-assisted use without assuming caregivers should access all user information.

## 6. Language, content, and communication

### Respectful content

Use language that is:

- Specific rather than generalized
- Plain and direct
- Natural in the target language
- Appropriate to audience, context, and formality
- Free from stereotypes and value judgments
- Reviewed by proficient speakers and, where necessary, community reviewers

Avoid treating one group as the default and others as deviations. Avoid humor based on accents, names, ethnicity, religion, disability, gender, nationality, age, class, or sexual orientation.

Avoid terms such as “primitive,” “exotic,” “normal user,” “special needs,” “foreign,” “third world,” and “illegal immigrant” in ordinary user-facing copy. Such terms may be discussed, quoted, or required in a legal or analytical context; if so, provide context and use precise wording.

Person-first and identity-first language preferences vary across communities and languages. Use a person’s or community’s preferred terminology where known, and neutral wording where it is not. Consult multiple sources when terminology is contested; do not present one term as universally preferred.

**Avoid:**

> “People from this region are family-oriented.”

**Prefer:**

> “Some participants described family involvement as important in this workflow, while preferences varied by age, location, and household.”

**Avoid:**

> “Your identity is invalid.”

**Prefer:**

> “We could not verify these details. Check the information or choose another verification method.”

Do not silently “correct” dialects, place names, identity terms, or reclaimed language.

### Literacy and clarity

- Use descriptive headings and short paragraphs.
- Explain acronyms and technical terms.
- Use descriptive link text.
- Put critical information before optional detail.
- Test content with people who have different literacy levels.
- Use audio, visual, human-assisted, or signed alternatives where appropriate.
- Do not assume icons are self-explanatory; label ambiguous icons.
- Do not equate low literacy with low intelligence, low digital ability, older age, or a particular region.

### High-stakes content

Use qualified human linguistic review for:

- Safety warnings
- Privacy and consent
- Legal terms
- Medical and financial information
- Identity and verification
- Crisis communications
- Moderation notices and appeals

Machine translation may assist with drafts, gap detection, or low-risk content, but must not be the sole authority for high-stakes content. If reliable translation is unavailable, use reviewed plain-language content, qualified interpretation, or delay the high-risk feature.

## 7. Internationalization and localization

### Engineering foundation

Implement internationalization before translation:

- Use Unicode throughout.
- Store language, locale, currency, calendar, and time zone separately.
- Use locale-aware formatting libraries.
- Support plural rules, grammatical agreement, formality, and case where needed.
- Do not infer or require personal gender because a language has grammatical gender.
- Support bidirectional text safely.
- Avoid hard-coded strings and sentence-fragment concatenation.
- Support text expansion and contraction.
- Make search, sorting, validation, and indexing work across scripts.
- Handle Unicode normalization, punctuation, confusable characters, and mixed-script security risks.
- Support local phone, address, postal-code, and naming conventions.
- Test local text-to-speech and screen-reader pronunciation where relevant.

Do not assume a language maps neatly to one country or that an official language serves every user in that country.

### Language coverage

Maintain a matrix showing whether each language supports:

- Interface
- Help content
- Safety notices
- Privacy and consent
- Account recovery
- Billing and cancellation
- Customer support
- Moderation notices
- Appeals
- Notifications
- Crisis communications
- User-generated-content tools

Language-support claims must state their scope. Do not describe a language as fully supported when only navigation labels are translated.

For each supported language, define:

- Human-review status
- Coverage of critical workflows
- Translation freshness
- Support and appeal availability
- Moderation coverage
- Whether machine translation is used
- Minimum comprehension or task-success results from local testing

Document deferred or unsupported languages, the risks of deferral, safe fallback behavior, the decision owner, and a review date. Commercial demand alone should not determine whether safety-critical language support is provided.

### Language prioritization

Prioritize support using more than market size. Consider:

- Number and vulnerability of affected users
- Safety and legal significance
- Consequences of misunderstanding
- Existing exclusion and lack of alternatives
- Community requests and lived-experience input
- Whether the product is essential or high impact
- Feasibility and long-term maintenance capacity

Record who made the decision, which communities were consulted, and what evidence would trigger reconsideration.

### Sign languages

Sign languages vary by country and community and are not visual versions of spoken languages.

Where video, education, support, or high-stakes communication is important:

- Consult Deaf users and sign-language experts.
- Consider localized sign-language content.
- Do not treat International Sign as a universal substitute.
- Provide captions while recognizing that captions do not replace signed communication for all users.
- Make video controls accessible.
- Review bandwidth, privacy, interpreter qualification, production, and versioning requirements.

## 8. Dates, times, names, identity, and verification

### Dates, times, numbers, and measurements

- Store timestamps unambiguously.
- Display dates according to user locale.
- Use month names or explicit examples where ambiguity matters.
- Support 12-hour and 24-hour preferences.
- Show time zones for cross-region events.
- Account for daylight-saving changes, leap days, and regions without daylight saving.
- Localize decimal and thousands separators.
- Show currency codes where symbols are ambiguous.
- Do not silently convert money or units.
- Show conversion basis and date where relevant.
- Support locally used measurement systems and calendars.

Avoid `03/04/26` where interpretation could differ. Use a localized format such as `4 March 2026`.

Holiday, school, work-week, weekend, and observance assumptions should be configurable. Do not infer religious identity or observance from location, name, or behavior.

### Names and addresses

Support:

- One-name and multi-part names
- Multiple family names
- Long names and diacritics
- Non-Latin scripts
- Name-order variation
- Prefixes, suffixes, titles, and patronymics
- Legal, chosen, and display names
- Regions without postal codes or standardized addresses

Do not require surnames, middle names, Latin characters, fixed first-name/last-name fields, or titles such as Mr. or Ms. unless genuinely necessary.

### Identity and age verification

- Explain which fields must match and why.
- Recognize that documents, transliterations, and everyday names may differ.
- Provide correction and appeal paths for diacritics, transliteration, name changes, aliases, mononyms, and document inconsistencies.
- Support documents issued in another country where legally and operationally possible.
- Provide alternatives for expired, unavailable, damaged, or inaccessible documents where permitted.
- Avoid gender markers, sex-characteristic fields, facial analysis, voice analysis, and biometrics unless strictly necessary.
- For biometric or automated age systems, document necessity, proportionality, legal basis, bias, accessibility, spoofing, coercion, privacy, and error risks.
- Provide human review for high-impact verification failures.

## 9. Representation, religion, gender, and imagery

Represent people as individuals with agency, not as cultural symbols or decorative proof of diversity.

Use imagery showing:

- Ordinary contemporary life as well as cultural events
- Different ages, abilities, body types, skin tones, and family structures
- Varied occupations and social roles
- LGBTQ+ people and diverse relationships where relevant
- People in active, professional, and non-stereotypical roles
- Relevant and contextually accurate environments

Review whether:

- The person is relevant rather than merely decorative.
- The image implies a social role or cultural practice that may not apply.
- Religious, disability, gender, sex-characteristic, or cultural markers are being used as shorthand.
- People are shown as agents rather than recipients of help.
- The image creates privacy, safety, or re-identification risks.
- Licensing, consent, model releases, and intended use are appropriate.

Do not:

- Reduce a country or community to flags, landmarks, food, festivals, traditional clothing, or religious objects.
- Use one person as a symbol for an entire community.
- Show disabled people only as recipients of help.
- Use generative imagery as documentary evidence of a real community.
- Present stock or synthetic diversity without human contextual review.

Where relevant, religious or belief-related features should be:

- Opt-in
- Configurable
- Privacy-preserving
- Based on user preference rather than inferred identity

Consider fasting, prayer, dietary requirements, religious attire, observance schedules, nonreligious identities, and minority beliefs without making religion public by default. Distinguish religion from ethnicity, nationality, and culture.

## 10. Privacy, safety, and consent

### Data minimization

For every sensitive field, document:

- Why it is needed
- Whether it is optional
- Who can access it
- How long it is retained
- How it can be corrected or deleted
- What happens in backups, logs, analytics, linked systems, and model-training pipelines
- Risks if it is breached, compelled, or repurposed

Potentially sensitive information includes identity, sex characteristics, location, health, religion, political affiliation, biometrics, contacts, precise behavior, language settings, relationship information, immigration status, and support activity.

### Shared, monitored, and high-risk environments

Assess whether the product could reveal:

- Location
- Language or identity settings
- Search history
- Payment activity
- Support contact
- Health, political, religious, or relationship information
- Use of a safety feature
- Notification sender or subject
- Deleted content through backups or linked devices

Where appropriate, provide:

- Discreet notification previews
- Session and device management
- Easy sign-out
- Local session clearing
- Backup and linked-device controls
- Protection against unwanted contact
- Recovery methods that do not alert an unsafe person
- Clear deletion behavior

Use neutral labels or quick-exit options only when threat-model testing shows they improve safety. Do not imply that these features guarantee anonymity or protection.

Do not claim a feature is anonymous, incognito, private, or deleted unless the technical guarantees support that claim.

For products affecting migrants, refugees, activists, journalists, undocumented people, LGBTQ+ users, religious minorities, children, or people in conflict settings, complete a documented misuse and abuse assessment before launch.

### Consent and lawful processing

Obtain consent before processing when consent is the applicable legal and ethical basis. Where another basis applies—such as security, fraud prevention, service operation, or legal obligation—clearly explain the purpose, basis, controls, and applicable rights.

Consent should be:

- Specific and informed
- Freely given
- Reversible
- Separate for distinct purposes
- Available in relevant languages
- Free from deceptive defaults, pressure, and repeated prompting

## 11. Safety, moderation, automation, and user-generated content

### Localized rules

Rules should explain:

- Prohibited behavior
- Harassment and targeted abuse
- Hate speech and dehumanization
- Threats and incitement
- Sexual exploitation and non-consensual imagery
- Impersonation and fraud
- Privacy violations and doxxing
- Relevant misinformation categories
- Enforcement steps
- Reporting and appeal processes

Distinguish disagreement, criticism, satire, reclaimed language, and harmful targeting. Local context matters, but local adaptation must not excuse discrimination, targeted abuse, or serious safety failures.

### Moderation operations

Use:

- Moderators with relevant language and contextual competence
- Localized examples and escalation guidance
- Human review for high-impact, contested, legally consequential, or safety-sensitive decisions
- Independent appeal review where feasible
- Careful handling of dialects, slang, code-switching, satire, and reclaimed terms
- Moderator training, workload limits, rotation, and wellbeing support

Do not assume a model trained on one language or culture performs equally well elsewhere.

Measure, with privacy safeguards:

- False-positive and false-negative rates
- Report handling times
- Appeal rates and outcomes
- Translation quality of enforcement notices
- Performance on dialects, code-switching, and minority-language content
- Comparable enforcement outcomes across languages and regions

Low training-data coverage should not result in automatic suppression. Low-confidence cases should be routed to human review, delayed, or labeled transparently.

### Translated user-generated content

- Keep the original text available where appropriate.
- Identify whether translation is automated, human-reviewed, or user-submitted.
- Allow users to report inaccurate or harmful translations.
- Explain uncertainty where translation quality is limited.
- Do not treat uncertain translation as authoritative evidence for enforcement, legal action, or identity decisions.

## 12. Data, AI, community, and vendor governance

For translation, moderation, recommendation, identity, analytics, and generative systems:

- Document data provenance, licensing, consent, purpose, and retention.
- Do not infer sensitive attributes for eligibility or personalization without a clear, justified purpose.
- Evaluate false positives, false negatives, accessibility failures, and performance by language, dialect, script, accessibility mode, and relevant context where ethically appropriate.
- Document known limitations and unsupported languages.
- Provide human oversight for high-impact decisions.
- Maintain correction, appeal, and deletion pathways.
- Review model and vendor changes before deployment.
- Document whether user data is used to train internal or external models.
- Protect Indigenous, community-owned, and sensitive linguistic data from extractive use.
- Establish processes for correcting or removing harmful training data where feasible.

Where applicable:

- Obtain community consent and governance approval.
- Define permitted uses and retention.
- Provide attribution and benefit-sharing.
- Limit commercial reuse and model training.
- Offer community-controlled correction or withdrawal mechanisms where feasible.

Audit third-party providers—including payment, identity, translation, analytics, CAPTCHA, support, and communications vendors—for:

- Accessibility
- Language and dialect coverage
- Data retention and deletion
- Model-training restrictions
- Subprocessor disclosure
- Security and incident notification
- Regional hosting and lawful-access procedures
- Human escalation and appeal support
- Audit rights
- Exit and migration plans

Include these obligations in contracts, with remedies for non-compliance.

---

## 13. Customer support and local operations

Provide, where relevant:

- Accessible ticketing and communication tools
- Translation and interpretation
- Relay, text, captioned, and signed channels
- Procedures for names, pronouns, identity corrections, and accessibility needs
- Escalation for discrimination, safety, privacy, and accessibility failures
- Agent authority to override rigid automated workflows
- Quality standards that do not penalize reasonable accommodations
- Support that does not expose sensitive identity, relationship, immigration, or safety information

“Local” does not automatically mean representative or culturally competent. Reviewers and advisors should have relevant linguistic, contextual, accessibility, legal, or lived experience; multiple viewpoints; compensation; and privacy protection.

## 14. Risk tiers, launch gates, monitoring, and remediation

### Use product-risk tiers

Not every feature requires the same level of research or oversight. Classify features according to factors such as:

- Access to money or essential services
- Sensitive identity, health, or location information
- Safety or emergency consequences
- Account suspension or eligibility decisions
- Children or other vulnerable users
- Automated decision-making
- Cross-border or government-access exposure

Map each tier to required research depth, translation review, accessibility testing, monitoring, human review, and incident-response times.

### Launch evidence package

Before release, require:

- Accessibility test results
- Localized usability findings
- Language coverage matrix
- Privacy and threat-model assessment
- Moderation quality results
- Verification and appeal testing
- Vendor and model-risk review
- Unresolved-risk list
- Named launch owner
- Rollback authority and pause criteria

### Defer or pause launch when:

- Critical safety, recovery, consent, billing, moderation, support, or appeal content is unavailable in an intended language.
- Identity or age verification has no accessible fallback or correction path.
- Moderation quality is materially unsafe for a language or community.
- Privacy or surveillance risks cannot be mitigated.
- Support cannot respond to high-risk incidents.
- The product fails on the devices or connectivity conditions used by intended users.
- A vendor or automated system creates unacceptable exclusion or harm.
- Community data is being used without appropriate consent or governance.

### Monitor post-launch

Using aggregation, minimum sample thresholds, and disclosure controls, monitor:

- Task completion and abandonment by language, region, device, and assistive technology
- Translation complaints and support contacts
- Accessibility defect severity and resolution time
- Verification and age-assurance failures and appeal outcomes
- Moderation error rates and appeals by language
- Privacy incidents and unintended disclosures
- Performance and crash rates on low-end devices
- Successful language, region, and accessibility setting changes
- Data cost, support cost, and payment access
- User trust, satisfaction, and qualitative feedback
- Harm reports, not only engagement or conversion

Do not use monitoring data for eligibility, pricing, or personalization without separate justification. Avoid re-identification of small or intersectional groups. Use qualitative review when quantitative samples are too small to interpret safely.

### Incident response

Define:

- Who receives reports
- Severity levels and response times
- Temporary mitigation
- User-notification requirements
- Data correction or deletion
- Public communication where appropriate
- Independent review
- Compensation or other remedy where applicable
- Criteria for redesigning, restoring, or withdrawing the feature

Set severity-based service levels for acknowledgement, mitigation, notification, correction, review, and remedy. Identify who can pause a feature immediately when harm is ongoing.

Version and review sensitive terminology, safety notices, moderation rules, translation glossaries, regional defaults, identity fields, and automated models. Changes should be tested in context, documented, and reversible where possible.

## Guiding principle

Inclusion is not achieved by adding flags, country settings, diverse imagery, or machine translation. It requires flexible architecture, meaningful user control, accessible interaction, respectful and natural language, local expertise, paid participation from affected communities, privacy-aware defaults, human oversight for high-impact decisions, accountable vendors, continuous testing with diverse users, measurable outcomes, and a willingness to change, pause, or withdraw features when evidence shows that they cause harm.
