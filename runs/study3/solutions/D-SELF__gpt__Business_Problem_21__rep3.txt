# Five-Warehouse Automation and Workforce-Transition Roadmap

## 1. Executive summary

Approve a **36-month planning framework**, beginning with a **90-day diagnostic and limited, reversible pilot**. Do not authorize full-network automation, fixed workforce-reduction targets, or vendor-promised three-year paybacks until site-level data and operating results validate them.

The transformation should pursue four outcomes in order:

1. Protect service to key accounts.
2. Reduce picking and inventory errors.
3. Improve productivity, safety, retention, and capacity.
4. Deliver sustainable, finance-validated cash and economic benefits.

### Recommended sequence

- **All sites, Months 0–9:** data cleanup, directed picking, scanning, slotting, quality controls, labor measurement, and workforce skills assessment.
- **Best-fit pilot, provisionally Site B, Months 6–12:** one bounded, reversible automation flow.
- **Reference-site scale-up, Months 12–18:** only after stable performance, peak testing, outage recovery, and financial validation.
- **Sites C and D, Months 15–30:** differentiated, modular automation based on site economics and operating fit.
- **Site A, Months 21–36:** controlled, zone-by-zone conversion after another site has demonstrated reliable performance through representative peak conditions.
- **Site E, Months 24–36:** digital and flexible improvements; defer major fixed automation unless the business case changes.

The program must treat labor productivity, redeployment, avoided hiring, reduced overtime, and actual payroll savings as separate outcomes. The previously suggested 15–20% workforce impact is **not a forecast or commitment**. Any reductions will be determined only after future-state staffing is validated.

---

## 2. Guiding principles

1. **Service and safety precede automation speed.**
2. **Automate stable, repetitive, high-volume work; retain manual flexibility for exceptions and variable flows.**
3. **Improve data, process discipline, and quality controls before committing to expensive physical systems.**
4. **Treat vendor payback claims as hypotheses.**
5. **Count only sustainable, finance-validated savings.**
6. **Use vacancies, attrition, redeployment, and voluntary programs before involuntary reductions.**
7. **Do not reduce staffing before the future-state labor model and service performance are proven.**
8. **Involve long-tenured employees in design, testing, training, and quality improvement.**
9. **Use common digital standards with modular physical automation.**
10. **Convert live sites by zone with tested fallback and rollback capability.**
11. **Use objective, lawful workforce criteria; never use age, tenure, retirement assumptions, or protected characteristics improperly.**
12. **Stop or redesign a deployment when service, safety, reliability, or economics fall below approved thresholds.**

## 3. Facts, assumptions, and validation requirements

### Confirmed facts

- Five regional warehouses.
- Rising labor costs.
- Approximately 35% annual picker turnover.
- Picking errors damaging key accounts.
- Vendors claiming approximately three-year payback.
- Hundreds of long-tenured employees.
- Largest warehouse is a major employer in its town.
- Competitor experienced a public automation failure.
- Capital is available but constrained.

### Planning assumptions—not commitments

- Sites differ materially in volume, SKU profile, building suitability, systems readiness, labor economics, and service risk.
- Some work is sufficiently repetitive for selective automation.
- Digital controls can reduce errors before major equipment investment.
- Benefits may arise from lower overtime and agency use, avoided hiring, reduced errors, capacity, and actual payroll reduction.
- Site B is a suitable pilot candidate, subject to validation.
- Site A requires enhanced community, labor, and service safeguards.

### Required baseline before equipment commitment

By site, shift, process, and order profile, validate:

- Orders, lines, units, cube, weight, SKU velocity, seasonality, and peak-hour volume.
- Labor hours, overtime, agency labor, vacancies, absenteeism, turnover, and fully loaded cost.
- Mix-adjusted productivity and standard labor hours.
- Errors per 1,000 lines, root causes, customer impact, and correction cost.
- Inventory accuracy, replenishment, packing, damage, backlog, cycle time, OTIF, cutoff attainment, and premium freight.
- WMS capabilities, master-data accuracy, interfaces, cybersecurity, and network reliability.
- Building, fire-protection, power, floor-loading, space, and traffic constraints.
- Equipment utilization, downtime, fault rates, and recovery times.
- Employee skills, certifications, training needs, interests, and mobility options.
- Labor agreements, consultation requirements, and employment-law obligations.
- Network effects on inventory, transportation, service territories, and labor movement.
- Comparable vendor reference-site performance.

No site-specific technology, automation level, or workforce impact should be approved before this assessment.

## 4. Site-by-site automation recommendations

The table below is a **planning hypothesis**. It is not a commitment to technology, timing, or headcount change.

| Site | Recommended posture | Initial focus | Provisional timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A — largest/community anchor** | Selective, medium automation | Scanning, slotting, quality gates, ergonomics, selected pallet movement, and stable goods-to-person zones | Foundation Months 3–9; automation Months 21–36 |
| **B — high-volume standardized candidate** | Highest potential, subject to validation | AMRs or goods-to-person, conveyors, automated replenishment, scan validation, and WMS/WES improvements | Pilot Months 6–12; scale Months 12–18 |
| **C — medium-volume stable profile** | Medium-high, modular | Voice or pick-to-light, AMRs, put walls, pack verification, selective sortation | Months 15–27 |
| **D — smaller or variable-volume** | Flexible, medium-low | Mobile robots, scanning, dimensioning, ergonomic aids, limited conveyor extensions | Months 15–30, subject to economics |
| **E — low-volume or constrained** | Digital/process automation first | WMS configuration, voice/scan, slotting, cycle counting, and quality controls | Foundation Months 3–9; reassess Months 24–36 |

### Sequencing rationale

Site B should be the first advanced-automation candidate only if it has:

- Stable, dense volume.
- Suitable SKU and order characteristics.
- Adequate building and systems readiness.
- Recoverable service risk.
- Reliable data.
- Sufficient workforce and maintenance readiness.
- A positive base-case business case.

If Site B fails these tests, select the highest-scoring alternative. Site A should not be the first advanced-automation site unless its feasibility and risk profile clearly justify the exception.

### Network-level assessment

Before scaling any site, assess:

- Inventory rebalancing and transfer requirements.
- Transportation and freight impact.
- Cross-site order allocation.
- Whether one site becomes a network bottleneck.
- Effects on inbound staging and outbound capacity.
- Cross-site labor movements.
- Whether Site A should retain specialized, urgent, or exception-heavy work.

## 5. Automation architecture

### Common digital foundation

Deploy progressively across all sites:

- Accurate SKU dimensions, weights, barcodes, case packs, and locations.
- Directed picking using scan, voice, or pick-to-light.
- Scan confirmation at pick, pack, and shipment.
- Velocity-, similarity-, and ergonomics-based slotting.
- Cycle-count prioritization.
- Weight, image, or other pack verification for high-risk flows.
- Standard exception codes and work instructions.
- Labor standards and training-certification records.
- WMS/WES integration with open interfaces and documented data ownership.
- Common network dashboards and KPI definitions.

### Physical automation by process

| Process | Candidate technologies | Selection rule |
|---|---|---|
| Each picking | Voice, pick-to-light, AMRs, goods-to-person | High line density and repeatable SKU profile |
| Case picking | Ergonomic lifts, selective robotics, case/layer palletization | Stable, high-volume case flows |
| Replenishment | AMRs, conveyors, directed tasks, pallet shuttles | Repetitive routes and measurable congestion |
| Put-away | Scan-directed equipment, selective automated storage | Sufficient standardized volume |
| Packing | Cubing, weighing, vision, auto-labeling | High-error or high-value flows |
| Sortation | Put walls or automated sorters | Sustained volume supports utilization |
| Pallet movement | Autonomous pallet movers or forklifts | Controlled, repetitive routes |
| Returns and exceptions | Digital work queues and scan controls | Primarily human-led due to variability |

## 6. Phased timeline and decision gates

### Phase 0 — Mobilize and diagnose: Months 0–3

**Activities**

- Establish the automation office, steering committee, and site councils.
- Validate all baseline data.
- Complete site scoring, building, systems, cybersecurity, and safety reviews.
- Conduct a formal “competitor failure replay.”
- Complete workforce skills and labor-relations assessment.
- Build site-level operating models and cash-flow cases.
- Shortlist vendors and validate comparable reference sites.
- Begin employee, customer, and community communication.

**Gate 0: pilot authorization**

A pilot may proceed only when:

- Baseline data is finance-approved.
- Scope is bounded and reversible.
- Technical and safety feasibility is confirmed.
- Manual fallback capacity is quantified and tested.
- Workforce and customer plans are ready.
- The base case is economically credible and the conservative case is not materially unacceptable.

### Phase 1 — Digital and quality foundation: Months 3–9

**Activities**

- Master-data remediation.
- Directed picking.
- Scan compliance.
- Slotting.
- Pack verification.
- Inventory controls.
- Labor standards.
- Training and certification systems.
- KPI dashboards.

This phase may generate benefits independently of advanced physical automation.

**Gate 1: physical pilot readiness**

Proceed only if the target process demonstrates:

- At least 20% improvement in mix-adjusted labor hours, or a documented alternative value case.
- At least 30% error reduction or achievement of the required absolute accuracy standard.
- No material service or safety deterioration.
- Reliable data and exception reporting.
- Qualified supervisors and operators.

### Phase 2 — Reversible pilot: Months 6–12

Pilot one bounded flow, such as:

- A stable high-volume SKU family.
- A segregated each-pick zone.
- A repetitive replenishment route.
- A controlled AMR or goods-to-person module.

Operate legacy and new processes in parallel. Include peak simulation, outage drills, vendor support, and finance-approved measurement.

### Phase 3 — Reference-site scale-up: Months 12–18

Expand only after:

- 8–12 weeks of stable performance.
- Representative peak testing or independently validated stress testing.
- Successful planned and unplanned outage recovery.
- Customer service at or above agreed thresholds.
- Finance validation of recurring benefits.
- No unresolved critical safety risk.
- Vendor service-level compliance.

### Phase 4 — Sites C and D: Months 15–30

Deploy site-specific modular solutions. Do not copy Site B’s design without confirming volume, layout, SKU, labor, and service fit.

### Phase 5 — Site A controlled conversion: Months 21–36

Convert by zone:

1. Stable high-volume flows.
2. Replenishment and internal movement.
3. Additional each-pick areas.
4. Variable, customer-specific, and exception-heavy work.

Retain manual capability for urgent orders, returns, unusual SKUs, recovery, and specialized work.

### Phase 6 — Site E and network rebalance: Months 24–36

Complete only investments that pass the site-level hurdle. Reassess inventory, transportation, service territories, and specialized work allocation.

## 7. Service-continuity safeguards

### Capacity and inventory

Before each go-live, validate:

- Manual fallback capacity in orders or lines per hour.
- Cross-trained staffing by shift.
- Time required to activate fallback.
- Maximum outage duration that can be absorbed.
- Backlog recovery time.
- Critical inventory accessibility.
- Vendor and maintenance support.
- Spare equipment and parts.
- Alternate transportation.
- Customer-specific order priorities.

Inventory buffers should be calculated by SKU and account using demand variability, supplier lead time, recovery time, service criticality, and working-capital cost—not a blanket coverage target.

### Conversion method

1. Build and test the new flow in a segregated area.
2. Keep legacy operations active for unaffected work.
3. Introduce limited live volume.
4. Increase automated volume through daily gates.
5. Maintain dual operation through stabilization.
6. Freeze nonessential WMS and master-data changes near cutover.
7. Operate a daily executive war room for 30–60 days.

### Customer protections

- Notify key accounts of planned risk windows.
- Monitor key-account OTIF and accuracy separately.
- Prioritize critical and same-day orders.
- Maintain premium transportation options.
- Communicate proactively on delays.
- Preserve customer-specific labeling, packing, and compliance rules.

### Rollback plan

Each site must document and rehearse:

- Which inventory and locations can revert to legacy processing.
- How in-process automated orders are recovered.
- How WMS/WES transactions are reconciled.
- Manual shipping and labeling procedures.
- Fallback staffing and activation time.
- Maximum time to restore legacy processing.
- Customer-notification triggers.
- Named decision authority.

A manual lane is not considered a fallback until it has passed a live or simulated capacity test.

## 8. Workforce-transition plan

### Workforce principles

- Redeployment and attrition precede involuntary reduction.
- Mandatory training is paid.
- Long-tenured employees are invited to design, test, and train on future processes.
- Staffing is not reduced based solely on vendor projections.
- Future-state role requirements, documented performance, and lawful criteria govern decisions.
- Training must be accessible across shifts, languages, abilities, and digital skill levels.
- Employees receive practical qualification, not merely classroom attendance.

### Potential future roles

- Automated-equipment operators.
- Robot fleet coordinators.
- Maintenance and controls technicians.
- Inventory-control and cycle-count specialists.
- Quality and exception-resolution specialists.
- WMS super-users and master-data specialists.
- Trainers and process-improvement leads.
- Replenishment, packing, returns, and value-added service roles.
- Customer-specific order and compliance specialists.

### Workforce bridge

For each site and year, document:

| Category |
|---|
| Baseline employees and labor hours |
| Future-state employees and labor hours |
| Roles reduced or eliminated |
| Roles created |
| Redeployment capacity |
| Training throughput and certification |
| External hiring requirement |
| Vacancies avoided |
| Overtime and agency reduction |
| Voluntary exits |
| Potential involuntary reductions |
| Actual payroll savings |

Redeployed hours are not cash savings unless payroll or external labor cost actually falls.

### Transition sequence

1. Complete skills inventory and employee consultation.
2. Define future-state roles and qualification requirements.
3. Control hiring in roles expected to decline, while protecting service and safety.
4. Reduce overtime and agency reliance where sustainable.
5. Redeploy qualified employees.
6. Provide paid training, apprenticeships, and practical certification.
7. Offer transfers across sites and functions.
8. Consider voluntary separation or retirement programs only after legal review.
9. Use involuntary reductions only after stable operations and validated staffing requirements.

### If reductions are unavoidable

- Provide legally compliant notice and consultation.
- Use objective, documented selection criteria.
- Offer severance and benefits support where feasible.
- Provide outplacement, résumé and interview support, and job-fair partnerships.
- Maintain an appeal and review process.
- Report voluntary and involuntary exits separately.
- Do not use age, tenure, retirement assumptions, union status, or protected characteristics improperly.

### Training standards

Track enrollment, completion, assessment, certification, time to proficiency, 90-day retention, internal placement, wage changes, and shift changes. Do not assume that every picker can become a technician; use aptitude assessment, apprenticeships, external hiring, and realistic conversion rates.

## 9. Site A community-impact plan

Site A requires separate executive oversight because of its role as a major local employer.

### Commitments

- No mass reduction announcement before future-state staffing is validated.
- Zone-by-zone conversion with continued manual work.
- Priority access to paid technical, quality, inventory, and training roles.
- Partnerships with local colleges, technical schools, and workforce agencies.
- Paid apprenticeships in maintenance, controls, logistics technology, and quality.
- Transfers, transportation, and relocation assistance where practical.
- Local job fairs before involuntary reductions.
- Outplacement and benefits support.
- Assessment of whether maintenance, training, control-room, customer-specific, or specialized work can remain at Site A.
- Regular, honest employee and community updates.

### Gate for Site A

Before advanced conversion, approve a community-impact assessment showing:

- Current local employment and payroll.
- Expected direct employment change.
- Technical roles created.
- Training and placement capacity.
- Wage effects.
- Contractor and supplier effects.
- Mitigation cost.
- Network economics of retaining selected work locally.
- Maximum planned workforce impact requiring board escalation.

## 10. Financial and capital framework

### Fully loaded cost

Include:

- Equipment, installation, construction, power, fire, and network work.
- WMS/WES integration.
- Engineering, project management, and data cleanup.
- Training, backfill, dual running, and temporary labor.
- Lost capacity during conversion.
- Inventory and transportation buffers.
- Maintenance, software, utilities, spares, batteries, and technical labor.
- Ramp-up and downtime losses.
- Decommissioning and residual legacy costs.
- Working capital, financing, and contingency.

### Benefit categories

Report separately:

1. Cash headcount savings.
2. Avoided overtime and agency expense.
3. Avoided hiring.
4. Redeployed labor hours.
5. Error, claim, damage, and premium-freight savings.
6. Capacity created.
7. Customer-retention or growth benefits.
8. Safety and retention benefits.

### Financial cases

Prepare three views:

- **Cash case:** realized labor, overtime, agency, maintenance, quality, and other cash effects.
- **Economic case:** capacity, retention, avoided expansion, and strategic benefits with documented valuation.
- **Accounting case:** depreciation, operating expense, and reported earnings effects.

Use a monthly model for the first 24 months and include ramp-up rather than full benefits from day one.

### Required sensitivities

Test:

- Lower productivity.
- Lower utilization.
- Delayed labor savings.
- 10%, 15%, and 25% capital overruns as appropriate.
- Higher maintenance and software costs.
- Downtime and recovery delays.
- Volume decline and growth.
- Error reduction below vendor claims.
- Redeployment rather than headcount elimination.
- Network freight and inventory effects.

A project may proceed with a payback longer than three years if it has positive base-case NPV, meets service and safety requirements, and has separately documented strategic value. Any such exception requires investment-committee or board approval.

### Capital release

The 36-month roadmap is an approved planning framework, **not authorization to spend the full program envelope**.

| Tranche | Scope | Release condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnostic, data, feasibility, foundation | Executive approval |
| 2 | Pilot equipment, integration, training | Gate 0 |
| 3 | Reference-site scale-up | Pilot performance and Gate 1 |
| 4 | Sites C and D | Site-specific cases |
| 5 | Site A | Proven reference site, community plan, service reserve |
| 6 | Site E and network rebalance | Site-specific case |

Indicative capital envelope, normalized to total 100%:

| Category | Planning share |
|---|---:|
| Pilot and reference-site deployment | 25–30% |
| Digital foundation and integration | 15–18% |
| Site C | 12–16% |
| Site A | 18–22% |
| Sites D and E | 5–8% |
| Central contingency and independent validation | 10–15% |

Use approximately 10–15% contingency for standard deployments and 15–25% for complex brownfield work. Hold contingency centrally. Site A should also have a separate service-continuity reserve.

## 11. Implementation governance

### Executive steering committee

Members should include the COO, CFO, CHRO, CIO, site operations leader, safety leader, commercial/customer representative, legal counsel, and finance benefits controller.

Responsibilities:

- Approve sequencing and capital gates.
- Resolve service, labor, safety, and capital conflicts.
- Review benefits and risks monthly.
- Approve workforce and community commitments.
- Oversee Site A.
- Report quarterly to the board.

### Automation program office

Core roles:

- Program director.
- Site deployment leads.
- Finance benefits controller.
- WMS/WES and data lead.
- Engineering and safety lead.
- Workforce-transition lead.
- Change and communications lead.
- Vendor-management lead.
- Network-design lead.

Every benefit, KPI, risk, and corrective action must have one accountable owner.

### Site change councils

Include site leadership, operations, maintenance, supervisors, frontline representatives, safety, HR, IT, customer service, transportation, and labor representatives where applicable.

Meet weekly during implementation and daily during cutover.

## 12. Risk register and contingency triggers

| Risk | Preventive control | Trigger | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poor master data | Data audit and remediation | Scan failures or location errors | Pause volume; correct data |
| Integration failure | Simulation and transaction testing | Interface errors or queue growth | Hold rollout; restore legacy flow |
| Vendor overstatement | Paid pilot, reference validation, holdbacks | Benefits below case | Correct, renegotiate, or stop |
| Building incompatibility | Structural, fire, power, and traffic studies | Unresolved feasibility issue | Redesign or select another site |
| Peak overload | Stress test and manual capacity | Throughput or backlog breach | Cap automated volume; activate fallback |
| Equipment downtime | Spares, redundancy, local support, SLA | MTTR or availability breach | Manual fallback; vendor escalation |
| Premature labor reduction | Stable performance before staffing change | Overtime or service deterioration | Restore staffing; pause reductions |
| Safety incident | Independent validation and stop authority | Serious incident or critical hazard | Immediate stop and investigation |
| Workforce resistance or knowledge loss | Paid training and employee councils | Certification or engagement decline | Add support; adjust scope |
| Capital overrun | Stage gates and central contingency | Spend exceeds approved tolerance | Reforecast; reapprove or stop |
| Network bottleneck | Network model and cross-site monitoring | Freight, inventory, or backlog shift | Rebalance allocation and capacity |
| Cybersecurity or data privacy issue | Testing, access controls, workforce monitoring safeguards | Security breach or control failure | Isolate system; invoke incident plan |
| Vendor financial distress | Continuity protections and data portability | Service or solvency warning | Activate alternate support and transition plan |

## 13. KPI and benefits-realization scorecard

Targets below are **provisional management thresholds** unless contractual or legal requirements are stricter.

| Category | KPI and definition | Initial threshold | Owner | Review |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Service | OTIF: complete and on-time orders ÷ total orders | At or above baseline; no material key-account decline | Site leader / Customer service | Daily during ramp; monthly |
| Service | Cutoff attainment: orders shipped by promise ÷ orders due | At or above baseline | Site leader | Daily |
| Quality | Line accuracy: correct lines ÷ shipped lines | At least 98.5% or higher account standard | Quality lead | Daily |
| Quality | Error rate: errors ÷ shipped lines × 1,000 | 30% reduction in targeted process | Quality lead | Weekly |
| Productivity | Mix-adjusted labor hours per line | At least 20% improvement in eligible flow | Operations lead | Weekly |
| Cost | Cash labor, overtime, and agency savings | Finance-approved case | Finance controller | Monthly |
| Reliability | Availability: available time ÷ planned operating time | At least 97%, with definition approved before pilot | Engineering/IT | Daily/weekly |
| Reliability | Effective throughput | Meets approved plan after downtime and recovery | Operations lead | Daily |
| Workforce | Certification: qualified employees ÷ required employees | 100% before go-live | HR/site leader | Weekly |
| Workforce | Redeployment | Actual rate against site workforce bridge | Monthly |
| Safety | Recordables, near misses, critical hazards | No material deterioration; zero unresolved critical hazards | Safety lead | Daily/monthly |
| Customer | Claims, complaints, premium freight | Improvement versus baseline | Commercial lead | Weekly/monthly |
| Capital | Spend variance: actual ÷ approved − 1 | Within 10% or formally reapproved | Finance | Monthly |
| Benefits | Finance-validated recurring benefits ÷ approved benefits | At least 80% overall before expansion; at least 90% of claimed cash savings before staffing reduction | Finance | Monthly |

Every KPI must have a documented formula, source system, baseline period, owner, reporting frequency, target, escalation threshold, abnormal-event treatment, and audit method.

### Benefits controls

- Freeze the baseline before pilot changes.
- Use volume- and mix-adjusted comparisons.
- Use a control site where practical.
- Separate labor hours removed from hours redeployed.
- Do not count benefits from data/process improvements again as physical-automation benefits.
- Include maintenance, software, training, dual running, and support costs.
- Validate monthly for 12 months after go-live.
- Conduct an independent benefits audit at 12 months.

## 14. Decision gates and rollback authority

### Pre-go-live

Require:

- 100% pass on critical transaction and safety scenarios.
- No unresolved critical defects.
- Approved master-data and interface quality.
- Certified operators, supervisors, maintenance staff, and emergency responders.
- Demonstrated manual fallback.
- Completed safety and cybersecurity reviews.
- Available parts and support.
- Approved customer, transportation, labor, and communications plans.
- Funded inventory and capacity buffers.
- Rehearsed cutover and rollback.

### Expansion

Require:

- OTIF at or above baseline within approved ramp tolerance.
- Key-account requirements met.
- Absolute accuracy at or above customer/site minimum.
- Targeted error reduction achieved.
- Mix-adjusted productivity improvement demonstrated.
- Availability and effective throughput achieved.
- Stable exception rates.
- No critical safety issue.
- Cost within approved tolerance.
- Finance validation of recurring benefits.
- 8–12 weeks of stable operation plus peak or validated stress testing.

### Automatic pause or rollback

Pause expansion and restore legacy/manual processing when:

- Key-account OTIF is materially below threshold for two consecutive review periods.
- Accuracy falls below customer or site minimum.
- Backlog exceeds the approved recovery window.
- Critical outage exceeds the recovery-time objective.
- Manual fallback cannot process required emergency volume.
- A serious safety incident or uncontrolled hazard occurs.
- Throughput repeatedly misses plan after corrective action.
- Vendor response, parts, or availability commitments are breached.
- Cash benefits fall below the minimum gate for two consecutive reviews.
- Safe certified staffing is unavailable.

The site leader may stop operations immediately for safety or service protection. The program director may pause deployment and initiate rollback. The steering committee authorizes restart, redesign, or termination.

## 15. Immediate executive decisions

Approve now:

1. The staged, site-differentiated strategy.
2. The 90-day diagnostic and data-validation program.
3. Network-wide digital, scanning, slotting, and quality improvements.
4. A reversible pilot at the best-scoring site, provisionally Site B.
5. Paid workforce assessment, training, and redeployment planning.
6. Independent vendor, safety, cybersecurity, and financial validation.
7. Capital release by tranche.
8. Quantified service, fallback, rollback, and escalation plans.
9. A formal Site A community-impact assessment.
10. Monthly benefits reporting and quarterly board oversight.

Do not approve now:

- A guaranteed three-year payback.
- A predetermined workforce-reduction percentage.
- Fixed automation percentages by site.
- Full deployment capital for all five warehouses.
- Involuntary reductions before stable performance and future-state staffing are proven.

The practical objective is not maximum automation. It is a more accurate, safer, lower-turnover, and resilient distribution network that uses automation where the work and economics justify it, preserves manual flexibility where they do not, and gives employees and communities a credible path through the transition.
