# Warehouse Automation & Workforce Transition Roadmap  
## Board decision paper

## 1. Recommendation

Approve a **modular, process-led automation program**, beginning with a 90-day network diagnostic and stabilization phase. Do **not** approve a simultaneous five-site rollout or accept vendor claims of a three-year payback without an independently validated company-specific business case.

### Rationale

This approach addresses rising labor cost, 35% picker turnover, and customer-impacting errors while limiting the risk of a service failure, workforce shock, or reputational event. It:

- Automates repetitive, error-prone, and ergonomically difficult work first.
- Preserves manual bypass and peak flexibility.
- Uses a medium-volume, operationally ready site as the learning environment.
- Defers the largest, community-dependent warehouse until the design and transition model are proven.
- Separates cash savings from redeployment, capacity, quality, and safety benefits.
- Releases capital only when operational, workforce, safety, service, and economic evidence supports the next stage.

**Program objective:** improve accuracy, labor productivity, safety, service resilience, and workforce capability at an acceptable risk-adjusted return—not maximize robot deployment.

## 3. Assumptions and evidence required

### Known facts

- The network comprises five regional warehouses.
- Picker turnover is approximately 35% annually.
- Labor costs and customer-impacting error rates are rising.
- The largest warehouse is a major employer in its community.
- Hundreds of employees have long tenure.
- Capital is available but constrained.
- Vendors claim approximately three-year payback.
- A competitor’s failed rollout creates elevated service and reputational risk.

### Labeled planning assumptions

The following are **not established facts** and must be validated:

- Site 3 is the most suitable pilot site.
- Site 2 is the strongest medium-site production candidate.
- The network can economically support goods-to-person, AMR, sortation, or AS/RS technologies.
- Only 20–40% of displaced labor hours will translate into immediate cash savings through attrition, vacancies, redeployment, and growth absorption.
- The program can achieve 98% stabilized availability and 90% of required peak throughput.

### Discovery required before final sequencing

Collect and reconcile:

- Volume, SKU velocity, order mix, peak profile, travel, touches, and labor hours by process.
- Overtime, agency labor, absenteeism, turnover, wages, and error costs.
- Inventory and WMS accuracy, interfaces, and data quality.
- Building, power, floor-loading, network, fire, dock, and lease constraints.
- Customer-specific service requirements and recovery capacity.
- Workforce skills, certifications, mobility, retirement eligibility where lawful, and training capacity.
- Collective-bargaining, consultation, job-classification, seniority, and technological-change obligations.

No major production capital should be approved until Finance signs off on the baseline and the role-impact assessment is complete.

## 4. Site sequencing

Automation will be selected at the **site-process level**, not by site size alone.

### Candidate scoring

Score each site-process combination from 1 to 5:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Cash labor, overtime, and agency opportunity | 20% |
| Volume and likely utilization | 15% |
| Process and SKU stability | 15% |
| Error and customer-service exposure | 15% |
| WMS, inventory, and data readiness | 10% |
| Building and infrastructure fit | 10% |
| Workforce-transition feasibility | 5% |
| Manual recovery and bypass capability | 5% |
| Capital efficiency | 5% |

**Minimum screening standard:** overall score of at least 3.5/5; at least 3/5 for process stability, data readiness, and recovery; no score below 2/5 for safety, customer risk, or building suitability.

### Recommended sequence

| Wave | Site / scope | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | All sites | Stabilize data, inventory, standard work, slotting, scanning, replenishment, and error controls |
| 1 | Site 3 or Site 2 | Pilot one defined process or zone representing approximately 15–25% of site volume |
| 2 | Second medium site or pilot expansion | Scale only after representative peak testing and 90-/180-day validation |
| 3 | Site 1 | Deploy modularly, beginning with verification, ergonomic movement, replenishment, and stable high-velocity zones |
| 4 | Sites 4 and 5 | Use mainly flexible digital, ergonomic, and selective transport automation |

### Site-specific posture

**Site 1 — largest/community anchor**  
Use as a later lighthouse. Begin with low-disruption controls, ergonomic improvements, replenishment, selected sortation, and stable high-velocity work. Defer broad AS/RS or highly fixed goods-to-person systems until proven elsewhere. Build Site 1 into a training, maintenance, quality, exception, and complex-order capability center. Its community importance requires a higher preparation and transition standard, not an uneconomic exemption.

**Site 2 — medium-high volume**  
Potential first production site if it has stable demand, strong data, adequate bypass capacity, and suitable infrastructure. Candidate technologies include modular goods-to-person, put walls, sortation, scan-and-weight verification, replenishment triggers, and AMR-assisted movement.

**Site 3 — medium-volume learning site**  
Default pilot candidate, subject to scoring. Pilot one defined process or zone, retaining manual processing for critical flows. Initial technologies should be low-regret and modular: RF/voice, pick-to-light, scan validation, small-scale sortation, AMR-assisted movement, and labor-planning tools.

**Site 4 — smaller/lower complexity**  
Prioritize RF/voice, slotting, scan validation, powered carts, lift assistance, and selective sortation. Avoid large fixed systems unless site economics clearly support them.

**Site 5 — specialized or volatile volume**  
Remain primarily manual with digital work instructions, ergonomic equipment, scan validation, selective AMRs, and flexible scheduling. Do not force standardization where it reduces resilience or utilization.

## 5. Workforce transition

### Operating principles

1. Map future roles before finalizing equipment scope.
2. Use vacancies, natural attrition, reduced agency labor, redeployment, and voluntary programs before involuntary reductions where commercially and legally feasible.
3. Provide paid training and accessible certification.
4. Treat long-tenured employees as a source of process knowledge and future capability.
5. Do not use age, health, disability, union activity, or tenure as proxies for selection.
6. Obtain legal and labor-relations review before applying selection criteria.

### Required workforce-flow model

For each site, process, role, shift, and wave:

\[
\text{Employees requiring placement}
=
\text{roles reduced}
-\text{attrition}
-\text{vacancies}
-\text{retirements or voluntary separations}
-\text{redeployment}
-\text{growth absorption}
\]

Classify outcomes as:

- Retrained into automation, quality, inventory, exception, or maintenance-support roles.
- Redeployed within the site or to another site.
- Retained for peak and complex work.
- Voluntarily separated.
- Involuntarily separated, if unavoidable.

Potential future roles include automation technicians, controls specialists, fleet coordinators, control-room operators, inventory analysts, exception specialists, quality auditors, trainers, maintenance planners, safety coordinators, and labor-planning analysts.

### Transition timeline

- **Months 0–3:** skills, role, mobility, and labor-obligation inventory.
- **Months 3–6:** future-role design, training capacity, consultation, and placement planning.
- **Six to nine months before cutover:** paid training, simulation, certification, and employee placement.
- **Post-placement:** 30-, 60-, and 90-day coaching and proficiency review.

For Site 1, engage local officials, workforce agencies, technical schools, and community organizations before material employment impacts become public.

## 6. Service continuity, safety, and resilience

Service continuity is a mandatory design requirement.

### Before each go-live

Define and fund:

- Manual throughput by hour and shift.
- Critical-SKU inventory buffers.
- Maximum acceptable backlog.
- Maximum outage duration.
- Overtime and temporary-labor capacity.
- Alternate-site capacity.
- Customer-priority rules.
- Recovery time and recovery point objectives.
- Rollback authority and procedure.

### Cutover controls

- Convert one zone, shift, or process at a time.
- Avoid peak and critical-account periods.
- Reconcile inventory and cycle-count affected locations.
- Run manual and automated flows in parallel.
- Maintain a network control tower for the first 90 days.
- Conduct outage, cyber, and manual-recovery drills.
- Do not decommission manual capability until exit criteria are met.

### Safety controls

- Complete task-level hazard and human-factors assessments.
- Validate labor standards with employee input.
- Monitor pace, fatigue, breaks, overtime, cognitive load, and near misses.
- Segregate pedestrians and robots using guarding, geofencing, speed controls, interlocks, and controlled crossings.
- Require lockout/tagout and certified jam-recovery procedures.
- Ensure automation targets cannot encourage unsafe bypasses.

A serious injury, repeated near miss, uncontrolled human-machine interaction, or unresolved cybersecurity vulnerability triggers immediate stop-work review.

## 7. Economics and capital discipline

Vendor payback claims are hypotheses, not approvals.

### Required total-cost-of-ownership model

Include:

- Equipment, software, integration, internal project labor, construction, utilities, safety, cybersecurity, training, inventory relocation, commissioning, and contingency.
- Parallel manual operations, temporary labor, cutover losses, and service-recovery costs.
- Maintenance, software escalation, utilities, technical labor, spare parts, insurance, upgrades, financing, residual manual capability, and decommissioning.
- Cashable labor, overtime, agency, error, damage, return, chargeback, and expedited-shipment benefits.

Separate:

1. Cash labor savings.
2. Overtime and agency savings.
3. Redeployment.
4. Avoided future hiring.
5. Capacity created.
6. Quality improvement.
7. Safety improvement.
8. Strategic resilience.

Required outputs are five- and ten-year NPV, IRR, simple and discounted payback, break-even volume, availability and labor capture, cost per order/line, capital-at-risk by gate, and downside-case economics.

Model base, downside, upside, and disruption cases. The downside case should include lower volume, lower labor capture, slower ramp, capital overrun, lower early availability, additional maintenance, and no customer-retention benefit.

### Capital release rules

- **Pilot:** may be approved for learning and risk reduction, but must have a credible path to economic viability.
- **Modular production deployment:** base-case NPV positive and not dependent on aggressive headcount elimination.
- **Fixed high-capital automation:** must remain economically positive under downside volume and availability assumptions or have a separately approved strategic-capacity rationale.
- **Contingency:** held centrally; not used to conceal poor economics or unresolved safety/service issues.

## 8. Benefits scorecard and thresholds

Finance owns the baseline and monthly benefits certification. Operations, HR, EHS, IT/Engineering, and Customer Service own their respective measures.

| Dimension | Measure | Scale threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Service | On-time shipment, fill rate, cycle time, perfect-order rate | No material deterioration; key-account thresholds met |
| Quality | Pick and inventory accuracy, error cost, damages, returns, chargebacks | Customer standard achieved; error cost improves |
| Labor | Cash savings, lines per labor hour, overtime, agency labor | Labor capture at least 75% of approved plan after stabilization |
| Reliability | Availability, peak throughput, MTTR, bypass use | At least 98% stabilized availability and 90% required peak throughput |
| Workforce | Certification, redeployment, placement, proficiency, turnover | At least 95% certification before go-live; placement plan substantially met |
| Safety | Recordables, near misses, ergonomic incidents | No deterioration; critical event triggers stop-work review |
| Capital | Spend, contingency, schedule | Normally within 10% of approval; greater variance requires review |
| Financial | Net benefit, NPV, payback, cost per order/line | Base case remains positive; benefits at least 90% of phased plan |
| Community | Local training, placement, involuntary reductions, stakeholder issues | Transparent reporting and mitigation at Site 1 |

### Decision triggers

**Scale** when:

- Availability is at least 98% after stabilization.
- Peak throughput is at least 90% of required rate.
- Service and quality targets are met.
- Labor capture is at least 75% of plan.
- Net benefits are at least 90% of the phased case.
- Operators and maintainers are certified.
- No critical safety, cyber, or rollback issue remains.

**Correct or redesign** when:

- Benefits are 60–90% of plan.
- Technology works but data, staffing, training, integration, or process design is weak.
- Service is protected but ramp-up is slower than forecast.
- Manual bypass use is persistently above plan.

**Pause or defer** when:

- Availability remains below 95% after the agreed ramp period.
- Customer service falls below approved protection thresholds.
- Capital exceeds 10% over approval without reforecast.
- Required technical or workforce capability is unavailable.
- Downside economics become uncertain.

**Stop or rollback** when:

- A serious safety event or unresolved cyber vulnerability occurs.
- Rollback cannot be executed within the approved recovery time.
- Labor capture is below 60% after six months without a credible correction.
- Capital exceeds 15% over approval without a revised positive case.
- Benefits depend mainly on theoretical capacity rather than cashable or validated quality benefits.
- Key-account service or customer retention is materially threatened.

## Bottom line

Approve the 90-day diagnostic and stabilization phase, the process-level scoring model, workforce-transition preparation, and gated pilot design now. Defer major fixed automation and full-site commitments until discovery, pilot evidence, and independently validated economics support them.

The program succeeds only if it delivers **measurable cash and quality benefits without unacceptable service disruption, unsafe work intensification, workforce harm, cyber exposure, or community damage**.
