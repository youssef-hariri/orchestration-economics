# Everyday Balance  
## Ethical Japan Launch Campaign  
### Executive Campaign Framework and Readiness Plan

**Market:** Japan  
**Campaign status:** Pre-launch validation  
**Product status:** Magnesium-containing product; final formulation, Japanese category, product description, serving, intended users, and claims pending approval

> **No public advertising, packaging, influencer content, retailer listing, search advertising, or paid media may be released until the launch gates in Section 12 are documented as complete.**

---

## 1. Executive summary

Everyday Balance is proposed as an information-led campaign for a magnesium-containing product in Japan. The campaign will present the product as an **optional, clearly explained consumer product**, not as medicine, treatment, prevention, diagnosis, or a substitute for food or professional care.

The campaign’s central principles are:

- **Clear information**
- **Voluntary choice**
- **No pressure**
- **Evidence before claims**
- **Local Japanese review before publication**

The strategy emphasizes transparent product information, understandable serving guidance, responsible safety communication, fair purchasing practices, privacy-safe targeting, and accountable monitoring.

This document is a strategic framework, not evidence of Japanese regulatory compliance. All product descriptions, labels, claims, translations, creative executions, sales mechanics, and disclosures require appropriate Japanese regulatory, scientific, privacy, and cultural review.

# 2. Product and regulatory readiness

## 2.1 Planning assumptions

The following are planning assumptions only and must not be used as public claims until validated:

- The product contains magnesium.
- It may be supplied in capsule form.
- It may be sold through Japanese e-commerce, pharmacies, retailers, and digital media.
- It may be intended for adults, subject to formulation-specific safety assessment.
- It may be positioned around transparent product information and label-compliant use.

The company must not publicly describe the product as a “supplement,” “for adults,” “daily,” “convenient,” or equivalent Japanese wording until the precise wording and product classification have been approved locally.

## 2.2 Required product-regulatory file

Before creative approval, compile:

- complete ingredient and excipient list;
- exact magnesium compound;
- elemental magnesium amount per serving;
- recommended serving and maximum intended use;
- allergen information;
- manufacturing country and facility;
- supplier qualification records;
- importer and distributor details;
- certificates of analysis;
- identity, purity, contamination, and stability testing;
- storage conditions and expiry period;
- proposed Japanese label and packaging;
- lot-release criteria;
- lot-tracking and withdrawal procedures;
- authenticity and counterfeit controls;
- proposed price and sales model;
- all proposed advertising, search, FAQ, retailer, affiliate, influencer, and customer-service language.

Testing claims must identify precisely what was tested. Identity, purity, contamination, stability, or lot-release testing must not be presented as proof of efficacy, superiority, or universal safety.

## 2.3 Required Japanese review

Qualified Japanese regulatory counsel or an appropriately experienced local specialist must provide written advice on:

1. Product classification.
2. Approved product description.
3. Applicable labeling and advertising requirements.
4. Required warnings and disclaimers.
5. Whether any nutrient-function wording is available for the exact formulation and dose.
6. Scientific substantiation requirements.
7. Online sales, pricing, subscription, cancellation, and refund requirements.
8. Influencer and stealth-marketing disclosure requirements.
9. Privacy and audience-targeting requirements.

A disclaimer must not be used to cure a misleading principal message.

# 3. Scientific substantiation and claims governance

## 3.1 Controlled claims register

Every consumer-facing statement must be entered into a controlled claims register.

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Exact Japanese wording | Final proposed copy |
| English meaning | Translation reference only |
| Claim category | Factual, contextual, functional, comparative, testimonial |
| Evidence | Source, quality, and date |
| Dose match | Whether evidence matches the actual product |
| Population match | Whether evidence matches intended users |
| Regulatory status | Approved, restricted, or prohibited |
| Reviewers | Scientific, legal, regulatory, and translation reviewers |
| Version and owner | Approval record |
| Review date | Reapproval or expiry date |

The register must cover:

- labels and packaging;
- websites and FAQs;
- paid advertisements;
- search keywords and metadata;
- retailer and marketplace listings;
- automated product feeds;
- affiliate pages;
- creator content and comments;
- expert content;
- customer-service scripts;
- brand replies and reposts.

## 3.2 Launch claim tiers

### Tier 1: Verified factual information

Potentially usable only after verification:

- ingredient identity;
- elemental amount per serving;
- package quantity;
- approved serving instructions;
- storage and expiry information;
- manufacturing or lot information;
- specifically defined quality-control facts;
- customer-service details;
- how to find ingredients, cautions, and serving information.

### Tier 2: Contextual and routine language

Potentially usable only after Japanese regulatory and consumer testing:

- “Review the ingredients and serving information before use.”
- “Use only according to the approved label.”
- “An optional product for consideration as part of a personal routine.”
- “Contact a pharmacist or customer service with questions.”

This language must not imply that use is necessary, appropriate for everyone, or beneficial for a symptom.

### Tier 3: Functional, outcome, comparative, and testimonial claims

Excluded from launch unless separately approved for the exact product, formulation, dose, category, wording, and execution:

- improves sleep;
- reduces fatigue, stress, cramps, or constipation;
- improves concentration, mood, energy, or performance;
- supports immunity;
- prevents or corrects deficiency;
- treats, cures, diagnoses, or prevents disease;
- works within a specified timeframe;
- “clinically proven”;
- “doctor recommended” or “pharmacist approved”;
- “best,” “number one,” “superior,” or other unsupported comparative claims;
- “safe for everyone” or “no side effects”;
- claims that the product replaces food, sleep, exercise, or medical care.

Biological plausibility alone does not establish that a claim is lawful, product-specific, dose-appropriate, or meaningful for the intended audience.

## 3.3 Implied-claim testing

Japanese-language consumer research must test the full execution, including copy, imagery, product placement, disclaimers, calls to action, landing pages, search context, and mobile cropping.

Testing should determine whether consumers infer that the product:

- treats or prevents a condition;
- corrects a deficiency;
- relieves symptoms;
- improves sleep, energy, mood, concentration, or performance;
- is medically recommended;
- is safe for all adults;
- is necessary for a healthy lifestyle.

A proposed pilot control is:

> No more than 5% of respondents selecting a treatment, deficiency, or guaranteed-benefit interpretation.

This is a provisional research trigger only, not a legal safe harbor. Any serious misunderstanding identified through open-ended responses requires review regardless of percentage.

# 4. Safety and responsible-use framework

## 4.1 Professional safety review

A qualified Japanese pharmacist, physician, or other appropriately qualified professional must review the final formulation, label, and customer-service materials for:

- gastrointestinal effects, including possible diarrhea depending on form and dose;
- kidney impairment;
- medication and mineral-absorption interactions;
- concurrent magnesium-containing products, laxatives, or antacids;
- pregnancy and breastfeeding;
- child use;
- allergies and excipients;
- accidental overconsumption;
- use by consumers attempting to manage symptoms or diagnosed conditions.

## 4.2 Consumer information

The approved label and product page must clearly provide:

- serving limits;
- relevant warnings;
- who should seek professional advice before use;
- storage and expiry information;
- what to do if an adverse reaction occurs;
- Japanese customer-service contact details.

Warnings must be readable on mobile and retail materials. They must not be hidden in low-contrast text, collapsed sections, or post-purchase-only pages.

Final Japanese wording, prominence, and placement require local professional review.

## 4.3 Customer-service boundaries

Customer-service staff must not:

- diagnose deficiency;
- interpret symptoms;
- recommend exceeding the label serving;
- advise consumers to stop prescribed treatment;
- claim universal safety or suitability;
- provide individualized medical advice.

Questions involving medication, pregnancy, kidney conditions, child use, diagnosed conditions, or adverse reactions must be escalated.

Consumers reporting serious or rapidly worsening reactions should be directed to seek appropriate urgent medical assistance. Final Japanese wording requires professional review.

## 4.4 Safety operating procedure

Before launch, approve a written safety SOP covering:

1. Complaint and adverse-event intake.
2. Required consumer, product, lot, and exposure information.
3. Seriousness classification.
4. Medical and quality review.
5. Lot investigation.
6. Advertising-pause and product-hold authority.
7. Recall criteria.
8. Distributor and regulator communications, where applicable.
9. Consumer follow-up.
10. Record retention and trend analysis.

A serious adverse event, material labeling error, or lot-related complaint cluster requires escalation regardless of aggregate frequency.

# 5. Japanese market and cultural strategy

## 5.1 Strategic hypothesis

The campaign will test a calm, practical, information-led communication style. This is a research hypothesis, not a claim about all Japanese consumers.

The campaign should avoid broad assumptions about Japanese health beliefs, routines, age groups, or shopping behavior.

## 5.2 Required research

Japanese-language research must establish:

- magnesium awareness and category trust;
- supplement-use behavior;
- purchase motivations and barriers;
- preferred pharmacy, marketplace, and e-commerce channels;
- acceptable price per serving;
- domestic versus imported-product perceptions;
- interpretation of “routine,” “optional,” and “clear information”;
- warning and serving comprehension;
- reactions across age groups, genders, regions, and accessibility needs;
- whether pharmacist, laboratory, or professional imagery increases perceived efficacy.

## 5.3 Representation principles

Use ordinary adults in realistic settings, with diverse ages and abilities consistent with the approved audience.

Avoid:

- exhausted commuter stereotypes;
- fragile older-person portrayals;
- narrow body ideals;
- fear of aging or social burden;
- unrelated traditional symbols;
- assumptions that all Japanese consumers share the same beliefs or routines.

Visual restraint should be supported by concrete, documented information rather than treated as proof of quality.

# 6. Audience and privacy-safe targeting

## 6.1 Primary commercial segment

The final audience must be selected through research using non-sensitive characteristics, such as:

- supplement familiarity;
- shopping behavior;
- preferred channel;
- price sensitivity;
- interest in ingredient transparency;
- approved age range.

The campaign must not use inferred medical status to define the audience.

## 6.2 Excluded targeting

Do not directly target:

- children or teenagers;
- people seeking urgent medical advice;
- people searching for treatment of fatigue, insomnia, cramps, constipation, or other symptoms;
- audiences inferred to have a disease, deficiency, medication use, pregnancy, or other sensitive condition;
- pregnant or breastfeeding people unless specifically assessed and approved.

Avoid copy such as:

- “Are you tired all the time?”
- “People with poor sleep need magnesium.”
- “Your age may mean you are deficient.”

Use contextual placement beside general nutrition or lifestyle content only after privacy review.

## 6.3 Privacy readiness

Before launch, approve:

- a data inventory;
- data-flow map;
- Japanese privacy notice;
- cookie and consent controls where required;
- vendor due diligence;
- cross-border transfer review;
- audience-construction rules;
- sensitive-audience suppression;
- retention and deletion schedule;
- privacy incident response.

Do not use symptom searches, medication questions, pregnancy information, kidney-condition information, customer-service disclosures, inferred deficiency, or inferred health status for advertising audiences or lookalike models unless privacy counsel documents a specific lawful and ethical basis.

# 7. Strategic positioning and funnel

## 7.1 Positioning

> For Japanese consumers seeking understandable product information, the product provides a clearly labeled option to consider within a personal routine, subject to the approved directions and warnings.

The final Japanese positioning must be created by a native Japanese copywriter and approved by regulatory reviewers. It must not imply broad suitability, physiological benefit, or necessity.

## 7.2 Documented differentiation

The product may compete through documented trust attributes, such as:

- transparent formulation and elemental amount;
- clear serving and caution information;
- verified manufacturing information;
- lot-level information, where available;
- responsive Japanese customer support;
- straightforward pricing and cancellation.

These are not superiority claims unless supported by current comparative evidence.

## 7.3 Funnel

1. **Awareness:** Product identity and transparent positioning.
2. **Consideration:** Ingredients, amount, serving, quality information, price, and cautions.
3. **Conversion:** Clear offer and purchasing terms without pressure.
4. **Post-purchase:** Correct use, safety information, and support.
5. **Retention:** Voluntary, informed replenishment—not pressure or implied efficacy.

Test at least four creative routes:

- information-led;
- convenience- or format-led, if substantiated;
- pharmacy label-literacy;
- product-detail and quality-information-led.

Select a route using both commercial and consumer-understanding outcomes.

# 8. Creative platform

## Big idea

### “A clear choice for your daily routine”

The campaign presents the product as optional and information-led—not as a solution to a problem or a required health ritual.

## Creative principles

Use:

- Japanese copy first and prominently;
- the full product label where practical;
- ordinary adults in realistic settings;
- readable serving and caution information;
- accessible contrast and typography;
- factual explanations;
- clear separation between advertising and reviews.

Avoid:

- before-and-after imagery;
- medical coats, clinics, diagnostic equipment, or physician-like endorsement;
- visible relief from symptoms;
- transformations in energy, mood, sleep, body, or performance;
- fear-based urgency;
- imagery suggesting deficiency or medical need.

## Working copy directions

The following are illustrative only and are not approved final Japanese copy.

**Website**

> Everyday Balance  
> Clear product information for an everyday routine.

**Product page**

> Review the ingredients, serving instructions, and cautions before use. Use only according to the approved Japanese label.

**Social content**

> An optional product to consider as part of an everyday routine. Check the label before use.

**Pharmacy material**

> Review the ingredients, serving information, and cautions. Ask a pharmacist or store staff if you have questions.

Native Japanese copywriters and Japanese regulatory reviewers must approve all final language. Terms such as “balance,” “support,” “refresh,” and “organize your day” must be tested for implied physiological or mental-health meaning.

English text must not communicate a stronger benefit or broader suitability than the approved Japanese version.

# 9. Influencer, affiliate, and expert controls

## 9.1 Platform disclosure matrix

Before publication, create a platform-specific matrix covering:

- disclosure wording;
- placement;
- size and contrast;
- on-screen duration;
- caption and thumbnail requirements;
- livestream procedures;
- affiliate-link disclosure;
- reposting and editing;
- monitoring frequency;
- responsible owner;
- correction and removal deadline.

Depending on the relationship and execution, wording may include **広告**, **提供**, or **タイアップ**. The appropriate wording and presentation must be confirmed under current Japanese guidance.

A disclosure hidden below the fold, behind an expansion, or only in a profile is insufficient where consumers cannot readily understand the commercial relationship.

## 9.2 Contracts

Creator, affiliate, and expert agreements must prohibit unapproved health, efficacy, safety, suitability, comparative, or personal-outcome claims in:

- videos;
- captions;
- comments;
- replies;
- livestreams;
- private messages;
- affiliate materials;
- reposts.

If personal use is mentioned, the company must retain evidence that the person used the product and that the statement reflects an honest opinion.

The company must not reward only positive reviews or suppress legitimate negative feedback.

## 9.3 Experts

Professional partners must have:

- verified, relevant credentials;
- documented compensation or material connection;
- evidence-linked approved statements;
- sponsorship disclosure;
- no diagnosis, treatment advice, or universal-safety claims.

Label-literacy education is lower risk than product-efficacy endorsement. A professional’s presence must not imply efficacy merely by association.

# 10. E-commerce, pricing, and promotions

The company must select and document the launch offer before creative approval:

- one-time purchase;
- subscription;
- trial;
- introductory discount;
- bundle;
- free shipping;
- marketplace checkout;
- pharmacy promotion.

For every offer, clearly disclose:

- total price;
- shipping and taxes;
- quantity;
- one-time or recurring status;
- renewal date;
- minimum term;
- cancellation method and deadline;
- refund policy;
- trial conversion;
- order confirmation.

Use affirmative subscription consent, no pre-checked boxes, mobile-readable terms, and simple cancellation.

Test the complete purchase journey on mobile and desktop, including cancellation, refunds, chargebacks, confirmation, and post-purchase communications.

Set operational standards for:

- cancellation acknowledgment;
- cancellation completion;
- refund processing;
- billing-error resolution;
- chargeback response.

Do not use countdowns, “last chance,” stock warnings, inflated reference prices, or continuously available discounts presented as unique opportunities unless objectively accurate, documented, and legally reviewed.

## 12.2 Readiness phases

### Phase 0: Validation — Weeks 1–8

Complete and document:

- formulation and dose;
- Japanese classification;
- safety assessment;
- final label and warnings;
- claims register;
- legal, scientific, translation, and cultural review;
- implied-claim testing;
- privacy assessment;
- offer and cancellation testing;
- safety, complaint, recall, and crisis SOPs;
- creator disclosure matrix;
- importer, distributor, storage, authenticity, and lot-tracking readiness;
- training records.

### Phase 1: Controlled pilot — Weeks 9–12

- Use only approved factual and contextual language.
- Launch owned product information and limited distribution.
- Test serving, warning, purchase-term, and non-medicine comprehension.
- Monitor complaints, adverse events, refunds, chargebacks, and cancellation attempts.
- Pause any execution producing treatment, deficiency, guaranteed-benefit, or universal-safety interpretations.

### Phase 2: Limited paid expansion — Months 4–6

Expand only channels meeting documented legal, privacy, disclosure, comprehension, safety, cancellation, and refund requirements.

### Phase 3: Ongoing review — Months 7–12

- Review law, evidence, formulation, and platform requirements quarterly.
- Reapprove claims after material changes.
- Audit creators, retailers, affiliates, and customer service.
- Review complaints and adverse events by lot and channel.
- Scale only where commercial performance remains consistent with informed choice.

## 12.3 Supply-chain and incident readiness

Before launch, confirm:

- importer of record;
- distributor responsibilities;
- storage and transport controls;
- expiry management;
- lot traceability from inventory to consumer;
- counterfeit and authenticity monitoring;
- product withdrawal capability;
- recall communications;
- crisis contact list.

Conduct readiness simulations covering:

- an unauthorized creator claim;
- a serious adverse-event report;
- a defective or mislabelled lot;
- a privacy incident;
- a failed subscription cancellation;
- a retailer listing containing an unapproved claim.

# 13. Measurement and stop rules

Every KPI must have a definition, baseline, target, warning threshold, stop threshold, source, sample or method, frequency, and owner.

| KPI area | Example measure |
|---|---|
| Commercial | Product-page visits, conversion, contribution margin, CAC, sell-through |
| Information use | Ingredient-page views, serving-information completion |
| Understanding | Correct identification as a non-medicine product; warning and serving comprehension |
| Fair purchasing | Failed cancellation attempts, refund time, chargebacks |
| Compliance | Ad disapproval, claims-register coverage, disclosure compliance |
| Content control | Unauthorized-claim rate and correction time |
| Safety | Adverse-event trends, serious-event escalation time, lot-related complaints |
| Privacy | Consent or control failures, audience-policy violations |
| Accessibility | Readability complaints and comprehension differences |

### Provisional pilot controls

The following are proposed internal control targets and must be confirmed by the legal, safety, privacy, and research leads:

| Measure | Proposed control |
|---|---:|
| Correct identification as a non-medicine product | At least 95% |
| Correct understanding of approved use | At least 90% |
| Ability to locate serving instructions | At least 90% |
| Ability to locate key warnings | At least 90% |
| Treatment, deficiency, or guaranteed-benefit interpretation | Below 5% |
| Public statements linked to approved claims register | 100% |
| Creator and expert disclosure compliance | 100% before publication |
| Failed cancellation attempts | 0 unresolved cases |
| Refunds processed within approved service standard | At least 95% within target period |
| Unauthorized paid or brand-controlled health claims | 0 |
| Serious-event escalation | 100% within the approved SOP deadline |
| Unresolved material privacy incidents | 0 |
| Public label or serving errors | 0 |

These targets are not legal safe harbors. Serious events, material errors, or systemic failures require escalation regardless of numerical rates.

### Mandatory stop or escalation conditions

Pause the affected execution, and where appropriate the wider campaign, for:

- any serious adverse event;
- material labeling, serving, or warning error;
- lot-related complaint cluster;
- privacy or data-use incident;
- systemic cancellation or refund failure;
- undisclosed sponsored content;
- repeated treatment, deficiency, guaranteed-benefit, or universal-safety interpretations;
- inability to monitor or correct unauthorized claims;
- evidence that the product is being marketed for diagnosed conditions.

Repeat purchase must not be treated as evidence of efficacy.

# 14. Final launch gate

No public launch or paid scale may occur until documentary evidence confirms:

1. Final formulation, magnesium compound, elemental amount, serving, storage, and expiry.
2. Written Japanese product-classification opinion.
3. Dose-specific scientific and safety review.
4. Final Japanese label and warnings.
5. Approved claims register.
6. Native Japanese translation and cultural review.
7. Consumer testing showing no material inference of medicine, treatment, deficiency, guaranteed benefit, or universal safety.
8. Privacy and audience-targeting approval.
9. Platform-specific creator, affiliate, and expert disclosures.
10. Validated pricing, subscription, cancellation, refund, and checkout journey.
11. Adverse-event, complaint, recall, crisis, and product-hold procedures.
12. Importer, distributor, storage, authenticity, expiry, and lot-tracking readiness.
13. Named owners, deputies, version control, and completed training.
14. KPI baselines, targets, stop rules, data sources, and reporting owners.
15. Active monitoring across retailer, affiliate, creator, marketplace, customer-service, search, and brand-amplified content.

If any gate is incomplete, the company may conduct internal research but must not release the affected public execution.

---

## Ethical success standard

Everyday Balance should remain an **optional, clearly explained product**. It must never be marketed as a medical solution, a required condition of health, a response to fear, or a substitute for professional care.

The campaign is successful only when it delivers sustainable commercial results alongside:

- accurate information;
- voluntary purchasing;
- fair pricing and cancellation;
- culturally respectful communication;
- effective safety monitoring;
- privacy protection;
- accountable consumer protection.
