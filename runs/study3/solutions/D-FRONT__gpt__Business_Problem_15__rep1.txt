# Global Future-of-Work Framework

## 1. Executive proposition

Adopt a **globally coherent, locally adaptable, role- and activity-based work model**.

The objective is not to impose identical office-attendance rules in every country. It is to create a consistent decision system that enables the company to:

- Deliver customer, operational, financial, and innovation outcomes
- Attract, develop, and retain diverse talent
- Provide flexibility where work permits it
- Use offices and collaboration technology purposefully
- Protect security, privacy, health, safety, and business continuity
- Provide equitable access to information, development, recognition, and advancement
- Respect local law, collective arrangements, infrastructure, labor markets, and cultural expectations

The model should distinguish four separate decisions:

1. **Where** work may be performed  
2. **When** work may be performed  
3. **How** teams collaborate  
4. **How** performance is assessed  

“Hybrid” should not be treated as a sufficient policy or operating model.

---

## 2. Global design principles

### 1. Start with work and outcomes

Work arrangements should reflect activities, required outcomes, customer needs, operational dependencies, safety, security, and legal requirements—not job title, seniority, historical practice, or manager preference.

### 2. Classify work, not people

A single role may contain both remote-capable and presence-dependent activities. Assess work at the appropriate level of detail rather than assigning a single location rule solely to a job family.

### 3. Global standards, local implementation

The company should standardize principles, minimum protections, terminology, decision criteria, security expectations, and measurement methods. Countries, business units, and teams may adapt implementation where justified by local evidence, law, collective agreements, infrastructure, customer needs, or labor-market conditions.

### 4. Flexibility with coordination

Flexibility must be paired with clear service coverage, handoffs, collaboration windows, response expectations, decision rights, and availability boundaries.

### 5. Employee choice within legitimate guardrails

Where business requirements permit, employees should have meaningful choice among approved work and schedule options. Requests should be evaluated transparently and separately from legally protected accommodations or other protected forms of flexibility.

### 6. Outcomes over visibility

Performance should not be judged by office attendance, badge swipes, online status, webcam use, meeting volume, or after-hours responsiveness. Assessment should use role-appropriate evidence such as quality, customer impact, delivery, judgment, collaboration, risk management, innovation, and team contribution.

### 7. Equity of opportunity

Work location must not informally determine access to:

- Information and decision-making
- Strategic assignments
- Senior leaders
- Recognition
- Sponsorship and mentoring
- Learning and development
- Promotion and pay progression

### 8. Design for all workforces

The model must support remote-capable employees as well as site-based, shift-based, frontline, customer-facing, field, and operational employees. For these groups, flexibility may mean predictable rosters, shift choice, schedule control, alternative sites, or other locally appropriate arrangements—not only remote work.

### 9. Inclusion and accessibility by design

Offices, technology, meetings, and policies should support different abilities, languages, time zones, cultures, religious practices, caregiving circumstances, and communication styles. Universal design should be supplemented by confidential individual accommodations.

### 10. Security, privacy, safety, and compliance are enabling conditions

Flexible work must be designed alongside information security, data privacy, employment law, tax, immigration, health and safety, insurance, records management, and business continuity.

### 11. Evidence, transparency, and iteration

Major policy, technology, and property decisions should use quantitative data, local qualitative evidence, employee consultation, and pilots. Results should be reviewed by country, role, level, work arrangement, and relevant employee groups—not only through global averages.

## 3. Global standards and local adaptation

### Global minimum standards

The enterprise framework should establish the following intended minimum outcomes:

- Non-discrimination and accessibility
- Outcome-based performance management
- Protection from retaliation and presence-based career penalties
- Clear communication of work-model eligibility and decision criteria
- A fair route to request, review, and appeal decisions
- Confidential and timely accommodation processes
- Minimum security, privacy, and safe-working expectations
- Reasonable working-time, availability, and disconnection principles
- Equitable access to development and advancement
- Approved processes for cross-border work
- Proportionate and transparent use of employee data
- Appropriate consultation with employee representatives

These standards define the company’s global intent. Their procedural form, documentation, consultation process, and enforcement mechanism must be adapted where local law, collective agreements, or employee-representation rights require a different implementation.

### Matters requiring country-specific validation

Do not standardize the following without country-level evidence and qualified local review:

- Whether and how remote work is legally permitted
- Working-time limits, rest periods, flexible schedules, and disconnection rules
- Tax, payroll, social-security, and permanent-establishment implications
- Immigration and work authorization
- Data residency and cross-border data transfers
- Home-working health and safety obligations
- Equipment, connectivity, workspace, utility, and travel support
- Collective bargaining, works council, or consultation requirements
- Office-attendance expectations
- Benefits, allowances, and geographic pay practices
- Rules for contractors and agency workers
- Religious and public-holiday arrangements
- Employee monitoring and workplace analytics
- Approved work locations within or outside a country

Each country should maintain a **country playbook** stating:

- What differs from the global framework
- Why the variation is necessary
- Which employees and entities it covers
- Who approved it
- How employees and representatives were consulted
- Request and appeal timelines
- When the variation will be reviewed

Cultural norms may inform communication and implementation, but may not justify discrimination, retaliation, opaque decision-making, or informal presence requirements.

## 4. Role- and activity-based work model

### Assessment criteria

Every role or team should be assessed using a common template. The assessment should document:

- Required outcomes
- Activities requiring physical presence or particular hours
- Evidence supporting the assessment
- Customer and operational dependencies
- Employee and inclusion impacts
- Legal, tax, privacy, security, accessibility, and safety considerations
- Default arrangement and permitted flexibility
- Decision owner
- Review date and change triggers

| Dimension | Key questions |
|---|---|
| Physical dependency | Are equipment, materials, facilities, laboratories, or controlled environments required? |
| Customer or operational need | Is in-person service, site coverage, or real-time response required? |
| Security and confidentiality | Does work require a controlled physical or digital environment? |
| Time sensitivity | Are shifts, fixed service windows, or rapid escalation required? |
| Collaboration | Are dependencies frequent, complex, or time-critical? |
| Learning and relationships | Are apprenticeship, coaching, innovation, or trust-building important? |
| Focus requirements | Would uninterrupted individual work improve quality or delivery? |
| Geographic dependency | Must work occur at a particular site, in a particular country, or within an approved jurisdiction? |
| Employee feasibility | Are safe workspace, connectivity, equipment, and accessibility available? |
| Legal and regulatory constraints | Are there employment, tax, privacy, immigration, safety, or representation limits? |
| Talent and resilience | Would flexibility improve talent access, retention, or business continuity? |
| Cost and environmental impact | What are the implications for offices, travel, equipment, energy, and employee costs? |

### Work archetypes

Use these archetypes as decision aids, not automatic entitlements:

1. **Site-dependent**  
   Regular or continuous presence is required for equipment, safety, operations, secure facilities, or physical materials.

2. **Customer-, field-, or travel-based**  
   Work occurs primarily at customer premises, branches, plants, laboratories, stores, or other operational sites.

3. **Hybrid with predictable presence**  
   Remote work is feasible, but recurring team or site presence is valuable for coordination, learning, service, or relationships.

4. **Hybrid with event-based presence**  
   Presence is mainly required for defined events such as onboarding, planning, training, innovation, customer sessions, or complex problem-solving.

5. **Primarily remote**  
   Most activities can be performed away from company premises, subject to approved location, security, connectivity, collaboration, and in-person requirements.

6. **Shift- or coverage-based**  
   Flexibility focuses on scheduling, handoffs, and location within approved sites rather than remote work.

7. **Flexible-time eligible**  
   Start and finish times may vary within customer, team, workload, and legal boundaries.

A role may combine archetypes. For example, analysis may be remote while secure customer workshops require presence.

### Decision rule

An arrangement should be approved when it:

1. Supports required business and customer outcomes  
2. Is feasible for the role and approved jurisdiction  
3. Meets applicable safety, security, privacy, accessibility, employment, tax, and representation requirements  
4. Uses transparent and non-discriminatory criteria  
5. Does not create unreasonable hidden costs or constant-availability expectations  
6. Preserves equitable access to information, development, and advancement  
7. Includes a defined review and escalation mechanism  

A presence requirement should identify the specific activity or risk requiring presence. General statements such as “culture,” “visibility,” or “that is how we work” are insufficient on their own.

## 5. Decision rights and escalation

| Decision | Accountable owner | Required review | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Role or activity classification | Business leader | HR, security, workplace, accessibility, and local legal review as relevant | Regional or global council |
| Team operating agreement | Team leader | HR and employee representative where required | Business leader or HR |
| Ordinary flexibility request | Line manager | HR under defined criteria | Independent review or appeal route |
| Legally protected request | Locally designated authority | Local HR and legal | Applicable statutory or internal process |
| Disability or health accommodation | Local accommodation function | Occupational health, HR, or legal as appropriate | Confidential appeal route |
| Caregiving or temporary adjustment | Line manager or HR | Local HR/legal where required | HR review or appeal |
| Cross-border work | Authorized mobility or risk function | Tax, immigration, payroll, privacy, security, and legal | Global mobility or risk committee |
| Country deviation | Country forum | Regional/global council for material deviations | Global council |
| Monitoring or analytics tool | Technology or security owner | Privacy, legal, HR, and representatives where required | Data-governance committee |
| Office closure or major redesign | Workplace executive | Finance, business, HR, accessibility, legal, and representatives | Executive council |
| Pilot scale or termination | Executive sponsor | Pilot governance and risk owners | Global council |

This table is a default governance template. It must be adjusted where local law, collective agreements, employee representatives, or entity-level authority create formal approval or negotiation rights.

Every exception should have an owner, rationale, evidence, risk assessment, duration, communication plan, and review date.

## 6. Employee choice and team operating agreements

### Distinguish request categories

The company should separately manage:

- **Ordinary flexibility:** an approved business option subject to role and team requirements
- **Legally protected requests:** governed by applicable local law
- **Disability or health accommodations:** handled confidentially through the accommodation process
- **Caregiving or temporary-life-event adjustments:** assessed under locally appropriate policy
- **Business-critical exceptions:** limited arrangements requiring enhanced review

For ordinary flexibility, provide:

- Published eligibility criteria
- A menu of approved arrangements
- Clear response timelines
- Documented reasons for approval or refusal
- Trial arrangements where useful
- Periodic review
- An appeal or independent review route

Managers should not create materially different rules for comparable work without a documented, legitimate reason.

### Team operating agreements

Each team should maintain a short, accessible agreement covering:

- Where activities will normally occur
- Required presence and advance notice
- Core collaboration hours
- Customer and service coverage
- Communication channels and response times
- Meeting versus asynchronous-work rules
- Decision rights and escalation
- Documentation and record-keeping
- Availability and disconnection expectations
- Time-zone burden sharing
- Remote participation standards
- Onboarding, coaching, and informal connection

Team autonomy must operate within global, country, legal, security, and employee-relations guardrails.

## 7. Remote work, flexible time, and offices

### Remote work

Remote work should be supported as a complete operating model, including:

- Approved work jurisdictions and cross-border controls
- Suitable equipment and assistive technology
- Connectivity, power reliability, and technical support
- Home-working safety and ergonomics
- Confidentiality and physical privacy
- Alternative workspaces where home working is unsuitable
- Clear working-time and disconnection expectations
- Onboarding, mentoring, learning, and social connection
- Business continuity if connectivity fails
- Transparent treatment of equipment, connectivity, workspace, utility, and travel costs

The company should not assume every employee has a quiet room, reliable broadband, suitable furniture, or affordable connectivity. Remote work should not simply transfer unreasonable costs and risks to employees.

### Flexible time

Location and time flexibility should be designed separately. Options may include:

- Flexible start and finish times
- Core collaboration windows
- Predictable rosters
- Shift swaps
- Part-time work
- Job sharing
- Compressed schedules where lawful
- Temporary schedule adjustments

For global teams:

- Use asynchronous documentation for routine information
- Set reasonable collaboration windows
- Rotate inconvenient meeting times
- Avoid treating headquarters’ time zone as the default
- Establish handoffs and escalation coverage
- Monitor meeting burden by region
- Protect local working-time and rest requirements

### Offices

Offices should be **purposeful destinations**, not default locations for individual work that can be done effectively elsewhere.

Appropriate purposes include:

- Complex collaboration and problem-solving
- Customer engagement
- Innovation
- Learning, coaching, and apprenticeship
- Community and relationship-building
- Secure or specialized work
- Operational resilience
- Wellbeing and connection

Portfolio decisions should consider:

- Workforce demand and role mix
- Utilization by purpose, day, time, and employee group
- Peak demand and space availability
- Customer and strategic importance
- Commuting time, cost, and safety
- Talent-market access
- Accessibility and facility quality
- Resilience and business continuity
- Lease flexibility and total cost
- Environmental impact
- Whether the site supports employees without suitable remote alternatives

Badge and booking data should inform planning but should not measure individual productivity, commitment, or career potential.

Office design should include:

- Collaboration and hybrid meeting rooms
- Focus spaces
- Learning and coaching areas
- Social and community spaces
- Secure work areas
- Accessible workstations and routes
- Quiet and sensory-considerate areas
- Nursing, prayer, rest, and wellness spaces where appropriate
- Reliable connectivity and simple room technology
- Adequate space for site-based and operational employees

## 8. Collaboration technology and digital workplace

Provide a coherent, interoperable toolset for:

- Messaging and video collaboration
- Shared documents and knowledge management
- Project and workflow management
- Searchable decisions and records
- Digital whiteboarding
- Secure identity and access management
- Managed devices and endpoint protection
- Translation, captions, transcription, and accessibility
- Reliable remote access and technical support

### Collaboration standards

Teams should:

- Use meetings for work that benefits from real-time interaction
- Use asynchronous methods for routine updates and information sharing
- Record decisions, owners, deadlines, and rationale
- Define urgent channels and response expectations
- Provide accessible materials before meetings
- Ensure remote participants can speak, see materials, and influence decisions equally
- Rotate unfavorable meeting times
- Protect recordings, transcripts, and sensitive information
- Retire redundant tools

Do not use keystrokes, screenshots, mouse movement, webcam status, message volume, or online presence as standalone productivity measures.

Where automated tools influence work-model eligibility, scheduling, performance, or career decisions, require human review, explainability, bias testing, and an appeal route.

## 9. Inclusion, culture, compensation, and fairness

### Accessibility

Build accessibility into procurement, office design, onboarding, and digital standards. Support:

- Captions and transcripts
- Screen-reader and keyboard compatibility
- Accessible documents and meeting materials
- Alternative participation methods
- Accessible routes, furniture, rooms, and facilities
- Quiet and sensory-considerate spaces
- Prompt assistive-technology provision
- Confidential accommodations
- Regular accessibility testing and issue tracking

### Cultural inclusion

Validate locally rather than assuming that a country has a single workplace culture. Consider:

- Hierarchy and willingness to challenge managers
- Communication and language barriers
- Expectations about office presence
- Religious observance and public holidays
- Direct and indirect communication styles
- Informal networking and leadership access
- Different interpretations of urgency and availability

Confidential listening channels are particularly important where employees may be reluctant to challenge local leaders.

### Compensation and geographic equity

Establish transparent, locally reviewed principles for:

- Pay location and geographic differentials
- Relocation and changes in approved work location
- Comparable work across different labor markets
- Equipment, connectivity, workspace, and commuting costs
- Benefits associated with office or site attendance
- Pay and promotion implications of work arrangements

Flexibility should not be used to shift unreasonable costs to employees or create unexplained differences in treatment. Compensation decisions require local legal, tax, HR, and employee-relations review.

### Nonemployee populations

Define how the framework applies to contractors, agency workers, interns, temporary employees, outsourced teams, and other nonemployees, including:

- Eligibility and location rules
- Security and privacy controls
- Equipment and technical support
- Health and safety responsibilities
- Accessibility
- Office and collaboration access
- Training and incident reporting
- Responsibility between the company and the supplying organization

### Fairness monitoring

Monitor whether work arrangements affect:

- Performance ratings
- Pay and promotion
- Strategic assignments
- Senior-leader access
- Sponsorship and mentoring
- Training and networking
- Recognition
- Retention and internal mobility
- Approval of flexibility and accommodation requests

Use findings to address disparate impact, not to restrict flexibility for particular demographic groups.

## 10. Leadership and performance

Managers are accountable for outcomes and employee experience, not physical visibility. They should:

- Set clear goals, priorities, and decision rights
- Explain the rationale for presence requirements
- Apply classification criteria consistently
- Establish team operating agreements
- Make work and decisions visible
- Run inclusive and accessible meetings
- Manage workload, meeting burden, and recovery
- Hold regular one-to-ones and career conversations
- Provide equitable access to development and opportunities
- Assess performance using multiple, role-appropriate sources of evidence
- Respect local legal, working-time, privacy, safety, and representation requirements
- Address performance issues directly rather than through blanket attendance mandates

Performance frameworks should recognize different forms of contribution, including direct deliverables, quality, prevention, customer relationships, research, innovation, judgment, risk management, and team enablement.

Use calibration across teams and audit whether office attendance, after-hours responsiveness, or proximity to senior leaders influences ratings or advancement.

Manager training should cover distributed leadership, cross-cultural communication, accessibility, outcome-based performance, presence bias, employee data, workload, and local obligations. Leadership scorecards should include fairness, team health, talent development, and operating effectiveness.

## 11. Security, privacy, health, and safety

### Security controls

Minimum controls should include:

- Strong identity management and multifactor authentication
- Managed and patched devices
- Endpoint protection
- Encryption and secure remote access
- Least-privilege access
- Secure collaboration-tool configuration
- Data-loss prevention for sensitive information
- Clear rules for personal devices and home networks
- Physical privacy requirements for confidential work
- Secure disposal of records
- Security awareness training
- Simple, nonpunitive incident reporting
- Business-continuity and backup procedures

Controls should be proportionate to the sensitivity of the work and should not unnecessarily exclude employees who need accessible or alternative working arrangements.

### Privacy and monitoring

Operational analytics may support cybersecurity, service management, workforce planning, workplace planning, and equity analysis. However:

- Collect only necessary data for defined purposes
- Prefer aggregated or de-identified information
- Publish what is collected, why, by whom, and for how long
- Restrict access and maintain audit trails
- Do not use badge data, keystrokes, screenshots, mouse movement, webcam status, message volume, or online presence as standalone productivity measures
- Conduct privacy and employee-impact assessments for monitoring tools
- Review for disparate impact across roles, time zones, disabilities, and caregiving circumstances

### Health, safety, and wellbeing

Local implementation should address:

- Ergonomics and workstation setup
- Electrical and fire safety
- Safe handling of equipment and confidential materials
- Working-time limits and rest periods
- Psychosocial risks, isolation, stress, and burnout
- Domestic safety and privacy concerns
- Travel and fatigue for site-based employees
- Safe office occupancy and emergency procedures
- Incident and work-related injury reporting
- Occupational-health support

The company should provide transparent, locally compliant principles for equipment, connectivity, workspace, utilities, travel, and coworking support.

## 12. Governance and employee consultation

### Global Future-of-Work Council

Include executive leadership, HR, business operations, technology, security, workplace, finance, legal, tax, privacy, compliance, inclusion and accessibility, regional leaders, and employee-representation stakeholders where appropriate.

Responsibilities:

- Set global principles and minimum safeguards
- Approve the assessment method
- Establish risk appetite and exception categories
- Align people, workplace, technology, and financial plans
- Review outcomes and disparities
- Sponsor pilots
- Resolve global-local conflicts
- Approve material policy, property, and technology changes

### Country and regional forums

Responsibilities:

- Conduct legal, tax, immigration, privacy, safety, cultural, and representation reviews
- Develop country playbooks
- Consult employees and representatives
- Approve local variations
- Monitor local outcomes and risks

Consultation should explain the problem, evidence, options, expected impacts, safeguards, and how feedback affected the design. Where formal employee representation exists, local counsel should confirm whether information, consultation, negotiation, or prior agreement is required.

## 13. Measurement framework

No single measure—especially attendance, utilization, engagement, or “productivity”—should determine success.

### Enterprise measures

Track a limited set of common indicators across the company:

- Customer satisfaction and service levels
- Quality, errors, rework, and safety
- Delivery predictability and cycle time
- Retention and regrettable attrition
- Engagement, belonging, and trust
- Workload and meeting burden
- Promotion and pay progression
- Technology reliability
- Security incidents and policy exceptions
- Office utilization by purpose and peak demand

### Function- and country-specific measures

Add measures appropriate to the work and local context, such as:

- Function-specific productivity or financial outcomes
- Innovation and time-to-market
- Shift coverage and service continuity
- Accommodation resolution time
- Strategic-assignment access
- Time-zone meeting burden
- Commuting and employee-borne work costs
- Accessibility defects
- Environmental impact

For every metric, define:

- Owner
- Baseline
- Target or tolerance range
- Review cadence
- Data source and limitations
- Privacy and retention rules
- Escalation threshold
- Corrective action

Use aggregated or de-identified data wherever possible. Protect small populations, test survey comparability across languages and cultures, and combine quantitative results with local qualitative evidence.

Environmental comparisons should use a consistent methodology covering office energy, commuting, business travel, home-working energy, equipment, data use, and alternative workspaces. Reduced office attendance should not automatically be treated as reduced environmental impact.

## 14. Implementation roadmap

### Phase 1: Diagnose

- Map activities, roles, countries, offices, tools, employment types, and representatives
- Identify legal, tax, immigration, privacy, safety, security, and infrastructure constraints
- Gather workforce, commuting, connectivity, office, technology, cost, and employee-experience data
- Conduct representative employee and manager listening
- Identify groups at risk of disadvantage
- Establish business, workforce, workplace, environmental, and security baselines

### Phase 2: Design

- Define global principles and minimum safeguards
- Create role-assessment templates and decision rights
- Establish request, accommodation, appeal, and cross-border processes
- Define team operating standards
- Set office, technology, security, privacy, accessibility, compensation, and cost principles
- Develop country playbooks
- Agree metric owners, baselines, targets or tolerance ranges, and escalation thresholds

### Phase 3: Consult and pilot

Pilot across varied:

- Countries and legal environments
- Role types and work archetypes
- Office and connectivity conditions
- Single-country and multi-time-zone teams
- New and experienced employees
- Manager capability levels
- Accessibility and caregiving circumstances, using confidential and voluntary participation
- Employee and nonemployee populations where relevant

Test failure modes such as meeting overload, poor onboarding, office crowding, promotion bias, security exceptions, inconsistent manager decisions, employee cost transfer, reduced leadership access, and disadvantage to site-based employees.

Use baseline comparisons and, where practical, phased or matched comparison groups. Do not generalize results across countries unless underlying conditions are comparable.

### Phase 4: Scale

- Complete local legal and representation reviews
- Train managers and employees
- Upgrade collaboration tools and accessibility
- Redesign offices
- Integrate work arrangements into hiring, onboarding, performance, promotion, payroll, property, workforce planning, and security
- Retire redundant policies and tools
- Publish guidance, contacts, timelines, and appeal routes

### Phase 5: Review and improve

Review at least annually, and sooner after:

- Material business changes
- New legal requirements
- Security or safety incidents
- Major office or technology changes
- Evidence of inequitable career outcomes
- Significant employee-experience disparities
- Failure to achieve customer or operational outcomes

---

## Conclusion

The sustainable choice is neither universally “office first” nor “remote first.” It is **work-design first**:

- Global in principles
- Local in implementation
- Activity-based in role decisions
- Flexible where feasible
- Explicit about coordination and boundaries
- Inclusive and accessible by default
- Secure and legally reviewed
- Fair to both remote-capable and site-based employees
- Measured by business outcomes, employee experience, and equity rather than visibility

This should be implemented as an operating-model change spanning people, technology, workplaces, leadership, governance, compensation, employee representation, and local legal review—not as a standalone attendance policy.
