# Operations Improvement Plan  
## Reduce Quoted Lead Time Without Compromising Certified Quality

## Executive recommendation

The company should not replace its 14-week promise with a universal six-week commitment. The competitor’s quote is a benchmark, not proof of equivalent configuration, certification scope, customer assumptions, delivery point, or reliability.

Adopt a staged, evidence-based program:

- **Days 0–30:** establish where the 14 weeks are actually lost, contain warranty risk, and stop unsupported promises.
- **Days 31–90:** pilot improvements on one repeatable standard product family.
- **After sufficient completed-order evidence:** publish a shorter promise only when delivery, quality, certification, capacity, cost, and commercial gates are met.
- **Within 6–9 months:** target 8–9 weeks for selected standard configurations.
- **Within 9–18 months:** assess six-week delivery only for configurations that demonstrate the required capability at the required service level.

The reduction must come primarily from queues, shortages, batching, handoffs, late engineering changes, rework, and release delays—not from removing certified inspections, shortening required tests, or weakening traceability.

---

## 1. Shared objective and non-negotiable rules

### Shared objective

> Deliver the correct, fully certified and traceable product to the customer by the confirmed date, at an acceptable total cost, with no unacceptable deterioration in first-pass quality, field reliability, or warranty performance.

### Decision rules

- **Sales** cannot promise dates unsupported by configuration, material, capacity, certification, and logistics evidence.
- **Production** cannot bypass quality gates or release unready work to achieve schedule.
- **Quality** retains authority over mandatory inspections, testing, certification, traceability, and release.
- **Engineering** controls configuration and product/process changes.
- **Supply Chain** cannot use unqualified suppliers, materials, substitutions, or undocumented expedites.
- **Finance** evaluates inventory, overtime, capacity, recovered margin, and warranty exposure together.
- **Service/Warranty** provides early field-failure signals and containment requirements.
- No lead-time improvement is credited if it merely shifts delay or risk to inspection, suppliers, documentation, customers, or warranty service.

Quality may stop shipment for a conformance, safety, certification, or traceability issue. Production may stop unsafe or unstable work. Neither authority may be overridden for commercial reasons.

## 2. Common lead-time clocks

Every target and quotation must state the applicable clock, endpoint, and customer assumptions.

| Clock | Start | End | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quote response time | Quote request received | Quote issued | Sales responsiveness |
| Commercial lead time | Accepted order | Confirmed customer delivery | Customer promise |
| Operational lead time | Configuration freeze or production release | Shipment | Internal flow |
| Logistics lead time | Shipment | Customer receipt | Delivery execution |
| Promise performance | Confirmed customer date | Actual customer receipt | Reliability |

Also specify:

- Calendar versus working weeks
- Whether certification documents are included
- Required customer inputs and due dates
- How approved customer-caused delays or changes affect the promise
- Whether the promise is to the plant, dock, site, or commissioning point

## 3. Known facts, hypotheses, and required measurements

### Known facts

- The current quoted lead time is 14 weeks.
- The fastest competitor quotes six weeks.
- Sales reports losing approximately one in three deals primarily on delivery speed.
- The product requires certified inspections at multiple stages.
- A previous compression effort caused a warranty-claim increase that took two years to recover from.

### Working hypotheses—not yet conclusions

The 14 weeks may include substantial:

- Queue and resource waiting
- Supplier and material delays
- Engineering clarification and late changes
- Batch scheduling and setup delays
- Inspection and testing queues
- Rework and repeat testing
- Documentation and final-release delays
- Commercial quoting or approval delays

The prior warranty increase may have resulted from rushed work, weakened controls, unqualified capacity, poor change validation, or defect escape. The specific causes must be confirmed.

### Measurements required within the first 30 days

Collect order-level timestamps for a representative sample of:

- Standard, configured, and engineered-to-order products
- On-time and late orders
- Shortage-affected and rework-affected orders
- Major product families and suppliers

Required events include:

- Quote request, clarification, quote issue, acceptance, and order entry
- Technical review, configuration freeze, engineering release, and engineering changes
- Purchase order, supplier promise, actual receipt, shortage, and incoming inspection
- Production release, material readiness, operation queues, starts, completions, and changeovers
- Inspection request, queue, completion, nonconformance, rework, and reinspection
- Test queue, completion, failure, and retest
- Certification-package completion, final release, shipment, and customer receipt
- Promise-date changes, schedule changes, expedites, and reasons

Missing timestamps are data-quality defects, not zero elapsed time. Pilot data completeness must be at least 98%.

## 4. Diagnose where the 14 weeks are lost

### Lead-time-loss waterfall

Reconcile every sampled order into:

1. Active production
2. Required inspection and testing
3. Queue and resource waiting
4. Batching and setup
5. Material and supplier delay
6. Engineering and approval delay
7. Rework, retest, and nonconformance
8. Documentation and logistics
9. Customer-dependent delay

Required inspection and testing are not waste. Separate necessary quality activity from avoidable inspection queues, incomplete requests, repeat inspections, and certification-document delays.

### Day-30 analyses

Produce:

- Pareto of lost days by cause and process
- Critical-path analysis for each sampled order
- Queue and utilization analysis for production, engineering, inspection, and testing
- Supplier receipt-date, shortage, documentation, and incoming-quality analysis
- First-pass yield and rolled-throughput yield
- Rework, test-failure, and nonconformance analysis
- Late engineering-change analysis
- Warranty linkage by product, revision, supplier, process, and shipment cohort
- Median, 80th, and 90th percentile lead time by product family
- Quote response time, clarification rate, quote-to-order conversion, and delivery-related lost-deal rate

Only losses that consume critical-path time or create material quality, warranty, cost, or customer risk should be prioritized.

## 5. Immediate containment: days 0–14

### Protect certified quality and warranty performance

Immediately prohibit:

- Removing mandatory inspections, tests, hold points, certification records, or traceability
- Shortening required tests without approved technical and regulatory evidence
- Shipping with open critical nonconformances
- Using unapproved materials, suppliers, substitutions, or personnel
- Informal verbal dispositions
- Reclassifying defects as “noncritical” because an order is late

Review the previous warranty increase by failure mode, product, revision, supplier, process, shipment cohort, and compression practice.

### Certification matrix

Before the pilot, create a controlled matrix for the pilot product family identifying:

- Mandatory inspections and tests
- Applicable standards, contracts, and regulations
- Hold points and release authority
- Personnel competence requirements
- Equipment and calibration requirements
- Record-retention and traceability requirements
- Internal, customer, regulator, registrar, or notified-body approvals required for changes

No internal Quality approval substitutes for required external approval.

### Promise containment

Until capability is demonstrated:

- Retain the 14-week promise for unvalidated products.
- Require joint Sales–Operations approval for shorter or nonstandard commitments.
- Record the reason, material check, capacity check, and approver for every override.
- State customer dependencies for technical information, approvals, configuration freeze, and requested changes.
- Validate the competitor’s six-week quote for equivalent scope before using it as an operating target.

### At-risk order review

Run a daily review of orders with:

- Material shortages
- Engineering or customer-information holds
- Quality holds
- Inspection or test queues
- Missed operations
- Promise-date risk

Each issue receives one accountable owner, one next action, one due date, and one escalation trigger.

## 6. Prioritized improvement portfolio

Score each action jointly on critical-path impact, frequency, risk reduction, feasibility, effort, and time to benefit. Re-rank monthly.

### Priority 1: Immediate, low-cost actions

1. Standardize order-entry and configuration-completeness checks.
2. Assign one owner for order handoff.
3. Run commercial, engineering, supply, and quality reviews concurrently where appropriate.
4. Establish configuration freeze and controlled change procedures.
5. Release work only when material, labor, tooling, instructions, and inspection/test capacity are ready.
6. Use finite-capacity scheduling across production, engineering, inspection, and testing.
7. Pre-book inspection and test capacity.
8. Complete inspection and certification packages before final release.
9. Freeze the near-term schedule and define emergency insertion rules.
10. Use one order-status view with reason-coded delays.
11. Standardize work instructions, routings, inspection plans, and test packages.
12. Move valid in-process checks earlier while retaining mandatory final verification.
13. Link nonconformance, rework, supplier, warranty, and promise data.

### Priority 2: Conditional actions requiring a business case

- Common-component safety stock
- Supplier-held inventory
- Blanket orders and scheduled releases
- Supplier capacity reservations
- Qualified overtime or additional shifts
- Additional inspectors or testers
- Fixtures or duplicate test capability
- Alternate suppliers
- Approved outsourcing
- Postponed common modules

No Priority 2 action proceeds without documented critical-path benefit, demand basis, quality controls, financial case, and exit criteria.

## 7. Quality-preserving process design

### Inspection and testing

Reduce delay by improving flow through quality resources:

- Pre-book inspection and test slots.
- Add qualified capacity only where analysis confirms a bottleneck.
- Improve completeness of inspection requests.
- Use standard, revision-controlled inspection packages.
- Digitize records and signatures only where permitted.
- Use documented disposition for properly classified issues.
- Move checks earlier only after validating acceptance criteria, sampling, measurement systems, equipment, competence, and records.

For every changed check, document whether it is added, relocated, or substituted. An in-process check cannot replace a mandatory final inspection unless formally authorized.

### Nonconformance control

All dispositions must follow the controlled nonconformance procedure and require:

- Authorized disposition personnel
- Engineering review where product or design intent is affected
- Quality approval for use-as-is, repair, or rework
- Affected-unit and lot traceability
- Reinspection or verification where required
- Recurrence analysis
- Corrective-action assessment

A nonconformance may not be reclassified because an order is late.

### Change classification

Classify changes as:

- **Class A:** Administrative or visual-management changes with no product/process effect
- **Class B:** Scheduling or flow changes with limited technical risk
- **Class C:** Changes to routing, tooling, inspection, testing, supplier, material, or configuration
- **Class D:** Changes affecting certified characteristics, regulatory compliance, or design intent

Classes C and D require formal validation, controlled documentation, and external approval where applicable.

### Warranty escalation

Create a severity-based playbook defining:

- Trigger thresholds
- Containment actions
- Shipment-hold authority
- Affected serial numbers, lots, revisions, and cohorts
- Customer and service communication
- Regulatory-reporting assessment
- Root-cause and corrective-action deadlines
- Criteria for resuming production, shipment, and scale-up

Leading indicators—including critical escapes, repeat nonconformances, test failures, supplier-lot failures, and documentation defects—must trigger action before warranty claims accumulate.

## 8. Inventory, supplier, and capacity controls

### Inventory

For every proposed stocked item, document:

- Demand and supplier lead-time distributions
- Desired service level
- Reorder point and safety-stock method
- Shortage probability and duration
- Critical-path exposure
- Safety and warranty criticality
- Obsolescence and cash exposure
- Shelf-life, storage, lot, revision, and traceability controls
- Review frequency and exit trigger

Supplier-held inventory must define ownership, title, inspection responsibility, lot control, access during supplier distress, and obsolete-stock disposition.

### Supplier qualification

A new supplier or substitute follows:

1. Technical specification review
2. Quality-system and capability assessment
3. Formal approval
4. First article or equivalent validation
5. Certificate and traceability verification
6. Enhanced initial incoming inspection
7. Controlled pilot use
8. Shipment-cohort and warranty monitoring
9. Reduction of controls only after approved evidence

Supplier promise-support thresholds must be differentiated by criticality and volume. As an initial standard, a critical supplier should demonstrate representative receipt history, at least 95% on-time receipt to the required date, at least 98% certificate completeness, no unresolved critical corrective action, and an approved incoming-quality rate. Quality and Procurement must adjust these thresholds for low-volume, sole-source, safety-critical, and warranty-critical parts.

### Capacity

Before adding labor, shifts, overtime, equipment, fixtures, testers, inspectors, or outsourcing, quantify:

- Demand and variability by product family
- Hours required by operation
- Qualified labor availability
- Equipment uptime and effective capacity
- Setup and changeover time
- Engineering, inspection, testing, documentation, and logistics capacity
- Training, absence, maintenance, and calibration allowances
- Required service-level buffer
- Capital, labor, and operating cost
- Payback under conservative demand

Production capacity is not added unless the demonstrated constraint is on the critical path and downstream resources can absorb the output.

### Fatigue controls

Before overtime is authorized, establish:

- Maximum weekly and daily hours
- Maximum consecutive shifts
- Minimum rest period
- Restrictions for certified and safety-critical work
- Required supervision
- Competence verification
- Fatigue reporting and stop-work criteria
- Quality review by shift and overtime exposure

## 9. Quote-process and promise-policy workstream

**Owner:** Sales Operations, supported by Engineering, Operations, Supply Chain, Quality, Finance, and Service.

Implement:

- Standard configuration library
- Preapproved routings, inspection plans, tests, and certification packages
- Quote-completeness checklist
- Capacity and critical-material checks before promise issuance
- Response-time service levels by product type
- Technical clarification tracking
- Quote-to-order conversion tracking
- Audited lost-deal coding

### Initial commercial objectives

Subject to baseline validation:

- Reduce opportunities lost primarily to delivery from approximately one in three to no more than one in four within six months and one in five within 12 months.
- Improve quote response time by 30% within six months without reducing quote accuracy.
- Improve quote-to-order conversion for validated short-lead-time products by at least 10% versus baseline.
- Track recovered revenue and contribution margin monthly.

Sales will code losses by delivery, price, technical fit, competitor relationship, or other cause. Disputed classifications are jointly reviewed by Sales and Operations.

### Segmented promise model

| Product type | Promise approach |
|---|---|
| Standard | Published family promise based on demonstrated percentile performance |
| Configured | Includes option-specific material, engineering, and testing requirements |
| Engineered-to-order | Individual technical and capacity review |
| Expedite | Separate approved service with capacity, price, and quality review |

## 10. Implementation roadmap

### Phase 0 — Mobilize and contain: weeks 0–2

Deliver:

- Executive sponsor and program manager
- Pilot product family
- Common definitions and data dictionary
- Certification matrix
- At-risk order review
- Quality non-negotiables
- Warranty-risk review
- Initial RACI, service levels, and escalation rules
- Program budget and staffing plan

**Gate:** No broad quote reduction or major inventory/capacity commitment.

### Phase 1 — Diagnose: weeks 2–6

Deliver:

- Lead-time waterfall
- Critical-path Pareto
- Bottleneck and inspection-capacity analysis
- Supplier recovery plan
- Standard order-entry process
- Shared order-status view
- Quantified addressable loss
- Pilot target and business case

**Gate:** Every proposed action must be linked to measured critical-path loss or defined quality/cost risk.

### Phase 2 — Controlled pilot: weeks 6–12

Pilot one repeatable standard family using:

- Configuration completeness and freeze
- Readiness-based release
- Finite-capacity scheduling
- Near-term schedule freeze
- Pre-booked inspection and test capacity
- Standard inspection and certification packages
- Reduced batching where justified
- Validated early checks
- Controlled common-component availability
- Supplier recovery actions
- Daily at-risk order management

### Phase 3 — Validate and decide

Before launch, define the evidence requirement. As an initial standard:

- At least 20 completed pilot orders, or a statistically justified alternative for low-volume products
- Representative configuration and option mix
- At least eight stabilized weeks of operating data
- Data completeness of at least 98%
- No exclusion of late, shortage-affected, or reworked orders except documented customer-dependent intervals
- Evaluation at the 80th and 90th percentiles
- Confidence intervals or an approved non-inferiority method

If sufficient orders have not completed by day 90, continue the pilot and retain the existing promise. Calendar timing cannot substitute for evidence.

### Phase 4 — Scale: months 4–9

Scale only validated changes. Introduce common modules, supplier commitments, qualified capacity, and digital controls where justified.

**Target:** 8–9 weeks for selected standard configurations only after the promise-validation gate is met.

### Phase 5 — Assess six-week capability: months 9–18

Consider six weeks only where demonstrated performance, material availability, qualified capacity, inspection/testing throughput, certification cycle time, supplier reliability, customer conditions, and warranty evidence support it.

## 11. Promise-validation gate

Before publishing a shorter promise:

1. Exact configurations, customer assumptions, and certification scope are defined.
2. The pilot contains the approved minimum volume and representative order mix.
3. Data completeness is at least 98%.
4. The proposed promise is supported at the approved 80th or 90th percentile, not merely the median.
5. Performance is stable for the defined observation period.
6. On-time customer receipt meets the product-family service target.
7. Critical materials and constrained resources are available.
8. Engineering, production, inspection, testing, documentation, and logistics capacity are confirmed.
9. FPY, rework, test failures, critical defects, escapes, and traceability meet explicit limits.
10. Supplier performance meets promise-support thresholds.
11. Customer dependencies are explicit in the quote.
12. Overtime, inventory, expedite, and warranty exposure are within the approved business case.
13. Rollback criteria and authority are documented.
14. Sales, Operations, Quality, Engineering, Supply Chain, Finance, and Service approve the change.

A six-week median is not sufficient to support a six-week promise if the 80th or 90th percentile is materially longer.

**Chair:** COO or General Manager  
**Members:** Operations, Quality, Sales, Engineering, Supply Chain, Finance, Service/Warranty

| Dimension | Measure | Initial control |
|---|---|---|
| Customer delivery | Actual customer receipt versus confirmed promise | Product-family target based on customer and contract requirements |
| Flow | Median, 80th, and 90th percentile lead time | Improve by segment |
| Critical-path loss | Days by cause | Reduce top causes monthly |
| Quote process | Response time, clarification rate, quote accuracy | Improve speed without reducing accuracy |
| Commercial result | Delivery-related loss rate, win rate, recovered revenue/margin | Toward ≤20% delivery-related loss within 12 months, subject to baseline validation |

| Rework | Hours per unit | Declining |
| Testing | Failures and retests | Within approved control limits |
| Customer quality | Escapes, serious field failures, warranty claims | No unacceptable increase |
| Supply | Required-date delivery and incoming acceptance | Improving for critical suppliers |
| Stability | Schedule changes inside frozen window | Reduce versus baseline |
| Inspection | Inspection queue time | Reduce without reducing inspection content |
| Cost | Expedite, overtime, inventory, cost of poor quality | Within approved business case |
| Certification | Records and traceability | 100% |
| Pilot data | Timestamp/status completeness | At least 98% |

Quality limits must be severity-specific. Critical escapes may require zero tolerance; traceability and certification completeness must remain 100%.

## 15. Feedback, stop, and rollback rules

- **Daily:** review at-risk orders and assign actions.
- **Weekly:** review critical-path losses, queues, suppliers, capacity, FPY, rework, inspection, and promise risk.
- **Biweekly:** steering committee reviews pilot evidence, costs, and warranty signals.
- **Monthly:** refresh product-family promise performance using percentiles.
- **By shipment cohort:** monitor early field and warranty indicators.
- **After each major change:** compare actual benefit with predicted benefit and update controls.

Pause the pilot or scale-up immediately if there is:

- A critical or safety-related escape
- A traceability or certification-record failure
- A warranty or field-failure threshold breach
- A test-failure or repeat-nonconformance limit breach
- An unapproved material, supplier, or substitution change
- Data completeness below 98% for the decision period
- Promise performance below the approved threshold
- A breach of overtime or fatigue controls

Contain affected product, investigate, notify customers or regulators where required, and revert to the last validated process where necessary.

---

## Definition of success

The program succeeds when the company can demonstrate, order by order:

1. How the original 14 weeks were divided.
2. Which losses were on the critical path.
3. Which actions removed those losses.
4. That shorter customer-receipt promises are achieved reliably at the approved service percentile.
5. That certified inspections, required tests, traceability, documentation, and release controls remain intact.
6. That first-pass quality, field reliability, warranty performance, supplier quality, and total cost remain within approved limits.
7. That delivery-related lost opportunities and recovered margin improve measurably.

Speed is successful only when it is repeatable, customer-relevant, certified, reliable, and economically sustainable.
