# Recommendation: Develop **Kome Break Crisps** for Japan  
*Working name only; Japanese naming, trademark, regulatory, and consumer testing required.*

## Market rationale

Japan’s savory-snack market is mature and highly competitive, with strong familiarity with senbei, okaki, arare, potato snacks, corn snacks, and grain products. Japanese flavors such as soy sauce, sesame, seaweed, miso, and ume are therefore credible entry points—but not sufficient differentiation on their own.

The recommended opportunity is a **rice-led, portioned crisp for individual work, tea, study, and short home breaks**, supported by a clean, controlled eating experience. Low crumb, limited seasoning transfer, moderate aroma, and a distinctive bite should remain development hypotheses until validated locally.

Launch first through major urban convenience stores, followed by selected supermarkets if the product demonstrates repeat purchase and sound economics.

## Recommended product platform

Thin, lightly aerated rice crisps with:

- A clean, controlled fracture
- Limited crumbs and loose seasoning
- Moderate aroma
- Balanced salt, umami, acidity, and sweetness
- A dry, shelf-stable format
- No wet fillings or high-moisture inclusions

Use one common rice-led base across the launch range. Evaluate sheet-and-cut, extrusion, expansion, or hybrid processes based on texture, breakage, yield, cost, seasoning adhesion, and shelf stability—not simply on the appeal of a “baked” claim.

### Seasonal limited edition: **Yuzu Shichimi**

Aromatic yuzu, sesame, seaweed, and mild layered spice. Use initially as a seasonal or premium limited edition because yuzu may involve higher cost, supply variability, aroma loss, and more complex formulation. Confirm the actual yuzu ingredient, concentration, origin, stability, and supply before using related imagery or claims.

### Defer

- **Wasabi Green Pea:** Plausible but already familiar as a pairing; test as a limited edition or benchmark.
- **Seafood Dashi:** Develop separately from the plant-based SKU and identify the seafood source clearly.
- **Miso Corn & Black Pepper:** Defer because it is less distinctive and risks resembling a generic Western savory snack.

## Target and occasion

**Primary target:** Urban working adults approximately 25–39 who purchase individually portioned savory snacks for work, tea or coffee breaks, and personal evening consumption.

**Secondary research groups:** Students, existing rice-snack buyers, household purchasers, premium snack buyers, and consumers interested in plant-based products.

The lead occasion should be treated as a hypothesis:

> **Individual work or tea breaks, with personal evening snacking as a secondary occasion.**

Study, commuting, lunchbox use, sharing, beverage pairing, gifting, and travel retail should be explored rather than assumed.

## Positioning

> **A contemporary Japanese rice crisp with a clean bite and carefully balanced savory flavors for everyday personal breaks.**

Position the range as an accessible-premium everyday snack—not a health food, luxury gift, or traditional regional specialty.

The most important proof points are:

- A demonstrably appealing texture
- Controlled crumbs and seasoning transfer, if consumers value them
- Clear flavor contrast across SKUs
- Convenient individual portions
- Transparent allergen and dietary information
- Seasonal discovery through limited editions

Avoid using “healthy,” “light,” “guilt-free,” “authentic,” “traditional,” “regional,” “Made in Japan,” or “Japanese quality” without substantiation.

## Packaging guidance

### Convenience-store launch

Test individual packs in two ranges:

- **18–25 g:** Trial and short-break use
- **25–35 g:** Greater perceived value and longer individual consumption

### Supermarket follow-on

Test **60–90 g resealable pouches** for home consumption and repeat purchase. Preserve crispness after repeated opening under realistic humidity conditions.

### Assortment pack

A four- or six-pack of individually wrapped portions may support e-commerce, trial, household use, and seasonal merchandising.

### Design direction

Use contemporary, ingredient-led packaging with:

- Clear Japanese flavor names and descriptors
- Flavor-specific color coding
- Realistic ingredient imagery
- Texture cues
- Prominent pack size
- Simple information hierarchy
- Japanese copy as the primary communication language

Avoid generic cultural shorthand such as Mount Fuji, cranes, fans, brush lettering, or traditional wave patterns unless there is a deliberate and relevant product connection. Test Japanese brand-name alternatives, including the current working name, for naturalness, pronunciation, shelf readability, meaning, and trademark availability.

Select packaging by balancing shelf life, breakage protection, resealability, material weight, cost, perceived waste, and compatibility with Japanese recycling systems. Make no environmental claim until the final structure is verified.

## Key risks and assumptions

- Low crumb, low seasoning transfer, and moderate aroma may not be meaningful purchase drivers.
- The product may still be perceived as another premium rice cracker.
- Shoyu Goma, Ume Goma, and Miso Negi may resemble existing Japanese flavor profiles.
- Convenience-store trial may not translate into supermarket repeat purchase.
- The 25–39 target is a working hypothesis, not validated evidence.
- A premium price will require demonstrable value beyond Japanese flavor naming.
- Domestic manufacture in Japan remains an assumption affecting cost, capacity, logistics, labeling, and origin communication.
- Vegan, vegetarian, gluten-free, halal, “baked,” yuzu, and regional claims require separate verification.
- Rice, seasoning carriers, miso, seaweed, sesame, and specialty ingredients require traceability and approved substitution plans.
- Packaging and flavor names may feel externally imposed if not reviewed by native Japanese specialists.

## Recommended validation steps

1. **Japanese-language qualitative research**  
   Test perceptions of novelty versus senbei, the relevance of the work/tea-break occasion, preferred textures, pack sizes, names, price expectations, and packaging authenticity.

2. **Blind sensory benchmarking**  
   Compare every SKU with relevant Japanese rice snacks and modern grain snacks on crispness, fracture, crumbs, seasoning transfer, aroma, salt, umami, acidity, overall liking, and repeat-purchase intent.

3. **In-use testing**  
   Simulate work, study, tea, and home occasions. Measure crumbs, powder transfer, opening convenience, aroma acceptability, eating noise, portion satisfaction, and crispness after opening.

4. **Naming, packaging, and price testing**  
   Test Japanese brand names, Japanese-led versus bilingual communication, pack sizes, mainstream versus premium prices, single packs versus assortments, and recyclable-material options.

5. **Technical and regulatory validation**  
   Confirm water activity, microbial stability, oxidation, shelf life, breakage, seasoning uniformity, packaging performance, allergen controls, claim substantiation, Japanese labeling, and recycling compliance. Confirm whether manufacture will occur in Japan or offshore before finalizing origin communication.

6. **Controlled retail pilot**  
   Launch the four core SKUs selectively through urban convenience stores, then expand to supermarkets only if agreed thresholds for sell-through, repeat purchase, margin, breakage, complaints, yield, crispness, and perceived differentiation are met.

## Recommendation

Proceed with development of the four-SKU range, led by **Shoyu Goma**, supported by **Kombu-Shiitake Nori**, **Ume Goma**, and **Miso Negi**. Use **Yuzu Shichimi** as a seasonal test.

Advance only if Japanese consumers confirm that the product offers a meaningful advantage over existing rice snacks—whether through a cleaner eating experience, a clearly superior texture, or both—and if the selected name, price, manufacturing model, claims, and packaging are technically and commercially viable.
