# Sustainable Tourism Development Proposal  
## A conservation-led, community-benefiting strategy for international visitors

**Prepared for:** Government tourism agency and relevant public authorities  
**Planning horizon:** 10 years, supported by a detailed five-year implementation program  
**Status:** Proposed for strategic endorsement in principle. Final sites, targets, budgets, legal instruments, visitor limits, and investment approvals require local research, legal review, and community validation.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The region has the potential to attract international visitors through its natural landscapes, biodiversity, historical sites, living cultural traditions, cuisine, and local hospitality. If carefully managed, tourism could support decent employment, local enterprises, conservation, heritage maintenance, and infrastructure that benefits residents as well as visitors.

Tourism growth can also create serious risks. Unmanaged development may damage ecosystems and historic sites, increase pressure on water and waste systems, raise housing costs, displace residents, commodify culture, generate insecure seasonal work, increase emissions, and concentrate profits among external investors.

This proposal therefore recommends a **managed, conservation-led tourism model**:

> **Attract the right visitors, to appropriate places, at suitable times, in ways that protect nature and heritage and increase local prosperity.**

Visitor arrivals should be monitored but should not be the main measure of success. The strategy should prioritize:

- Healthy and resilient ecosystems;
- Protected tangible and intangible heritage;
- Resident support and quality of life;
- Local ownership, decent employment, and income retention;
- Reliable, inclusive, and climate-resilient infrastructure;
- Longer stays, appropriate visitor spending, and managed geographic distribution;
- Transparent conservation and community benefits.

Implementation should proceed through four phases:

1. **Evidence, safeguards, and preparation:** Years 1–2  
2. **Pilot destinations and market testing:** Years 2–4  
3. **Selective regional scale-up:** Years 5–7  
4. **Resilience, evaluation, and renewal:** Years 8–10  

The government should initially select three to five pilot destinations only after assessing environmental sensitivity, community support, infrastructure readiness, market potential, legal feasibility, and institutional capacity. International promotion should begin with specialist and longer-stay markets after products are safe, bookable, adequately staffed, culturally authorized, and managed within agreed limits.

This proposal is not authorization for unrestricted tourism development. It is a framework for disciplined preparation and conditional implementation.

# 2. Vision and Strategic Objectives

## 2.1 Vision

**By 2035, the region will be an internationally respected, high-quality destination where protected nature, living heritage, and welcoming communities provide meaningful visitor experiences and generate fairly distributed local prosperity without exceeding ecological, cultural, social, housing, or infrastructure limits.**

## 2.2 Strategic objectives

### 1. Protect natural and cultural assets

Maintain or improve the condition of priority ecosystems, biodiversity, historic sites, cultural landscapes, and living traditions.

### 2. Manage visitation within agreed limits

Use spatial planning, site-specific thresholds, reservations, seasonal controls, infrastructure standards, and adaptive management.

### 3. Increase local participation and economic benefit

Expand local ownership, decent employment, procurement, skills, enterprise finance, income retention, and transparent benefit-sharing.

### 4. Build resilient and inclusive infrastructure

Improve water, sanitation, waste, transport, safety, digital access, accessibility, and climate resilience in ways that serve residents and visitors.

### 5. Attract appropriate international visitors

Target visitors who fit the region’s assets and capacity, stay longer, spend locally, travel responsibly, and respect environmental and cultural safeguards.

### 6. Establish accountable governance

Clarify institutional mandates, protect community rights, disclose decisions and revenues, and adjust policies when evidence shows that limits are being approached or exceeded.

## 2.3 Guiding principles

- Conservation before expansion;
- Quality and local value over volume;
- Meaningful participation and consent;
- Respect for cultural privacy and cultural property;
- Inclusion, accessibility, affordability, and public access;
- Fair labor and benefit-sharing;
- Climate responsibility and resilience;
- Evidence-based and transparent decisions;
- Tourism as a means of improving regional well-being, not an end in itself.

# 3. Assumptions and Required Research

The region’s precise geography, population, legal framework, ecological profile, heritage assets, gateways, and institutional capacity have not been specified. The recommendations below are therefore adaptable and must be localized before implementation.

The region is assumed to contain some combination of:

- Protected landscapes, forests, mountains, coastlines, wetlands, rivers, or wildlife habitats;
- Historic buildings, archaeological sites, traditional settlements, religious places, or cultural landscapes;
- Living cultural traditions, crafts, foodways, music, festivals, and local knowledge;
- Rural and small-town communities with underdeveloped tourism potential;
- Airports, ports, border crossings, or regional transport corridors;
- Existing constraints in water, sanitation, transport, waste management, housing, conservation, and governance.

## 3.1 Required baseline studies

No major tourism expansion, high-risk construction, or broad international campaign should proceed until the following work is completed or a precautionary exception is publicly justified.

### Environmental and climate

- Biodiversity, habitat, ecological-sensitivity, and connectivity mapping;
- Water availability and peak-season demand assessment;
- Waste and wastewater audits;
- Tourism emissions inventory;
- Flood, drought, wildfire, erosion, landslide, heat, storm, and other climate-risk mapping;
- Site-specific visitor-impact assessments;
- Strategic environmental assessment of the tourism strategy;
- Identification of areas where tourism is prohibited or tightly restricted.

### Cultural and heritage

- Inventory and condition assessment of tangible and intangible heritage;
- Ownership and management-responsibility review;
- Identification of sacred, private, restricted, or culturally sensitive places;
- Heritage buffer-zone and visual-impact assessment;
- Archaeological, structural, fire, security, and disaster-risk assessments;
- Community-defined protocols for access, interpretation, photography, filming, and commercial use;
- Review of applicable Indigenous, customary, religious, and community rights.

### Social and economic

- Household income, employment, wage, and business baseline;
- Resident attitudes and social-license survey;
- Gender, youth, disability, and vulnerable-group assessment;
- Local supply-chain and tourism-leakage analysis;
- Housing, rent, land-price, second-home, and short-term-rental baseline;
- Potential displacement and public-access assessment;
- Labor conditions and seasonal-employment assessment.

### Market and infrastructure

- Visitor origins, motivations, spending, seasonality, length of stay, and satisfaction;
- International route, visa, border, payment, and travel-friction analysis;
- Accommodation and tourism-business inventory;
- Transport accessibility, reliability, and travel-time assessment;
- Utility, emergency, health, waste, and wastewater capacity;
- Digital connectivity and accessibility audits;
- Competitor and source-market analysis;
- Product-readiness assessment;
- Marketing cost and expected local-economic-return assessment.

Targets and project locations should be finalized only after these studies and local consultations. Where evidence is incomplete, the government should apply the precautionary principle.

# 4. Destination Planning and Zoning

Tourism should be planned as a regional land-use, conservation, infrastructure, housing, and livelihood issue—not simply as a marketing program.

A regional tourism and conservation plan should map:

- International and domestic gateways;
- Existing tourism concentrations;
- Protected areas and ecological corridors;
- Heritage sites and buffer zones;
- Water-stressed and climate-risk areas;
- Communities suitable for tourism enterprise;
- Existing accommodation and service nodes;
- Areas where development is prohibited or tightly restricted;
- Public beaches, trails, historic spaces, cultural areas, and other access rights that must be protected.

## 4.1 Recommended zones

| Zone | Management approach |
|---|---|
| **Strict conservation zones** | No general tourism, or only research, education, conservation, or tightly controlled access |
| **Managed-visitation zones** | Permits, timed entry, guides, seasonal controls, group limits, route rotation, or temporary closure |
| **Community tourism zones** | Locally managed products under agreed cultural protocols, environmental conditions, and benefit-sharing arrangements |
| **Heritage conservation zones** | Controls on construction, signage, events, visitor flows, filming, lighting, and commercial activity |
| **Appropriate development zones** | Accommodation and visitor services concentrated in existing towns, gateways, and transport nodes |

Zoning should be incorporated into land-use, protected-area, heritage, building, water, transport, housing, and public-access decisions. Tourism promotion and investment approvals must follow zoning rather than override it.

# 5. Product Development and International Market Positioning

## 5.1 Priority tourism products

The region should develop a limited initial portfolio, selected through transparent criteria covering conservation sensitivity, community support, infrastructure readiness, market demand, local ownership potential, cost, climate exposure, and replicability.

Potential products include:

- Nature and protected-area tourism;
- Hiking, cycling, kayaking, birdwatching, and wildlife observation;
- Heritage towns, archaeological routes, and historic landscapes;
- Community-hosted rural tourism;
- Culinary, agricultural, fisheries, and craft experiences;
- Wellness and slow travel;
- Educational, scientific, and conservation-related travel;
- Accessible cultural and nature experiences;
- Multi-day itineraries linking gateways, towns, rural areas, and heritage sites.

Priority should be given to products that:

- Encourage longer stays;
- Spread spending across several communities;
- Use existing settlements and infrastructure;
- Create opportunities for local ownership;
- Can be managed within ecological and community limits;
- Complement rather than displace agriculture, fisheries, forestry, conservation, and other livelihoods.

## 5.2 Priority international segments

Subject to research and product readiness, initial promotion should focus on:

- Nature and heritage travelers;
- Hikers, cyclists, birdwatchers, photographers, and other special-interest visitors;
- Slow-travel and longer-stay travelers;
- Culinary, agricultural, and rural travelers;
- Educational and scientific groups;
- Diaspora and heritage travelers;
- Wellness travelers;
- Visitors seeking locally connected experiences;
- Accessible-tourism segments where facilities are suitable.

No segment should be considered sustainable by definition. Impacts depend on transport, accommodation, resource use, behavior, and management.

## 5.3 Ethical positioning

A suitable positioning statement is:

> **A distinctive living destination where remarkable landscapes, historic places, and local communities can be experienced responsibly and at a human scale.**

The brand should emphasize:

- Verified natural and cultural assets;
- Local hosts and locally owned businesses;
- Conservation and heritage restoration;
- Longer stays and multi-destination travel;
- Off-season opportunities;
- Shared, public, walking, and cycling transport where practical;
- Respect for local customs, privacy, and protected places.

The region should not use terms such as “untouched,” “undiscovered,” “authentic,” “regenerative,” “eco-friendly,” or “carbon-neutral” without defined criteria and supporting evidence. Communities should be represented as rights-holders and partners, not as attractions.

Higher-spending visitors should not automatically be preferred. Market value must be assessed against total environmental impact, emissions, resource consumption, and local economic benefit.

# 6. Conservation, Climate, and Resource Protection

## 6.1 Biodiversity safeguards

The region should implement:

- Biodiversity-sensitive zoning;
- Seasonal closures during breeding, nesting, spawning, migration, or fire-risk periods;
- Wildlife-distance and no-feeding rules;
- Restrictions on drones, noise, lighting, fires, and off-road vehicles;
- Biosecurity procedures for boats, footwear, livestock, and equipment;
- Invasive-species monitoring;
- Trail and habitat restoration;
- Vehicle controls and wildlife crossings where necessary;
- Cumulative-impact assessment for tourism clusters;
- Tourism-supported conservation and community stewardship programs.

Some high-value natural areas should remain non-visitor conservation zones.

## 6.2 Water, waste, and pollution standards

Before licensing, new tourism projects should demonstrate:

- Adequate water availability under peak-season conditions;
- Water-use monitoring and efficiency;
- Safe wastewater treatment and discharge;
- Waste prevention, collection, transport, and disposal;
- Reduced single-use packaging;
- Stormwater and erosion controls;
- Financing for ongoing operations and maintenance.

Phase 1 should establish minimum standards for:

- Water use per visitor-night;
- Dry-season water reserves;
- Wastewater treatment and discharge;
- Solid-waste management;
- Lighting and noise near sensitive habitats;
- Wildlife interaction;
- Construction near ecological or heritage assets.

Repeated noncompliance should result in corrective orders, fines, license suspension, or closure where authorized by law.

## 6.3 Climate resilience and emissions

The region should:

- Map climate exposure for tourism assets and communities;
- Restrict development in high-risk flood, wildfire, erosion, landslide, or water-stressed areas;
- Require climate-risk screening and stress testing for significant projects;
- Establish peak-season water budgets;
- Prepare fire, flood, heat, storm, health, and evacuation protocols;
- Use resilient materials, drainage, shade, cooling, and fire-resistant design;
- Establish early-warning and visitor-communication systems;
- Reduce transport and accommodation emissions;
- Prioritize shared transport, rail, walking, cycling, and shuttles where feasible;
- Diversify products across seasons and destinations;
- Require business-continuity planning for tourism enterprises.

Emissions should be reported separately for international aviation, domestic transport, accommodation, activities, construction, and waste. Longer stays, multi-destination travel, and lower-emission ground transport should be encouraged. Renewable energy and offsets should not be presented as sufficient responses to tourism emissions.

# 7. Visitor Management and Site Capacity

The region should not adopt one fixed visitor number. Capacity varies by season, weather, infrastructure, site condition, visitor behavior, and community tolerance.

## 7.1 Limits of Acceptable Change

For each priority destination:

1. Define ecological, heritage, visitor-experience, infrastructure, and community objectives.
2. Establish a baseline condition.
3. Select measurable indicators.
4. Set warning and critical thresholds.
5. Assign management responses and responsible authorities.
6. Monitor regularly and revise limits as conditions change.

Indicators may include:

- Wildlife disturbance;
- Trail erosion and vegetation loss;
- Heritage damage;
- Queue duration and congestion;
- Noise;
- Water consumption and dry-season availability;
- Waste accumulation;
- Emergency incidents;
- Resident complaints;
- Visitor satisfaction;
- Housing pressure;
- Public-access loss.

## 7.2 Management hierarchy

Use measures in the following order:

1. Pre-trip information and responsible-behavior guidance;
2. Spatial and seasonal dispersal;
3. Reservations, timed entry, permits, and differential pricing;
4. Guided access and group-size limits;
5. Vehicle restrictions, shuttles, and park-and-ride systems;
6. Route rotation, restoration periods, or temporary closure;
7. Physical expansion only where environmental and community conditions remain acceptable.

## 7.3 Site-level decision rules

Each pilot destination must define:

- Baseline condition;
- Warning threshold;
- Critical threshold;
- Monitoring method and frequency;
- Responsible and decision-making authorities;
- Required response;
- Review timetable;
- Public reporting requirement.

Examples include:

- Pausing new accommodation approvals when peak-season water availability falls below the agreed safety margin;
- Reducing access when ecological or heritage indicators deteriorate;
- Suspending site marketing during overcrowding, fire risk, restoration, or infrastructure failure;
- Triggering a community review when resident support falls below the agreed level;
- Redesigning or rejecting projects whose cumulative impacts exceed acceptable conditions.

Temporary exceedance caused by weather or a special event should be distinguished from persistent exceedance caused by excessive accommodation, transport, or visitor numbers.

# 8. Heritage Protection and Cultural Integrity

## 8.1 Tangible heritage

Each priority site should have:

- A condition assessment;
- A conservation and maintenance plan;
- A visitor-flow and interpretation plan;
- Structural-load and vibration limits where relevant;
- A disaster-risk plan;
- Buffer-zone and visual-impact controls;
- Rules for filming, photography, events, lighting, signage, and commercial use;
- Archaeological monitoring and anti-looting procedures;
- Fire, security, and emergency arrangements;
- Trained conservation personnel;
- A reliable maintenance budget.

Restoration should use appropriate materials, techniques, and local expertise where practicable. Tourism infrastructure must not compromise historic character, archaeological context, significant views, public access, or cultural landscapes.

## 8.2 Living and intangible heritage

Communities and authorized cultural, religious, or heritage institutions should determine which traditions are:

- Public and suitable for visitors;
- Restricted or available only under conditions;
- Sacred or private and unavailable for tourism.

The government should require:

- Appropriate authorization or consent for use of cultural knowledge, images, music, designs, stories, and ceremonies;
- Fair remuneration for cultural practitioners;
- Protection against unauthorized copying and commercial appropriation;
- The right to withdraw or modify tourism products;
- Accurate interpretation that avoids stereotyping;
- Protection of resident privacy and everyday spaces.

Where Indigenous, traditional, customary, religious, or community rights apply, the required standard may be consultation, negotiated agreement, or free, prior, and informed consent, depending on applicable law and legitimate community governance systems. The tourism authority must not determine this standard unilaterally.

Heritage conservation should not depend solely on visitor fees. Low-capacity sites may require continuing public support.

# 9. Community Participation, Ownership, and Livelihoods

## 9.1 Participation and decision rights

Establish local destination committees including:

- Residents;
- Women and youth;
- Traditional, cultural, or religious representatives where relevant;
- Local businesses and tourism workers;
- Conservation and heritage organizations;
- Local government;
- Tourism authorities;
- People with disabilities and other underrepresented groups.

Participation arrangements must specify:

- Which decisions communities may approve, reject, or amend;
- How representatives are selected;
- Minimum representation requirements;
- How minority voices are protected;
- How representatives are compensated;
- How information is provided in suitable languages and formats;
- How disagreements are resolved;
- How decisions become binding on investors and public agencies.

Consultation must begin before project selection, not after plans are substantially complete.

## 9.2 Local enterprise and ownership

Create a local-enterprise program providing:

- Grants, low-interest loans, guarantees, and revolving funds;
- Business planning, accounting, insurance, and digital support;
- Hospitality, language, guiding, food-safety, first-aid, accessibility, and conservation training;
- Affordable licensing and certification;
- Support for women-, youth-, and community-owned businesses;
- Cooperatives for accommodation, food, transport, crafts, and guiding;
- Market access through responsible tour operators and a regional platform;
- Mentoring and succession support.

Publicly supported projects should include realistic local hiring and procurement commitments.

## 9.3 Workforce quality

Tourism programs should monitor:

- Wage levels and legal compliance;
- Working hours and occupational safety;
- Seasonal insecurity;
- Worker housing and transport where relevant;
- Career progression;
- Social protection;
- Worker grievance access;
- Participation by women, youth, people with disabilities, and marginalized groups;
- Training-to-employment conversion;
- Enterprise survival.

Tourism job numbers alone should not be treated as a sufficient economic outcome.

## 9.4 Benefit-sharing and anti-capture safeguards

For tourism on or near community-managed lands or cultural assets, agreements should specify:

- Revenue-sharing formulas and payment schedules;
- Eligible recipients;
- Employment and procurement commitments;
- Conservation responsibilities;
- Access rights;
- Cultural restrictions;
- Monitoring and dispute resolution;
- Public reporting and independent audit.

Safeguards should include conflict-of-interest rules, transparent beneficiary selection, public disclosure of recipients, and appeals against allocation decisions.

## 9.5 Housing, land, and public access

Monitor:

- Rents and land prices;
- Short-term rentals;
- Second homes;
- Resident displacement;
- Land ownership concentration;
- Loss of agricultural or customary land;
- Restriction of public access to beaches, trails, historic spaces, or other public assets.

If agreed thresholds are exceeded, government should consider:

- Short-term-rental registration and caps;
- Housing and development controls;
- Protection of agricultural and customary land;
- Community land trusts or cooperative ownership;
- Anti-speculation measures;
- Public-access easements;
- Local-benefit conditions for major developments;
- Restrictions on opaque beneficial ownership.

# 10. Inclusive and Resident-Serving Infrastructure

## 10.1 Investment priorities

Initial investment should prioritize infrastructure that improves residents’ welfare and enables safe, low-impact tourism:

- Water supply and conservation;
- Wastewater treatment;
- Waste collection and reduction;
- Public and shared transport;
- Emergency and health services;
- Safe trails, roads, and public spaces;
- Digital connectivity;
- Accessible facilities and information;
- Renewable energy and efficient buildings.

Accommodation and visitor services should generally be concentrated in existing settlements and appropriate development zones.

## 10.2 Transport

The region should:

- Improve gateway-to-town connections;
- Provide reliable shuttles to sensitive sites;
- Use park-and-ride systems;
- Limit private vehicles in fragile areas;
- Develop safe walking and cycling routes;
- Integrate schedules, fares, maps, and booking information;
- Disclose travel times, accessibility, and seasonal limitations;
- Monitor emissions, congestion, and reliability.

New roads, airports, ports, parking areas, or major attractions require cumulative environmental, climate, heritage, housing, and community assessment.

## 10.3 Accessibility and affordability

Accessibility should be incorporated into procurement, licensing, building standards, and concessions through:

- Universal-design requirements;
- Accessible transport and emergency evacuation;
- Digital-accessibility standards;
- Accessible complaints channels;
- Staff training;
- Consultation with disability organizations;
- Site-specific information on terrain, surfaces, distances, toilets, and assistance.

Fees and premium positioning should not prevent residents from accessing public heritage and natural areas. Where appropriate, provide resident, student, or lower-income access arrangements and monitor local use.

Where full physical access is impossible because of site-protection requirements, provide high-quality alternative interpretation.

## 10.4 Project approval requirements

Every major project should document:

- Capital, operating, and maintenance costs;
- Funding source and budget holder;
- Water, energy, waste, and wastewater demand;
- Climate, biodiversity, heritage, housing, and public-access impacts;
- Local employment and procurement;
- Accessibility;
- Demand assumptions;
- Maintenance, adaptation, and decommissioning arrangements;
- A plan for lower-than-expected demand.

No project should be approved solely because it could increase visitor capacity.

# 11. Marketing and Communications

Marketing should be linked to product readiness, infrastructure capacity, site-management performance, and community acceptance.

## Stage 1: Prepare and test

- Register and verify tourism providers;
- Build a multilingual information and booking platform;
- Test products with domestic, regional, and specialist visitors;
- Correct service, access, safety, staffing, and information gaps;
- Establish responsible-marketing and claims-approval rules.

## Stage 2: Specialist international promotion

- Work with suitable tour operators;
- Use carefully selected media and content creators;
- Promote nature, heritage, food, walking, and community products;
- Focus on shoulder and off-season travel;
- Direct demand toward prepared secondary destinations;
- Provide clear transport, booking, safety, cultural, and environmental guidance.

## Stage 3: Broader promotion

Expand campaigns only when:

- Visitor limits are functioning;
- Water, waste, wastewater, transport, and emergency systems are adequate;
- Local enterprises can meet demand;
- Conservation and heritage conditions are stable or improving;
- Resident support remains acceptable.

Marketing claims such as “sustainable,” “community-based,” “authentic,” “regenerative,” “low-impact,” or “carbon-neutral” must have defined criteria, evidence, periodic review, and a process for withdrawal if misleading.

Campaigns should be evaluated by:

- Length of stay;
- Local spending;
- Seasonality;
- Geographic dispersal;
- Visitor satisfaction;
- Transport mode;
- Environmental and community impacts;
- Marketing cost per appropriate visitor or booking.

Reach, impressions, and arrival numbers should not be the sole performance measures.

# 12. Governance, Accountability, and Integrity

## 12.1 Governance model

Existing institutions should be strengthened wherever possible. New bodies should be created only where a clear legal or operational gap exists.

### Interagency steering group

Provides policy direction and resolves conflicts among tourism, environment, culture, transport, finance, land use, labor, housing, and local government.

### Sustainable tourism delivery authority

A designated existing agency or legally established unit should be accountable for:

- Coordinating implementation;
- Managing the approved program budget;
- Maintaining the project pipeline;
- Supporting pilot destinations;
- Preparing annual reports;
- Escalating unresolved interagency issues;
- Triggering formal reviews when thresholds are breached;
- Recommending project pauses, site closures, or marketing suspensions.

Its mandate, staffing, budget, and decision rights must be approved during Phase 1.

### Local destination committees

Coordinate local plans, community participation, visitor management, enterprise support, monitoring, and grievance handling.

### Conservation and heritage authorities

Retain authority over ecological thresholds, protected-area permits, heritage approvals, cultural safeguards, and site closures.

### Data and monitoring function

An observatory or strengthened existing unit should collect and publish visitor, environmental, heritage, economic, labor, housing, and community data.

## 12.2 Responsibility matrix

Every action should identify:

| Required field |
|---|
| Lead institution |
| Supporting partners |
| Legal authority |
| Budget holder |
| Funding source |
| Deadline |
| Indicator |
| Reporting frequency |
| Corrective action |
| Escalation route |

The matrix should be approved with the five-year program and updated annually.

## 12.3 Oversight and integrity

The program should include:

- Independent environmental, heritage, financial, social, procurement, and claims audits;
- Public disclosure of concessions and beneficial ownership;
- Conflict-of-interest rules;
- Transparent grant and investment criteria;
- Independent grievance escalation;
- Appeals against administrative and benefit-sharing decisions;
- Public reporting of projects paused, rejected, or sanctioned;
- Privacy, cybersecurity, and data-sharing rules.

# 13. Financing and Investment Discipline

A detailed financing plan must be completed during Phase 1 and approved before large-scale implementation. It should distinguish:

- Capital expenditure;
- Operating expenditure;
- Maintenance expenditure;
- Public-good investments;
- Revenue-generating investments;
- One-time grants;
- Recurring liabilities;
- Contingency reserves;
- Funding gaps.

## 13.1 Required five-year cost categories

The plan should cost:

- Baseline studies and spatial planning;
- Legal and regulatory development;
- Pilot-site conservation and heritage work;
- Water, sanitation, waste, and wastewater;
- Transport and shuttles;
- Accessibility improvements;
- Enterprise finance and workforce programs;
- Product development and marketing;
- Data and monitoring systems;
- Enforcement and inspections;
- Climate adaptation and emergency preparedness;
- Independent audits;
- Operations and maintenance.

Costs should be modeled under low-, medium-, and high-demand scenarios. Every asset should have a named owner, operating model, maintenance budget, replacement plan, and post-grant funding source.

## 13.2 Public funding

Initial public finance should prioritize:

- Conservation and heritage protection;
- Water, sanitation, waste, transport, safety, and accessibility;
- Baseline studies and planning;
- Skills and local-enterprise support;
- Monitoring, enforcement, and data systems;
- Climate adaptation and emergency preparedness.

Tourism revenues should supplement, not replace, government responsibility for public services and conservation.

## 13.3 Tourism-related revenue

Potential instruments include:

- Park and heritage entrance fees;
- Accommodation levies;
- Conservation surcharges;
- Parking and shuttle fees;
- Licensing and concession fees;
- Voluntary visitor contributions.

Before adoption, assess legal authority, collection costs, evasion risks, visitor affordability, resident access, equity among destinations, and audit arrangements.

Revenues should be transparently allocated among conservation, heritage maintenance, community benefits, visitor safety and infrastructure, monitoring, enforcement, and administration. The allocation formula should be based on documented needs rather than fixed in advance.

## 13.4 Private and external investment

Private investment incentives should be conditional on:

- Appropriate zoning;
- Environmental and heritage compliance;
- Water and energy performance;
- Local employment and procurement;
- Community participation;
- Transparent beneficial ownership;
- Publicly disclosed concession terms;
- Long-term maintenance and restoration obligations;
- Labor and accessibility compliance.

Potential external sources include climate, biodiversity, heritage, development, philanthropic, impact, diaspora, and community investment funds.

A project-preparation facility should help municipalities and small enterprises develop financeable proposals so that funding is not concentrated among large external investors.

# 14. Phased Implementation Roadmap

## Phase 1: Evidence, safeguards, and preparation — Years 1–2

### Priority actions

- Define the region, destinations, gateways, and institutional responsibilities;
- Complete environmental, climate, heritage, social, housing, infrastructure, market, and institutional baselines;
- Conduct a strategic environmental assessment;
- Map ecological, cultural, climate, water, housing, and public-access risks;
- Establish tourism and conservation zoning;
- Assess institutional and enforcement capacity;
- Select three to five pilot destinations;
- Establish community participation, consent, grievance, and benefit-sharing procedures;
- Confirm legal authority for zoning, visitor limits, fees, licensing, concessions, data, and enforcement;
- Prepare a prioritized, costed five-year capital, operating, maintenance, and contingency plan;
- Establish baseline indicators and site-level thresholds;
- Designate the accountable delivery authority;
- Complete connectivity, market, accessibility, emissions, and product-readiness assessments;
- Review or pause high-risk developments.

**Gate 1:** No major expansion or broad international promotion without minimum evidence, legal, community, infrastructure, and conservation readiness.

## Phase 2: Pilot implementation and market testing — Years 2–4

### Priority actions

- Upgrade essential water, sanitation, waste, safety, transport, signage, and accessibility systems;
- Implement conservation, heritage, and visitor-management plans;
- Launch local-enterprise finance and skills programs;
- Test fees, reservations, shuttles, timed entry, seasonal closures, and visitor codes;
- Introduce environmental, labor, accessibility, and cultural standards;
- Test selected products with domestic, regional, and specialist international markets;
- Begin targeted international promotion;
- Conduct independent environmental, cultural, social, financial, procurement, and claims reviews.

**Gate 2:** Expand only destinations meeting agreed environmental, cultural, community, infrastructure, financial, and maintenance thresholds.

## Phase 3: Selective regional scale-up — Years 5–7

- Extend successful models to additional prepared destinations;
- Improve multi-destination transport and itineraries;
- Strengthen licensing, inspection, and enforcement;
- Expand conservation and heritage finance;
- Develop regional booking and data systems;
- Improve workforce career pathways and retention;
- Apply housing and land safeguards where required;
- Broaden marketing selectively;
- Reassess demand, carrying capacity, emissions, and maintenance liabilities before each expansion decision.

## Phase 4: Resilience, evaluation, and renewal — Years 8–10

- Reassess carrying capacities and climate risks;
- Invest in adaptation and circular resource systems;
- Restore degraded sites;
- Review market mix, transport emissions, housing, labor quality, public access, and benefit distribution;
- Conduct independent resident and visitor reviews;
- Update land-use, biodiversity, heritage, housing, and tourism plans;
- Prepare the next ten-year strategy.

Annual reviews and an independent review around Year 4 or 5 should be mandatory.

# 15. Risk Mitigation

| Risk | Principal mitigation |
|---|---|
| Overcrowding | Reservations, timed entry, dispersal, differential pricing, closures |
| Water scarcity | Peak-season water budgets, efficiency standards, development pauses |
| Waste or wastewater failure | Minimum licensing standards, treatment, collection, inspections, penalties |
| Biodiversity disturbance | Zoning, seasonal closures, guide rules, wildlife-distance requirements |
| Heritage damage | Conservation plans, visitor-flow controls, maintenance, security, enforcement |
| Cultural commodification | Community protocols, consent, fair payment, restricted access |
| Unequal benefits | Local ownership, procurement, transparent benefit-sharing, audits |
| Housing displacement | Short-term-rental controls, housing monitoring, land safeguards |
| Labor exploitation | Wage standards, inspections, worker grievances, social protection |
| Climate hazards | Risk mapping, resilient design, early warning, emergency plans |
| Greenwashing | Evidence-based claims, independent verification, public reporting |
| External shocks | Diverse markets, domestic and regional demand, business-continuity support |
| Weak governance | Clear mandates, responsibility matrix, independent oversight |
| Infrastructure failure | Readiness gates, maintenance funding, demand management |
| Market dependence | Diversified source markets, products, and transport options |
| Corruption or capture | Transparent procurement, beneficial-ownership disclosure, audits, appeals |
| Loss of public access | Access mapping, legal protections, public-access conditions |
| Lower-than-expected demand | Phased investment, flexible assets, scenario planning, maintenance review |
| Data misuse | Privacy rules, anonymization, access controls, cybersecurity, audit |

# 16. Monitoring, Evaluation, and Accountability

Each indicator must have a baseline, target, threshold, methodology, reporting frequency, responsible institution, data-quality process, and management response.

## 16.1 Core regional indicators

### Environment

- Habitat and biodiversity condition;
- Wildlife disturbance and mortality incidents;
- Water availability per resident during peak season;
- Water consumption per visitor-night;
- Waste generated and recovered per visitor-night;
- Wastewater compliance;
- Tourism emissions by transport and accommodation;
- Ecologically triggered closures;
- Conservation funds collected and spent;
- Trail erosion and vegetation loss;
- Invasive-species detections.

### Heritage and culture

- Condition of priority heritage assets;
- Conservation and maintenance backlog;
- Visitor-impact incidents;
- Unauthorized alterations, damage, or archaeological violations;
- Sites with approved conservation and visitor plans;
- Cultural practitioners fairly compensated;
- Community satisfaction with cultural representation;
- Compliance with cultural protocols;
- Heritage projects completed and maintained.

### Visitors

- Average length of stay;
- Visitor spending and spending outside the principal gateway;
- Seasonal distribution;
- Visits to prepared secondary destinations;
- Visitor satisfaction and recommendation;
- Congestion and queue duration;
- Safety incidents;
- Accessibility performance;
- Shared and public transport use;
- Visitor awareness of environmental and cultural rules.

### Community and economy

- Resident support for tourism;
- Local ownership;
- Local procurement;
- Income retained locally;
- Wage levels and job quality;
- Year-round employment;
- Participation by women, youth, people with disabilities, and marginalized groups;
- Training-to-employment conversion;
- Enterprise survival;
- Community benefit payments made on time;
- Rent, land-price, and short-term-rental trends;
- Grievances received and resolved;
- Public-access incidents.

### Governance

- Priority sites with current management plans;
- Inspection and enforcement activity;
- Major projects with documented consultation and required consent;
- Participation diversity;
- Revenue reports published on time;
- Procurement and concession audits completed;
- Corrective actions completed;
- Projects paused or rejected because thresholds were exceeded;
- Data reports published with privacy safeguards.

## 16.2 Monitoring structure

The monitoring system should include:

1. A small core regional dashboard;
2. More detailed indicators for pilot destinations;
3. Annual public reporting;
4. Independent evaluation at least every three years;
5. Privacy, cybersecurity, and data-sharing protocols;
6. Public reporting of adverse trends, failed targets, and corrective actions.

Arrival numbers may be reported for planning purposes but should not be the principal success measure. Directional ambitions—such as improving length of stay, reducing resource use per visitor-night, increasing local income retention, and reducing overcrowding—should be established only after baseline validation.

# 17. Binding Safeguards and Approval Gates

The following safeguards should be incorporated into legislation, regulations, permits, management plans, or funding agreements where applicable:

1. No development in prohibited conservation or hazard zones.
2. No access to sacred or restricted sites without required authorization or consent.
3. No new accommodation without adequate peak-season water and wastewater capacity.
4. No major project without environmental, heritage, climate, housing, public-access, and community assessment.
5. No broad promotion of sites exceeding critical visitor thresholds.
6. No public support for operators with unresolved serious labor violations.
7. No concession without transparent ownership, public terms, and maintenance obligations.
8. No privatization or obstruction of protected public access.
9. No commercial use of community cultural assets without authorization and fair payment.
10. No expansion where required monitoring data are absent, unless a precautionary decision is publicly justified.
11. No public funding without transparent procurement and conflict-of-interest controls.
12. No tourism project without a funded operating and maintenance plan.

# 18. Immediate Government Decisions

Within six months, government should:

1. Endorse the conservation-led, quality-over-volume vision.
2. Define the region’s geographic and institutional scope.
3. Designate one accountable sustainable-tourism delivery authority.
4. Fund the required environmental, climate, heritage, social, housing, market, infrastructure, and institutional studies.
5. Select pilot destinations using transparent criteria.
6. Introduce stricter review of high-risk projects in sensitive areas.
7. Confirm legal authority for zoning, visitor limits, fees, licensing, concessions, data, benefit-sharing, public access, and enforcement.
8. Establish community participation, consent, grievance, appeal, and independent escalation procedures.
9. Prepare a costed five-year capital, operating, maintenance, and contingency plan.
10. Complete a market, connectivity, accessibility, emissions, and product-readiness assessment.
11. Establish a public monitoring, procurement, claims, and revenue-transparency system.
12. Approve responsible-marketing criteria and a claims-review process.
13. Begin international promotion only after readiness criteria are approved and verified.

## Conclusion

The region should not pursue tourism growth first and attempt to manage its consequences later. It should protect and measure its natural and cultural assets, prepare communities and infrastructure, test products and markets carefully, and expand only when environmental, cultural, social, housing, infrastructure, and operational thresholds are being met.

The recommended sequence is:

> **Protect first; prepare locally; pilot carefully; market selectively; scale conditionally; monitor continuously.**

International competitiveness should come from distinctive experiences, reliable services, responsible stewardship, and local participation—not uncontrolled visitor numbers. Long-term success should be judged by healthy ecosystems, protected heritage, resident support, decent livelihoods, local ownership, visitor satisfaction, transparent governance, public access, and resilience to climate and economic shocks.

This proposal is suitable for strategic endorsement and Phase 1 preparation. It should not be treated as approval for unrestricted development until the required place-specific studies, budgets, legal instruments, institutional assignments, community decisions, and site-level thresholds have been completed.
