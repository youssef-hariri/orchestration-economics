# Executive Operations Improvement Plan  
## Reduce Quoted Lead Time Without Compromising Certified Quality

## Executive summary

The company quotes 14 weeks versus approximately six weeks from its fastest competitor and reportedly loses one in three deals on delivery speed. A prior acceleration effort increased warranty claims, demonstrating that speed cannot be purchased through rushed work, reduced controls, or uncontrolled overtime.

The recommended strategy is to reduce **waiting, variability, rework, batching, shortages, and handoffs** while preserving every required certified inspection, test, hold point, traceability, approval, and release record.

The company should:

1. Establish a fact-based lead-time waterfall within 30 days.
2. Pilot flow improvements on one stable standard product family and one verified constraint.
3. Improve engineering readiness, material availability, finite-capacity scheduling, inspection coordination, WIP control, and defect prevention.
4. Segment commercial promises by product complexity.
5. Offer six-week commitments only after the relevant product family demonstrates sustained, quality-safe capability.

A credible path is to move selected families toward **10–11 weeks initially, then 8–9 weeks, and ultimately six weeks only where demonstrated performance supports the promise**.

---

## 1. Current diagnosis

### Confirmed facts

- Standard quoted lead time: **14 weeks**
- Competitor quote: **approximately six weeks**
- Sales reports delivery speed contributes to losing **approximately one-third of deals**
- Certified inspections are required at multiple stages
- The last compression effort increased warranty claims
- Production, quality, and sales disagree on the causes of delay

### Diagnosis requiring validation

The likely time losses are not the certified inspection activities themselves, but the time surrounding them:

- Incomplete orders and engineering queues
- Material shortages and supplier variability
- Production queues, excess WIP, batching, and setup delays
- Inspection scheduling and final-release waiting
- Rework, retest, and quality holds
- Frequent priority changes and uncontrolled expedites
- Administrative and documentation handoffs

Other causes—including actual processing time, capacity limitations, product complexity, supplier lead time, or commercial schedule padding—may prove more significant.

## 2. Where time is lost

Within 30 days, reconstruct at least:

- 30 standard/configured orders
- 15 late orders
- 10 custom orders
- 10 orders with rework, warranty, or significant quality history

Stratify by product family, complexity, order size, supplier exposure, customer, and demand period.

### Lead-time waterfall

Measure mutually exclusive intervals for:

1. Customer clarification and order correction
2. Quote and order administration
3. Engineering queue, design, and revision
4. Purchasing and supplier acknowledgment
5. Supplier production and delivery
6. Material shortage and incoming inspection
7. Production processing, setup, batching, and queueing
8. Transport and staging
9. Inspection waiting versus inspection execution
10. Quality holds, rework, and retest
11. Final documentation and shipment release

Concurrent blockers should be recorded separately to avoid double-counting.

Report:

- Median
- 75th percentile
- 90th percentile
- Touch time versus waiting time
- Primary delay cause and owner
- Schedule changes and expedite impact

ERP timestamps must be checked against direct observation. Commercial padding must be separated from actual process delay.

### Additional validation

- Normalize the competitor’s six-week definition for product scope, customization, inspection requirements, start/end points, and commercial exclusions.
- Validate the sales loss estimate against CRM records, won/lost order comparisons, and customer interviews.
- Audit adherence to current work instructions, inspection procedures, calibration, traceability, and nonconformance controls.
- Assign confidence levels to each conclusion: high, medium, or low.

## 3. Target state

### Product-family promise structure

| Product lane | Definition | Target promise |
|---|---|---:|
| Standard | Approved configuration, common materials, repeatable routing and inspection | Six weeks only after demonstrated capability |
| Configured | Controlled options, minor engineering variation, or special testing | Typically 7–9 weeks |
| Custom/high-risk | Significant engineering, unique materials, validation, or supplier exposure | Milestone-based quote |

Use three visible clocks:

1. Customer request to shipment availability
2. Order receipt to commercially and technically complete order
3. Complete-order acceptance to shipment availability

The third clock is the primary operations metric, but the first two remain visible so internal improvement does not conceal customer-facing delay.

### Six-week qualification criteria

A product family may receive a six-week commitment only when it demonstrates:

- Defined scope and customer assumptions
- Stable engineering content
- Approved and available materials
- Adequate production and inspection capacity
- Reliable suppliers
- Controlled WIP and schedule stability
- First-pass quality at or above baseline
- No adverse early-life warranty trend
- At least 30 qualifying orders where volume permits, or statistically justified equivalent evidence
- At least three consecutive months of qualifying performance
- At least 95% on-time delivery against the defined committed date
- Relevant 90th- or 95th-percentile performance within the quoted window

Three months of delivery performance does not establish long-term warranty equivalence; field surveillance must continue.

## 4. Prioritized interventions

### Priority 1: Establish order and engineering readiness

Create objective entry criteria for:

- Commercially complete
- Technically complete
- Engineering released
- Material ready
- Ready to start
- Production complete
- Quality released
- Available to ship

Implement:

- A standard-product fast lane using approved configurable BOMs, routings, drawings, software, inspection plans, and work instructions
- A controlled configured-product lane
- A separate custom-product lane
- Customer-change cutoff with formal schedule, cost, quality, and certification review
- Right-first-time engineering release target of at least 95%

Speed must come from standardization and queue reduction—not incomplete design review.

### Priority 2: Control materials and suppliers

Classify components by demand stability, lead time, value, obsolescence risk, certification requirements, and customer impact.

Use:

- Reorder points or Kanban for stable common components
- Forecast-backed reservations or blanket orders for long-lead critical items
- Order-triggered procurement for high-value, low-frequency items
- Controlled prebuild only for stable common modules
- Critical-item shortage escalation before production release

For critical suppliers:

- Share rolling forecasts and a frozen near-term schedule
- Track confirmed-date adherence, completeness, documentation, incoming acceptance, and variability
- Establish recovery plans
- Negotiate reserved capacity
- Qualify alternate sources where required

No supplier, source, or material substitution may be made solely by purchasing.

### Priority 3: Schedule the actual constraint

After the diagnostic identifies the bottleneck:

- Use finite-capacity scheduling
- Limit WIP before the constraint using measured buffer levels
- Freeze the next five to ten working days
- Sequence compatible products to reduce setup
- Reduce lot sizes only after analyzing setup time, quality risk, and total flow
- Protect bottleneck time from low-priority interruptions
- Coordinate material, engineering, production, inspection, and downstream capacity before release

Schedule exceptions require an approver, reason, impact assessment, and post-event review. Quality, regulatory, and safety requirements always override delivery pressure.

### Priority 4: Improve inspection flow without weakening inspection

Preserve all certified inspections, tests, hold points, acceptance criteria, qualified personnel, calibrated equipment, traceability, and release records.

Reduce waiting by:

- Providing advance notice of expected inspection demand
- Reserving qualified inspectors and equipment
- Scheduling inspection capacity with production
- Performing inspections at the earliest compliant point
- Separating inspection execution from inspection waiting
- Pre-checking documentation without replacing formal inspection
- Qualifying additional personnel where authorized
- Eliminating unnecessary inspection or documentation batching

Production releases must not exceed available qualified inspection capacity.

Any change to inspection method, timing, location, sequence, frequency, or acceptance criteria requires documented risk review, applicable customer/regulatory/certification review, validation where required, quality and engineering approval, updated controlled documents, and training.

### Priority 5: Reduce rework and defects

Use first-pass yield, rolled-throughput yield, rework, retest, final-test failures, escapes, and warranty linkage to identify the top three defect modes.

Apply:

- Root-cause analysis
- Error-proofing
- Fixture and tooling improvements
- Standard work and operator qualification
- Capability studies for critical characteristics
- Supplier corrective action
- Layered process audits
- Formal FMEA and change control

Do not compensate for poor process capability through final inspection, overtime, or shipment pressure.

### Priority 6: Simplify administrative and digital flow

Create one order-status dashboard with standard delay codes:

- Customer clarification
- Engineering queue
- Material shortage
- Supplier late
- Production queue
- Inspector unavailable
- Quality hold
- Rework
- Documentation incomplete
- Schedule change
- Capacity unavailable

Automate routine workflow only while preserving authentication, role-based access, attributable signatures, audit trails, revision control, traceability, record retention, backup, and outage procedures.

Until electronic workflows are validated, use a controlled interim manual process.

## 5. Certified-quality protections

### Non-negotiable controls

The following may not be bypassed to meet a date:

- Certified inspections and tests
- Required hold and witness points
- Approved methods and acceptance criteria
- Qualified personnel
- Calibrated equipment
- Approved suppliers and materials
- Serial, lot, and component traceability
- Nonconformance disposition
- Engineering review and revision control
- Complete quality records and final release

### Pre-pilot approval package

Before the pilot, approve:

1. Current and proposed process maps
2. Applicable quality, regulatory, customer, and certification requirements
3. Compliance matrix covering inspections, records, approvals, and notifications
4. PFMEA and control-plan revisions
5. Inspection and test plan
6. Routing, traveler, and work-instruction revisions
7. Production and inspection capacity analysis
8. Training and qualification evidence
9. Supplier and material readiness
10. Measurement-system validation where applicable
11. Traceability test
12. Electronic-workflow validation
13. Stop criteria and containment plan
14. Named owners and approval signatures

Any process, supplier, material, inspection, staffing, routing, software, or document-system change triggers formal management of change.

## 6. Warranty safeguards

### Leading indicators

- First-pass and rolled-throughput yield
- Rework and retest hours
- Final-test failures
- Nonconformances and escapes
- Supplier incoming defects
- Documentation errors
- Inspection discrepancies
- Overtime and fatigue exposure
- Training completion
- Unauthorized overrides
- Audit findings

### Lagging indicators

- Warranty claims and cost per unit
- Returns and field failures
- Early-life failures
- Failure mode by serial number, supplier, shift, and revision
- Customer corrective-action requests
- Repeat failures after repair

Review results by production cohort. Define alert and stop thresholds before the pilot begins.

### Stop or rollback triggers

Pause the pilot or revert the change if:

- Critical or major defects increase
- First-pass yield falls beyond the approved limit
- Final-test failures rise
- A quality escape occurs
- Warranty or early-life indicators deteriorate
- Mandatory records are incomplete
- Inspection backlog pressures staff to defer required gates
- Unapproved deviations occur
- Supplier incoming quality worsens

Quality owns technical containment. Operations owns physical segregation and schedule control. Service, legal, regulatory, and customer-support functions participate when notification or field action is required.

## 7. Governance and alignment

### Accountability

| Area | Accountable owner |
|---|---|
| Business result and resources | Plant/business-unit leader |
| Program execution | Improvement leader |
| Daily schedule and compliant priority sequencing | Operations planning |
| Engineering readiness and release | Engineering manager |
| Materials and suppliers | Supply-chain/purchasing manager |
| Certified inspection and release | Quality manager |
| Quoting policy and customer assumptions | Sales leader |
| Inventory and investment cases | Finance and supply chain |
| Warranty surveillance and containment | Quality with service/customer support |
| Metric definitions and data quality | Operations excellence/data owner |

### Decision rights

- Quality has final authority over conformity, certified release, and nonconformance disposition.
- Engineering controls design release, configurations, technical changes, and alternate materials.
- Operations planning controls finite scheduling within approved quality and capacity limits.
- Sales cannot unilaterally override capacity-validated dates.
- The improvement leader may stop a pilot when predefined criteria are breached.
- The plant/business leader makes final resource and phase-advancement decisions.
- The council resolves tradeoffs but cannot override quality, regulatory, or certification requirements.

Any quote shorter than the system-generated date requires documented capacity evidence and plant-leader approval.

### Operating cadence

**Daily, 15 minutes**

- Orders due within 15 working days
- Material shortages
- Engineering blocks
- Bottleneck loading
- Inspection demand and capacity
- Quality holds and rework
- Customer changes
- Expedites
- Owner and next action

**Weekly Lead-Time Council**

- Lead-time waterfall and late-order causes
- On-time delivery and quote accuracy
- Quality and warranty indicators
- Supplier and inventory exposure
- Capacity and investment decisions
- Pilot go/stop decisions
- Open disputes and assigned actions

Disputes are resolved using the order record and common definitions, not functional anecdotes.

## 8. Phased roadmap

### Phase 0: Diagnose and stabilize — Weeks 0–4

**Actions**

- Define clocks, statuses, timestamps, reason codes, and metric rules.
- Normalize the competitor benchmark.
- Validate the sales loss estimate.
- Reconstruct the order sample.
- Audit current process compliance.
- Establish quality, warranty, supplier, inventory, capacity, and inspection baselines.
- Assess planning-system and electronic-record capability.
- Stop undocumented changes and uncontrolled expedites.
- Start daily blocked-order management.

**Exit criteria**

- 90% of active orders have an owner, reason, and next action.
- Required inspection and release records are complete.
- Top three delay causes are supported by order-level evidence.
- Pilot family, constraint, intervention bundle, and stop criteria are approved.
- Dependencies have owners, dates, and required evidence.

### Phase 1: Narrow flow pilot — Weeks 5–12

Pilot one stable standard family and one primary constraint.

Implement:

- Order-completeness control
- Material-readiness control
- Bottleneck finite scheduling
- Inspection advance notification and reserved capacity
- WIP and expedite limits

Treat this as a bundled operating-model pilot and measure the mechanism of each intervention separately.

**Planning targets, subject to validation**

- Lead time: 10–11 weeks
- Engineering release reduction: 30–50%
- Inspection waiting reduction: 50%
- Material available at planned start: at least 95%
- Critical supplier on-time delivery: at least 90%
- No deterioration in quality or warranty indicators

### Phase 2: Controlled expansion — Months 4–9

After evidence supports expansion:

- Add validated defect-reduction changes
- Reduce setup and changeover time
- Establish product-family lanes
- Introduce selective safety stock
- Establish supplier capacity agreements
- Qualify alternate sources
- Introduce controlled postponement or common-module prebuild
- Implement validated electronic travelers
- Add targeted capacity at the proven constraint
- Formalize product-family quoting rules

**Planning targets**

- Standard-family lead time: 8–9 weeks
- On-time delivery: at least 95%
- Rework hours reduced at least 25%
- Expedites reduced at least 50%
- Standard-order right-first-time release: at least 90%
- Critical supplier on-time delivery: at least 95%
- Certified inspection and traceability compliance: 100%

### Phase 3: Conditional six-week commercialization — Months 9–15

- Expand modularization and configurable BOMs.
- Add permanent or flexible capacity at proven constraints.
- Increase common-component inventory only where justified.
- Integrate available-to-promise with material, engineering, production, inspection, and release status.
- Add predictive risk flags.
- Audit changed processes and certification implications.
- Activate six-week quoting only for qualifying families.
