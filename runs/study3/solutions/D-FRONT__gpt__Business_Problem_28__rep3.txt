## 1. Recommendation and immediate board decisions

Three consecutive years of losses, two restructurings that reduced costs without restoring profitability, a shrinking order book, and the transfer of strong managers indicate a persistent business problem. They do **not yet establish** whether the losses are structural, execution-driven, or mixed.

The unit should not receive a third broad cost-reduction program. The appropriate response is a **short, independently validated diagnostic followed by stage-gated funding**.

### Approve now

The board should approve:

1. **Capped stabilization and diagnostic funding only**, through the first evidence gate.  
   - Tranche 1 amount: **[Board to set after CFO liquidity review]**.  
   - Minimum liquidity reserve: **[Board to set]**.  
   - Maximum total exposure before a new board decision: **[Board to set]**.

2. **Immediate cash, safety, customer, and contractual-protection measures**, including:
   - Weekly 13-week cash forecasting.
   - Freeze on discretionary capital expenditure and expansion.
   - No new work outside approved margin, cash, safety, and contract-risk rules.
   - Immediate review of all material projects, bids, claims, and customer exposures.

3. **Appointment of a named turnaround leader** with authority over P&L, cash, bidding, contracts, staffing, and asset actions within approved limits.

4. **Independent validation** of:
   - The loss attribution.
   - Contract estimates at completion.
   - The profit and cash bridge.
   - The minimum viable core.
   - Shared-customer synergies.
   - Exit values and liabilities.

5. **Confidential preparation of exit alternatives**, including selective integration, service-line or asset sale, whole-unit sale, partnership, and orderly wind-down. Market sounding should be authorized subject to the Board Turnaround Committee’s approval.

6. **No release of further turnaround funding** until management delivers:
   - A validated loss bridge.
   - A quantified minimum viable core.
   - A monthly cash model.
   - A management-capacity assessment.
   - A probability-weighted comparison of turnaround and exit values.

### Decision principle

Retain the unit only if a focused core demonstrates recurring, cash-backed profitability after realistic standalone overhead, capital, working capital, risk, and management costs. Shared customers and group synergies count only where incremental value is evidenced and separately accounted for.

# 3. Diagnosis: structural versus execution-driven loss

## 3.1 Working hypothesis

The most plausible hypothesis is that the unit contains some potentially viable activities but is being diluted by a combination of:

### Potentially structural issues

- Commoditized and low-bid work.
- Weak differentiation or pricing power.
- Cyclical or intermittent demand.
- Insufficient geographic density.
- Underutilized specialist labor or equipment.
- Fixed costs too high for the current revenue base.
- Risk, inflation, labor, or schedule exposure that cannot be priced or transferred.
- Market share or customer demand deterioration.

### Potentially execution-driven issues

- Poor estimating and contract selection.
- Bid margins deteriorating during delivery.
- Scope leakage and unapproved work.
- Weak change-order recovery and underbilling.
- Rework, warranty, overtime, and subcontractor overruns.
- Weak scheduling and labor utilization.
- Poor working-capital management.
- Management attrition and inadequate project controls.

The burden of proof rests with management. Three years of losses and two unsuccessful restructurings increase the likelihood that the problem is more than excess overhead.

## 3.2 Required loss attribution

Management must quantify the dollar and percentage contribution of each driver to the reported and normalized loss:

| Driver | Required test |
|---|---|
| Market pricing | Is comparable market pricing below the unit’s fully loaded cost? |
| Commoditization | Is the offering readily substitutable and purchased primarily on price? |
| Contract selection | Was work accepted below required economic hurdles? |
| Execution leakage | Do margins deteriorate after award? |
| Productivity | Are labor hours, utilization, overtime, or labor mix uneconomic? |
| Rework and warranty | Are defects and repeat work material and controllable? |
| Scope and billing | Is earned value unbilled or unrecovered? |
| Geographic density | Do travel, standby, supervision, and equipment movement destroy contribution? |
| Equipment | Is capital underutilized or obsolete? |
| Overhead | Is fixed cost excessive for the focused business? |
| Working capital | Does work consume disproportionate cash? |
| Legacy liabilities | Are claims, leases, pensions, tax, environmental, or warranty matters material? |
| Strategic subsidy | Is loss-making work retained for another group benefit? |
| One-time items | Are results distorted by restructuring or exceptional costs? |

The analysis must distinguish recurring from nonrecurring items and assign high, medium, or low confidence.

## 3.3 Required market tests

The diagnostic must include:

- Three-year market-share and customer-retention trends.
- Win/loss performance against named competitors.
- Competitor pricing and capacity.
- Customer procurement and insourcing trends.
- Labor-rate competitiveness.
- Minimum efficient scale.
- Required geographic density.
- Break-even revenue at realistic margins.
- Probability of achieving the required revenue and utilization.

No viable core should be claimed until it is demonstrated after standalone costs, capital, cash, risk, and liability requirements.

# 4. Required profitability architecture

Management must reconstruct the last 36 months, current year-to-date results, backlog, and pipeline by:

- Customer and parent account.
- Group-affiliated versus external customer.
- Contract and project.
- Service line.
- Geography and operating location.
- Manager and sales originator.
- New versus recurring work.
- Labor category, equipment, and subcontractor.

## 4.1 Four economic views

### Contribution economics

\[
\text{Contribution Margin}
=
\text{Revenue}
-\text{Direct Labor}
-\text{Materials}
-\text{Subcontractors}
-\text{Equipment}
-\text{Travel and Mobilization}
-\text{Project-Specific Selling Costs}
-\text{Rework and Warranty}
\]

### Standalone economics

\[
\text{Standalone EBIT}
=
\text{Contribution Margin}
-\text{Required Operating Overhead}
-\text{Support Functions}
-\text{Maintenance Capex Cost}
-\text{Claims and Bad-Debt Provision}
\]

### Strategic group economics

\[
\text{Strategic Value}
=
\text{Standalone EBIT}
+\text{Verified Incremental Group Contribution}
+\text{Avoided Group Costs}
+\text{Customer Retention Value}
-\text{Integration Costs}
-\text{Required Subsidy}
\]

### Cash economics

\[
\text{Operating Cash Contribution}
=
\text{EBIT}
-\text{Working-Capital Investment}
-\text{Maintenance Capex}
-\text{Cash Claims and Settlements}
\]

A contract that produces accounting profit but consumes excessive cash or carries unacceptable downside risk is not economically attractive.

# 5. Portfolio direction

Actual classifications require the diagnostic. The decision framework is:

| Category | Test | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Keep / Core** | Positive contribution, repeatable demand, differentiation, acceptable cash conversion | Protect and selectively invest |
| **Fix** | Attractive demand or capability, but identifiable pricing or execution problem | 90–180-day plan with maximum investment and exit date |
| **Strategic / Integrate** | Standalone loss but measurable group benefit | Retain only through transparent transfer pricing or integration |
| **Harvest** | Existing work generates cash but future economics are weak | Complete profitable backlog; no expansion |
| **Partner** | Capability is valuable but ownership lacks scale, density, or equipment economics | JV, subcontract, or specialist partnership |
| **Exit** | Negative economics or unacceptable risk after realistic corrective action | Stop bidding; complete, novate, or wind down |

## 5.1 Potential priority segments

These are industry hypotheses, not findings about this unit. Subject to validation, prioritize:

- Compliance, inspection, testing, and certification.
- Asset integrity, reliability, and predictive maintenance.
- Installed-base support for profitable group divisions.
- Multi-year technical support and maintenance.
- Specialist shutdown, turnaround, or outage work.
- Emergency response where standby, mobilization, overtime, and equipment readiness are paid.

## 5.2 Activities to scrutinize or exit

- Commodity labor supply.
- Routine low-bid work.
- Open-ended or poorly protected fixed-price contracts.
- Work with uncapped liability.
- Low-density locations and dispersed small accounts.
- Chronic late payers and claims-heavy customers.
- Highly cyclical project work without a recurring base.
- Equipment-intensive activities with persistently low utilization.

## 5.3 Minimum viable core

Management must define the smallest independently viable business, including:

- Named service lines and customer cohorts.
- Required monthly and annual revenue.
- Contribution-margin requirement.
- Fixed-cost base.
- Headcount and critical roles.
- Equipment and facilities.
- Geographic footprint and density.
- Working-capital requirement.
- Maintenance capex.
- Backlog coverage.
- Customer concentration.
- Expected ROIC.
- Downside revenue and margin case.
- Transition cost from the current business.

“Smaller specialist platform” is not an investable conclusion without this quantified model.

Every “fix” activity must have a root cause, owner, corrective actions, required investment, monthly milestones, maximum loss, bid restrictions, existing-contract treatment, and automatic exit date.

# 6. Shared-customer economics

Customer overlap with profitable divisions may create value, but it can also conceal subsidy.

For each material shared customer, management must quantify:

1. Revenue and contribution by group division.
2. Standalone industrial-services contribution and EBIT.
3. Evidence that the unit causes retention or cross-selling.
4. Probability of losing other group revenue if the unit exits.
5. Contribution margin actually at risk.
6. External replacement cost and feasibility.
7. Required service level and price.
8. Benefiting division and cost-bearing division.
9. Duration of the benefit.
10. Integration and transition costs.
11. Required capital.
12. Liability and service-failure risk.

### Counterfactual model

\[
\text{Net Shared-Customer Value}
=
(\text{At-Risk Group Contribution} \times \text{Causal Probability})
+\text{Avoided Replacement Cost}
-\text{Industrial-Services Loss}
-\text{Integration Cost}
-\text{Required Capital}
-\text{Risk Provision}
\]

The terms must be non-overlapping. Cross-sell, retention, avoided replacement cost, and standalone contribution must not be counted twice.

Any deliberate subsidy must be:

- Approved by the benefiting division.
- Recorded through transparent transfer pricing.
- Time-limited.
- Subject to an economic hurdle.
- Reported separately from standalone performance.

2. Mandatory Board notification.

5. Formal sale launch.
6. Mandatory wind-down recommendation.

Safety, environmental, legal, and catastrophic liability events may override an otherwise favorable financial outcome.

# 13. Exit alternatives and ranking

The fallback order should ultimately be determined by net risk-adjusted value, not generic preference. The preliminary hierarchy is:

## 1. Selective integration

Use where installed-base access, technical expertise, or customer retention has demonstrable value to profitable divisions and can be absorbed at lower cost than standalone ownership.

## 2. Sale of specialist service lines, teams, contracts, or assets

Use where value is concentrated in transferable certifications, people, equipment, or recurring contracts.

## 3. Whole-unit sale

Pursue only where a coherent, transferable perimeter and credible buyer interest exist. A loss-making unit with shrinking backlog may attract only asset, team, contract, or certification buyers.

## 4. Partnership or joint venture

Use where external scale, geography, capital, labor, or specialist capability can address the unit’s structural disadvantages.

## 5. Orderly wind-down

Use where no buyer, integration, or partnership case exceeds the value of stopping future losses.

For each alternative, quantify:

- Buyer or receiving division.
- Strategic rationale.
- Valuation methodology.
- Enterprise value and equity proceeds.
- Assumed liabilities.
- Working-capital requirement.
- Transaction, tax, and separation costs.
- Escrow, indemnity, and deferred consideration.
- Stranded costs.
- Probability of closing.
- Timing.
- Customer-consent probability.
- Employee-retention requirements.
- Funding required through closing.

### Valuation framework

\[
V_T =
PV(\text{Focused-Core Cash Flows})
-\text{Additional Funding}
-\text{Execution Risk}
-\text{Legacy Liabilities}
\]

\[
V_S =
\text{Enterprise Value}
-\text{Debt and Debt-like Items}
-\text{Transaction Costs}
-\text{Taxes}
-\text{Working Capital}
-\text{Escrow and Indemnities}
-\text{Stranded Costs}
\]

\[
V_I =
\text{Incremental Group Contribution}
+\text{Avoided External Cost}
+\text{Retention Value}
-\text{Inherited Losses}
-\text{Integration Costs}
-\text{Required Capital}
-\text{Stranded Costs}
\]

All alternatives must be probability-weighted for execution, customer retention, liability, and closing risk. Historical investment and sunk restructuring costs are irrelevant.

# 16. Decision criteria

## Continue turnaround only if

- A minimum viable core is identified and evidenced.
- Core contribution is positive and recurring.
- Standalone EBIT can meet the Group hurdle.
- Operating cash flow is positive or credibly reaches positive within the approved funding limit.
- Risk-adjusted backlog supports the focused cost base.
- New and renewed contracts meet approved hurdles.
- Management capacity is adequate.
- Capital and working-capital requirements are acceptable.
- Shared-customer benefits are independently quantified without double counting.
- Turnaround value exceeds risk-adjusted exit value.
- No liquidity, safety, legal, liability, or funding hard stop is breached.

## Launch formal sale or integration if

- The core is plausible but not yet proven.
- Valuable capabilities are transferable.
- Buyer or receiving-division synergies exceed continued ownership value.
- Separation and liability costs are manageable.
- Customer and employee transfer risks are acceptable.

## Wind down if

- No activity earns acceptable returns after realistic standalone costs.
- Backlog continues to deteriorate.
- Cash burn exceeds the Board-approved envelope.
- Material liabilities are unquantifiable or exceed recoverable value.
- Critical managers or customers cannot be retained.
- No buyer, integration, or partnership case exceeds the value of stopping future losses.

## Final conclusion

The available evidence supports **limited, capped stabilization and diagnostic funding only**. It does not yet support a full one-year turnaround investment or a final ownership decision.

The unit may contain a viable specialist core, but that remains unproven. The next funding decision must reconcile accounting profit, economic profit, operating cash flow, capital employed, ROIC, shared-customer value, management capacity, liabilities, and risk-adjusted exit proceeds.

Until that evidence is independently validated, the Group should fund only actions necessary to protect liquidity, safety, contractual obligations, customers, and exit value. If the diagnostic fails to establish a minimum viable core with recurring cash-backed profitability, the Group should stop funding the unit and execute the highest-value alternative—preferably selective integration or sale of valuable capabilities, and otherwise an orderly wind-down.
