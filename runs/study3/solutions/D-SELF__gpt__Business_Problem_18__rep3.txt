# Repositioning Strategy  
## From Trusted Classic to Trusted, Useful, and Current

### Executive summary

[Brand] has the assets many newer brands would like to build: high awareness, strong distribution, genuine heritage, and deep trust among loyal customers. Its challenge is not visibility. It is contemporary consideration.

Consumers under 35 know [Brand], but many do not see a compelling reason to choose it over newer competitors, private label, substitutes, or different ways of meeting the category need. Previous modernization campaigns failed because they changed the brand’s surface—visual style, language, or media choices—without making the product or experience more relevant.

The recommendation is to:

> **Preserve the performance people trust. Modernize how that performance is accessed, experienced, and demonstrated.**

[Brand] should not try to look young. It should become more useful in current lives while remaining recognizably itself.

The working positioning is:

> **For consumers under 35 who want a dependable way to get [specific category task] right without wasting time, money, or effort, [Brand] is the proven choice that makes the decision easier today—combining trusted performance, accessible value, and practical improvements for current routines.**

The category task, priority occasion, competitive alternative, and proof change must be finalized before national launch. The transition should follow a proof-before-promise sequence:

1. Diagnose the specific younger-consumer barrier.
2. Select one priority occasion and distinctive advantage.
3. Make one tangible product or experience improvement.
4. Prove it in retail and controlled pilots.
5. Communicate the change modestly and credibly.
6. Scale only what improves younger trial and repeat without weakening core trust.

---

# 1. Strategic diagnosis

## The business problem

[Brand] has:

- High awareness.
- Strong retail distribution.
- Established product credibility.
- A genuine 70-year heritage.
- Loyal, repeat customers.
- Existing functional and emotional equities.

However, purchase share among consumers under 35 is declining. The likely issue is not that younger consumers have never heard of the brand. It is that they do not associate it with a current need, occasion, routine, or advantage.

The brand may be perceived as:

- Familiar but dated.
- Reliable but not personally relevant.
- Available but not actively considered.
- Functional but not distinctive.
- Trusted by parents or grandparents rather than chosen by them.
- Less convenient, transparent, or flexible than newer alternatives.

The strategic implication is clear:

> **Do not make the brand look younger. Give younger consumers a credible, current reason to choose it.**

## Required diagnosis before investment

Before finalizing the position, leadership should establish:

1. Whether under-35 consumers are leaving the brand or leaving the category.
2. The specific purchase or usage occasion where the brand loses consideration.
3. The competitor, substitute, or behavior currently winning that occasion.
4. The most important barrier to trial and repeat.
5. The distinctive advantage [Brand] can credibly own.
6. The tangible improvement that will make the new position true.

The initial target should prioritize current-category users, competitor users, and lapsed users before attempting to recruit category rejectors.

# 2. New positioning

## Strategic positioning

> **For consumers under 35 who want a dependable way to get [specific category task] right without wasting time, money, or effort, [Brand] is the proven choice that makes the decision easier today—combining trusted performance, accessible value, and practical improvements for current routines.**

The bracketed category task must be replaced with one specific, research-supported consumer job. It should not be a list of benefits such as convenience, sustainability, value, and flexibility.

## Consumer-facing campaign platform

> **Proven Then. Ready Now.**

This platform connects the brand’s heritage to current usefulness, but it is not sufficient as a standalone proposition. Every execution must explain what “ready now” means for the consumer:

- A more convenient format.
- A lower-risk first purchase.
- Better value per use.
- Easier product selection.
- A current use occasion.
- Improved accessibility.
- More transparent performance.
- A simpler or more flexible routine.

Possible supporting expressions include:

- **Proven performance. Easier choice.**
- **Decades of knowing what works. Made for today.**
- **The trusted original, in a format that fits life now.**
- **Experience you can count on, without the extra effort.**

The final expression should be selected after the priority category task and proof change are established.

## Promise to younger consumers

> **[Brand] gives me a dependable, accessible choice that works in real life, is easy to understand and find, and carries less risk than an unfamiliar alternative.**

## Promise to loyal customers

> **The performance, quality, value, and availability you trust are protected. We are improving the formats, service, access, and experience so the brand remains useful for current and future routines.**

## Positioning guardrails

[Brand] should be:

- Experienced, not old.
- Current, not trendy.
- Accessible, not cheap.
- Confident, not boastful.
- Useful, not preachy.
- Inclusive, not youth-exclusive.
- Improving, not pretending to be reinvented.
- Specific, not broadly inspirational.

# 3. Audience roles

## Primary growth audience: under-35 category users and lapsed users

Define the initial growth audience behaviorally:

> **Consumers under 35 who participate in the category but choose competitors, private label, substitutes, or no consistent brand because [Brand] feels inconvenient, difficult to evaluate, culturally distant, or poorly matched to their current lives.**

They should be prioritized because they already recognize the category need and are more likely to trial and repeat than category rejectors.

### Core tension

> **“I want a dependable result and good value, but established brands often feel like they belong to someone else’s life.”**

This tension must be narrowed to one actionable barrier, such as:

- The product does not fit current routines.
- The format requires too much commitment.
- The value is unclear at the point of purchase.
- The product is difficult to compare.
- The brand feels familiar but not relevant.
- The category is being replaced by another behavior.

## Loyal base: existing repeat customers

Loyal customers are a source of credibility, revenue, advocacy, and product knowledge. They should not be treated as an obstacle to modernization.

Their practical expectations are:

- Familiar performance.
- Quality and safety.
- Continued availability.
- Recognizable packaging.
- Fair price-to-value.
- Continued access to core products.
- Responsive service.
- Respect from the brand.

They should be invited to test and improve new products, formats, and experiences—not simply reassured after decisions are made.

## Recruitment audience: category rejectors

Consumers who no longer participate in the category may represent a longer-term opportunity, but they should not be the first growth priority unless research shows that the category can be re-entered through a new occasion, format, or service.

# 4. Preserve, evolve, and retire decisions

## Preserve

### Product and trust equities

- Core product performance.
- Quality and safety standards.
- Reliability and consistency.
- Strong distribution and availability.
- High-performing core products.
- Familiar customer rituals.
- Proven service practices.
- Accessible value architecture.

### Distinctive assets

Retain the assets that drive recognition and habitual choice:

- Brand name.
- Logo architecture.
- Highest-equity colors.
- Pack shape or silhouette.
- Signature sound, mnemonic, or visual cue.
- Recognizable product structure.

A 70-year-old brand should not discard recognition without evidence that the commercial upside outweighs the risk.

### Heritage

Preserve the parts of the heritage story that demonstrate capability:

- The problem the brand originally solved.
- The standards that have remained non-negotiable.
- What the brand has learned.
- How the product has adapted before.
- What current improvements demonstrate that learning continues.

Heritage should answer:

> **Why should I trust this brand now?**

It should not be used as nostalgia for its own sake.

## Evolve

- Packaging information hierarchy.
- Shelf navigation.
- Digital legibility and product education.
- Photography, casting, and motion.
- Trial sizes, entry formats, or bundles.
- Convenience and accessibility.
- Product transparency.
- Customer service responsiveness.
- Retail and e-commerce execution.
- Innovation tied to the selected current need.

The desired outcome is:

> **Recognizably [Brand], easier to understand, easier to choose, and more useful today.**

## Retire

- Forced slang, memes, and youth-coded language.
- “New generation” claims without a product reason.
- Nostalgia-only advertising.
- Anniversary-led messaging without contemporary proof.
- Unrelated celebrities and trend partnerships.
- Generic “revolutionary” or “reinvented” claims.
- Unsupported purpose or sustainability claims.
- Creative that implies loyal customers are outdated.
- A separate youth identity.
- Abrupt logo or packaging replacement before testing.

## Avoid

- Using younger casting as a substitute for relevance.
- Treating consumers under 35 as one lifestyle group.
- Replacing a trusted core product before proving parity or improvement.
- Reducing pack quantity or increasing price without value proof.
- Moving familiar products out of established channels too quickly.
- Inviting public criticism without response and remediation plans.
- Using older consumers only as nostalgic symbols.

# 5. Brand expression

## Voice

[Brand] should speak with confidence earned through experience.

### Use

- Plain, direct language.
- Specific claims.
- Practical demonstrations.
- Warmth without sentimentality.
- Light self-awareness.
- Honest limitations.
- Evidence before superlatives.

### Avoid

- Borrowed slang.
- Trend references that overwhelm the product.
- Defensive claims that the brand is “still cool.”
- Overly polished purpose language.
- Speaking about younger consumers as a demographic stereotype.
- Suggesting that the brand’s history is an embarrassment to overcome.

## Visual identity

Modernize selectively:

- Simplify layouts.
- Improve information hierarchy.
- Use contemporary, natural photography.
- Show real routines and environments.
- Make the product visible and easy to understand.
- Create flexible motion and digital systems.
- Retain high-equity brand cues.
- Use retro elements only when they support a current story.

The visual test is:

> **Would a loyal customer recognize [Brand] immediately, and would a younger shopper understand why it is relevant?**

# 6. Channel and content approach

## Channel roles

| Channel | Role | Required behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Retail and e-commerce | Convert availability into trial | Make the product easy to find, compare, understand, and buy |
| Search and retailer media | Capture active need | Answer performance, value, usage, and comparison questions |
| Short-form video | Demonstrate usefulness | Show real tests, current use cases, guidance, and improvements |
| Creators | Provide credible evaluation | Use genuine category users and allow honest reactions |
| Owned content | Resolve doubt | Explain performance, changes, limitations, and choice |
| PR and earned media | Make real action visible | Lead with product, access, value, or measurable improvement |
| CRM and loyalty | Protect and extend relationships | Preview changes, gather feedback, and support repeat |
| Customer service | Make the promise real | Resolve concerns and feed insight into decisions |

Retail and e-commerce should be the first commercial proving ground. The brand’s strong distribution is an advantage only if it makes trial and repeat easier.

## Content territories

### 1. Proven

Show what the brand reliably does well:

- Product tests.
- Quality standards.
- Customer reviews.
- Performance comparisons.
- Consistency over time.

### 2. Made for now

Connect the product to the selected current occasion:

- Current household or usage situations.
- Time- or budget-constrained routines.
- Lower-risk first purchases.
- Flexible formats.
- Demonstrations of the new improvement.

### 3. Still improving

Show that heritage is active stewardship:

- Packaging or format changes.
- Customer feedback incorporated into development.
- Measurable progress and remaining gaps.
- Product-development decisions.
- Honest answers to common objections.

### 4. Passed forward

Make heritage intergenerational without reducing older consumers to props:

- An experienced user shares a useful technique.
- A younger user adapts it to a current context.
- Employees explain how standards have evolved.
- New and long-time users test the product against different needs.

Intergenerational storytelling should provide genuine utility or exchange, not staged sentiment.

### Starting content mix

Use the following only as an initial hypothesis:

- 40% product utility and proof.
- 25% current-life applications.
- 20% improvement and transparency.
- 15% heritage and intergenerational content.

Adjust the mix according to consideration, trial, repeat, and distinctiveness—not reach or engagement alone.

## Creator principles

Select creators based on genuine category relevance rather than follower count.

Require:

- Natural use or knowledge of the category.
- Clear disclosure.
- Honest evaluation.
- Freedom to identify limitations.
- No scripted positive conclusion.
- Defined rules for comparison, comments, usage rights, and crisis escalation.

# 7. Phased transition roadmap

The roadmap should be evidence-gated. Timing is indicative; progression depends on results.

## Immediate actions: 0–3 months  
### Diagnose and protect

Establish:

- Category participation among consumers under 35.
- The primary need-state.
- The priority purchase or usage occasion.
- The main barrier to trial and repeat.
- The main competitor or substitute.
- The brand’s distinctive advantage.
- The strongest heritage proof.
- The brand assets that drive recognition.
- The core products and channels that must be protected.

Conduct:

- Lapsed and lost-customer interviews.
- Age-cohort brand and category research.
- Retail and e-commerce path-to-purchase analysis.
- Prior-campaign backlash analysis.
- Blind and branded product testing.
- Distinctive-asset recognition testing.
- Competitive concept testing.

**Decision gate:** Proceed only when one audience, one occasion, one barrier, one advantage, and one strategic territory are supported by evidence.

## Near-term actions: approximately 3–9 months  
### Make the repositioning true

Select and implement one primary proof change:

- A lower-commitment entry format.
- Easier packaging or use.
- Better price-per-use value.
- Clearer product guidance.
- More convenient access or replenishment.
- A current use occasion.
- A measurable quality, accessibility, or sustainability improvement.

Also:

- Preserve the core product and familiar access.
- Improve product pages and guidance.
- Prepare customer-service and frontline teams.
- Brief retailers and distribution partners.
- Test with younger consumers and loyal customers.
- Confirm supply, pricing, claims, and operational readiness.

Communication should be restrained:

> **Here is what we improved and why.**

Not:

> **We are a completely new brand.**

**Decision gate:** Proceed only when the change can be delivered consistently and does not create unacceptable risk to value, recognition, availability, or core performance.

## Pilot stage  
### Prove commercial value

Use matched test and control markets, retailer pilots, or holdout audiences.

Measure:

- Younger trial.
- Product satisfaction.
- Early repeat.
- Core-customer response.
- Price and promotion dependence.
- Distinctive-asset recognition.
- Incremental sales.
- Margin.
- Cannibalization.
- Retailer acceptance.

**Decision gate:** Proceed only when the proof change meets pre-agreed thresholds for younger trial and satisfaction while protecting core retention, recognition, value, and contribution.

## Later-stage change: after pilot evidence  
### Reframe the brand publicly

Launch **Proven Then. Ready Now.** only when the brand can demonstrate a real improvement.

Every major execution should connect:

1. Heritage or performance credibility.
2. A specific current consumer tension.
3. A visible product or experience benefit.
4. Easy access or trial.

Use:

- Broad video and digital media for reconsideration.
- Search and retail media for evaluation.
- Sampling and entry formats for trial.
- Product guidance, CRM, and service for successful use and repeat.

## Scale stage: approximately 15–24 months, subject to evidence  
### Scale what works

Expand only the combinations that improve:

- Younger trial.
- Younger repeat.
- Relevance.
- Distinctiveness.
- Core trust.
- Recognition.
- Incremental contribution.

Do not scale based on reach, views, or launch sentiment alone.

## Ongoing  
### Institutionalize relevance

- Track age-cohort brand health annually.
- Maintain a rolling product and format pipeline.
- Monitor distinctive assets.
- Continue loyal-customer consultation.
- Test retail and e-commerce execution quarterly.
- Maintain a claims and heritage evidence library.
- Establish formal criteria for creators and partnerships.
- Review the positioning as consumer needs evolve without abandoning the core promise.

# 8. Authenticity safeguards

Authenticity must be demonstrated through consistency between:

> **What [Brand] claims, what consumers experience, and what the company is willing to change.**

## Before launch

- Red-team creative with younger consumers, loyal customers, skeptics, and category non-users.
- Test whether the work feels more useful or merely younger.
- Stress-test product, price, packaging, performance, heritage, and sustainability claims.
- Test shelf recognition and findability.
- Confirm evidence for every public claim.
- Brief employees, retailers, creators, and customer service.
- Prepare factual FAQs.
- Define pause, correction, withdrawal, and apology criteria.
- Name authorized spokespeople and escalation owners.

## During launch

- Monitor complaints by issue, not sentiment alone.
- Separate creative criticism from product, price, quality, and availability problems.
- Respond with evidence and plain language.
- Do not argue with consumers.
- Do not delete legitimate criticism.
- Escalate operational complaints rapidly.
- Pause unsupported or misleading claims.

## After launch

- Publish meaningful progress and unresolved gaps where relevant.
- Show how customer feedback changed decisions.
- Correct product, packaging, or communication problems visibly.
- Permit creators to share honest reactions and limitations.
- Do not characterize criticism as consumer misunderstanding.

# 9. Governance and internal alignment

The organization should resolve the debate over modernization through agreed decision rules, not opinion.

## Change architecture

### Non-negotiable

- Product performance and safety.
- Core quality standards.
- Customer respect.
- Key distinctive assets.
- Availability and reliability.
- Legal and regulatory requirements.

### Testable

- Formats and pack sizes.
- Packaging hierarchy.
- Visual expression.
- Digital experiences.
- Bundles and price architecture.
- Creator partnerships.
- New usage occasions.
- Service and replenishment models.

### Open for reinvention

- Content formats.
- Retail activation.
- Product education.
- Community participation.
- Innovation pipeline.
- Ways of demonstrating product performance.

## Governance model

Appoint:

- One executive sponsor.
- One accountable repositioning owner.
- A cross-functional relevance council.
- A dedicated test-and-learn budget.
- Named owners across product, insights, brand, sales, retail, e-commerce, customer service, finance, creative, legal, and operations.

The council must have authority to approve, pause, revise, or scale initiatives. Its charter should define:

- Pilot markets and retailers.
- Budget authority.
- Operational milestones.
- Measurement ownership.
- Stop, pause, and scale criteria.
- Crisis escalation authority.
- Decision deadlines.
- Agency and retailer responsibilities.

## Decision questions

Every major initiative must answer:

1. What established strength does this build on?
2. What current consumer problem does it solve?
3. What proof makes the claim credible?
4. Does it improve trial, repeat, or both?
5. What might loyal customers lose or misunderstand?
6. Is the change recognizable as [Brand]?
7. What is the smallest viable test?
8. What result would cause us to stop, modify, or scale?

# 10. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Younger consumers see another attempt to look young | Lead with a tangible improvement, current occasion, and evidence. |
| The positioning remains generic | Select one category task, occasion, alternative, and distinctive advantage. |
| The category itself is declining among under-35 consumers | Diagnose category participation before investing in broad communication. |
| Loyal customers feel rejected | Protect core products, performance, availability, value, recognition, and access. |
| New formats increase cost or reduce value | Track price-per-unit, price-per-use, margin, and affordability. |
| Packaging modernization reduces recognition | Test in controlled markets and retain high-equity assets. |
| Trial rises but repeat does not | Diagnose product experience and value before increasing media. |
| New formats cannibalize profitable core products | Measure incremental buyers, mix shift, margin, and repeat. |
| Creators appear scripted or opportunistic | Use genuine category users, disclose partnerships, and permit honest reactions. |
| Heritage becomes nostalgia-only | Require every heritage execution to demonstrate a current benefit. |
| Sustainability claims create backlash | Use only measurable, operationally supported claims. |
| Transparency exposes weaknesses without action | Publish only where owners, timelines, and remediation plans exist. |
| Internal teams overprotect or overchange the brand | Use the non-negotiable, testable, and reinvention framework. |
| Campaign criticism becomes an operational crisis | Monitor issue-level complaints and establish rapid escalation. |
| Younger relevance improves while loyal value perception declines | Pause or modify the change if core value, repeat, or availability breaches agreed thresholds. |

# 11. KPIs and measurement

Success requires improvement in both younger growth and core stability.

## Brand health

Track separately among consumers under 35 and the loyal base:

- Relevance.
- Consideration.
- Preference.
- Perceived quality.
- Perceived value.
- Trust.
- “Feels current.”
- “For people like me.”
- Understanding of the selected current occasion.
- Distinctive-asset recognition.
- Perception that the brand is improving.
- Perception that the brand is becoming more useful rather than pretending to be younger.

## Commercial performance

- Under-35 penetration and purchase share.
- New-to-brand rate.
- Trial-to-repeat conversion.
- 90-day and 180-day repeat.
- Purchase frequency.
- Category share among under-35 households.
- Full-price versus promotion-led purchase.
- Distribution-weighted velocity.
- Incremental sales in test markets.
- Incremental contribution.
- Customer acquisition cost.
- Customer lifetime value.
- Payback period.
- Retailer reorder rates.
- Cannibalization of core products.

## Core-customer guardrails

- Core-SKU repeat and frequency.
- Trust and quality perception.
- Price/value perception.
- Availability.
- Distinctive-asset recognition.
- Product, packaging, and price complaints.
- Churn following changes.
- Customer-service contacts.
- Recommendation and advocacy.

## Channel and content measures

- Search lift.
- Product-page engagement and conversion.
- Retail interaction.
- Trial-format conversion.
- Sampling redemption.
- Qualified creator engagement.
- Product education completion.
- CRM repeat and referral.
- Complaint resolution time.
- Retailer execution compliance.

## Measurement principles

Targets should be set after establishing:

- Historical brand baselines.
- Category norms.
- Competitor performance.
- Required share recovery.
- Pilot economics.
- Statistical confidence.
- Financial hurdle rates.

Every KPI should have:

- A baseline.
- A target.
- A time horizon.
- A data source.
- A segment.
- An owner.
- A decision consequence.

### Illustrative scorecard

| Metric | Baseline | Target | Timing | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under-35 trial | Establish during diagnosis | Improvement versus control | Pilot | Scale or revise |
| 180-day repeat | Establish by cohort | Meets agreed commercial hurdle | Post-pilot | Continue or stop |
| Core-SKU repeat | Historical baseline | Within agreed tolerance | Monthly | Pause change if breached |
| Distinctive-asset recognition | Pre-change baseline | No material decline | Pre/post test | Revise packaging |
| Incremental contribution | Finance baseline | Positive after all change and media costs | Pilot and scale | Reallocate investment |
| Price-per-use | Current baseline | Maintains agreed value boundary | Pilot | Modify offer or pricing |
| Authenticity diagnosis | Pre-launch baseline | No material increase in “trying too hard” perception | Pre/post | Revise creative or claim |

The strategy succeeds only when:

> **Relevance + distinctiveness + trial + repeat**

improve while maintaining:

> **Trust + recognition + availability + performance.**

---

# Final recommendation

[Brand] should not choose between remaining traditional and becoming modern.

It should:

> **Preserve its earned confidence, then modernize how that confidence is accessed, experienced, and proven in current life.**

The brand’s heritage is not an obstacle to overcome. It is a source of credibility—but only when translated into a current consumer benefit and supported by visible action.

The immediate priority is to identify one specific under-35 need-state, occasion, competitive alternative, distinctive advantage, and tangible proof change. Once those decisions are made, the brand should proceed through controlled pilots before introducing a broad repositioning campaign.

The strongest final proposition will not be:

> “We are a 70-year-old brand that is still young.”

It will be:

> **“We know how to deliver [specific category benefit] because we have earned that expertise over decades—and we have improved how people experience it today.”**

That is the credible path from trusted classic to trusted, useful, and current—without forced youth culture, nostalgia-only messaging, or another inauthentic modernization backlash.
