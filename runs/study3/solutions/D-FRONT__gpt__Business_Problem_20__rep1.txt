# Operations Improvement Plan  
## Reduce quoted lead time without compromising certified quality

## 1. Objective and operating boundaries

The company quotes 14 weeks versus a competitor’s 6 weeks and reportedly loses one in three deals on delivery speed. The immediate objective is not to promise six weeks prematurely. It is to identify where the 14 weeks are consumed, remove avoidable delay, validate the revised process, and then shorten quotes based on demonstrated capability.

### Non-negotiable requirements

No initiative may bypass, weaken, or retrospectively approve:

- Certified inspection, witness, or hold points.
- Approved test methods and acceptance criteria.
- Qualified personnel requirements.
- Calibration and measurement-system controls.
- First-article or first-piece requirements.
- Material, lot, serial-number, and configuration traceability.
- Nonconformance containment and authorized disposition.
- Required Engineering, Quality, customer, regulatory, or certification approvals.
- Required inspection, test, and release records.

The program may change timing, sequencing, staffing, layout, inventory, supplier arrangements, and scheduling—but not mandatory technical or quality requirements without formal approval and validation.

---

# 2. Where time is likely being lost—and how to verify it

The 14 weeks are not yet explained by evidence. The following are the primary hypotheses to test:

- Incomplete customer or engineering information.
- Material shortages and supplier variability.
- Internal queues and excess work-in-process.
- Batch and changeover waiting.
- Unstable priorities and schedule resequencing.
- Inspection and test queues.
- Rework, NCR disposition, and repeat testing.
- Final documentation and quality-release delays.
- A capacity constraint such as a machine, test stand, skilled operation, or certified inspector.

## 2.1 Establish one lead-time clock

Use a common definition:

> **Order-to-ship lead time = shipment timestamp minus commercially complete order timestamp.**

Also track:

1. Order received to commercially complete.
2. Commercially complete to technically complete.
3. Technical completion to Engineering release.
4. Engineering release to material-ready.
5. Production release to shipment.
6. Quality release to shipment.

This prevents an apparent improvement caused by changing the start date.

## 2.2 Use controlled process statuses

Every order should have timestamped entry and exit criteria for:

1. Order received.
2. Commercially complete.
3. Technically complete.
4. Engineering released.
5. Material-ready.
6. Production-ready.
7. Released to production.
8. In production.
9. Ready for inspection.
10. Inspection or test.
11. NCR or quality hold.
12. Rework.
13. Quality released.
14. Packed and shipped.

Each status requires a named owner and an escalation threshold.

## 2.3 Analyze representative order history

Review 6–12 months of orders, segmented by:

- Standard, configured, and engineered-to-order products.
- Product family and complexity.
- On-time and late orders.
- Supplier and production cell.
- Orders with shortages, engineering changes, NCRs, rework, or warranty issues.
- Expedited orders.

Reconcile ERP data with MES, PLM, inspection logs, NCR records, supplier confirmations, travelers, and physical observation. Missing timestamps should be recorded as data gaps, not estimated.

## 2.4 Build a lead-time bridge

For each order, quantify:

| Time category | What to measure |
|---|---|
| Order clarification | Waiting for Sales or customer information |
| Engineering | Review, approval, and release queue |
| Materials | Shortage and kit-completion delay |
| Suppliers | Confirmed versus actual delivery |
| Production | Touch time, queue time, and movement |
| Batching | Batch release, setup, and changeover waiting |
| Inspection | Waiting, execution, and disposition time |
| Rework | NCR, repair, retest, and repeat inspection |
| Final release | Documentation and approval delay |
| Shipping | Packing, paperwork, and logistics delay |

Calculate:

\[
\text{Elapsed lead time}
=
\text{Processing}
+\text{queues}
+\text{material/supplier waiting}
+\text{inspection waiting}
+\text{approvals}
+\text{rework}
+\text{scheduling effects}
+\text{shipping}
\]

Report median, P90, and P95—not only average lead time. Customer promises should be based on reliable percentile performance by product family.

## 2.5 Identify the critical constraint

Determine whether late orders are primarily constrained by:

- A machine or special process.
- Final assembly.
- A test stand.
- Engineering approval.
- A scarce skill.
- Certified inspection capacity.
- A supplier or long-lead component.

Measure effective capacity:

\[
\text{Effective capacity}
=
\text{Available time}
\times
\text{Performance}
\times
\text{First-pass yield}
\]

Use a weekly Pareto of late-order hours by cause:

1. Material shortage.
2. Supplier lateness.
3. Engineering or customer clarification.
4. Production queue.
5. Inspection queue.
6. Rework or NCR.
7. Approval or disposition.
8. Schedule change.
9. Shipping or documentation.

# 3. Immediate containment and fact-finding

## Phase 0: Weeks 0–2

### Immediate containment

- Prohibit bypassing certified quality gates.
- Prohibit undocumented rework, repair, concessions, or retrospective approvals.
- Require Quality approval for any process, inspection, supplier, material, routing, or test change.
- Start daily review of late orders, shortages, inspection queues, NCRs, rework, and aged WIP.
- Establish one temporary escalation path and one visible promise date.

### Diagnostic work

- Appoint an executive sponsor and cross-functional steering team.
- Define status codes, timestamps, and data owners.
- Select the first pilot product family.
- Map the end-to-end value stream.
- Build the lead-time bridge and delay Pareto.
- Identify the likely constraint.
- Review the prior warranty spike, comparing before, during, and after the previous compression attempt for:
  - Process and product changes.
  - Supplier changes.
  - Overtime and staffing.
  - Training and qualification.
  - First-pass yield, NCRs, rework, and test failures.
  - Inspection or approval deviations.
  - Calibration or measurement issues.
  - Field failure modes by serial number, lot, revision, and supplier.

### Required outputs

- Verified current-state lead-time distribution.
- Delay Pareto and critical-path analysis.
- Quality and reliability risk register.
- Quality-gate and traceability matrix.
- Baseline KPI dashboard.
- Pilot charter, guardrails, and stop criteria.

# 4. Prioritized changes

## Priority 1: Remove avoidable waiting

These are the first changes to implement because they generally improve flow without changing product requirements.

### A. Improve order and engineering readiness

- Require a complete commercial and technical order before production release.
- Use pre-approved configurations for standard products.
- Create a daily Sales–Engineering–Planning review for incomplete orders.
- Define “commercially complete” and “technically complete.”
- Prevent late customer changes from entering production without formal review.
- Remove duplicate administrative approvals and data entry where permitted.

**Measures:** order-entry time, clarification cycles, engineering-release queue, post-release changes, complete-release rate.

### B. Synchronize inspection with production

- Define a “ready for inspection” standard.
- Pre-book inspection and test capacity from the finite production schedule.
- Use a visible inspection queue with aging and escalation.
- Provide advance notice when work is likely to be ready.
- Stage drawings, records, calibrated instruments, fixtures, and test setups.
- Locate qualified inspection capability closer to the operation where permitted.
- Add certified capacity only through formal qualification and authorization.

The aim is to reduce waiting before inspection, not to shorten or weaken the inspection itself.

**Measures:** inspection queue hours, ready-to-start time, rejected inspection requests, repeat inspection rate, disposition queue age.

### C. Stabilize scheduling

- Establish one integrated schedule owned by Planning.
- Freeze the near-term schedule except for defined exceptions.
- Route all priority changes through Planning.
- Record every expedite and its reason.
- Protect the constraint from incomplete kits, avoidable downtime, and noncritical work.
- Do not optimize local utilization if it increases WIP or total lead time.

**Measures:** schedule adherence, priority changes per order, WIP aging, expedite rate, constraint downtime, queue hours.

### D. Improve material readiness

- Review shortages daily.
- Pre-stage material, tools, travelers, and approved fixtures.
- Release work only when the required kit is complete or a controlled partial-release method is approved.
- Identify parts that are consistently on the critical path.

**Measures:** complete-kit release rate, shortage hours per order, critical-part coverage, receipt-to-quality-release time.

### E. Reduce recurring rework

- Create a Pareto of NCRs, repeat tests, and rework hours.
- Correct the largest recurring cause using improved work instructions, fixtures, first-piece checks, or in-process controls.
- Separate product defects from measurement-system problems.
- Require formal Engineering and Quality review for recurring design-related defects.

**Measures:** first-pass yield, rework hours per unit, NCR rate, repeat test rate, escape rate.

---

## Priority 2: Pilot structural flow changes

These changes require controlled testing before being used to support shorter customer commitments.

### Process and layout

- Create a focused cell for repeatable product families.
- Reduce movement and handoffs.
- Use approved alternate routings where technically valid.
- Reduce batch sizes only after setup, traceability, and inspection impacts are understood.
- Reduce setup time through pre-staging, standardized fixtures, and external setup preparation.

### Inventory and postponement

Use selective inventory only for:

- Common, stable items.
- Low-obsolescence components.
- Approved materials with manageable certification and shelf-life requirements.
- Parts that materially affect the customer critical path.

Potential mechanisms:

- Safety stock.
- Kanban or two-bin replenishment.
- Blanket orders.
- Supplier-managed or consigned inventory.
- Approved common subassemblies completed before final configuration.

Do not broadly stock unique, expensive, rapidly changing, or certification-sensitive items without technical and financial review.

### Supplier improvement

For critical suppliers:

- Use rolling forecasts with firm near-term windows.
- Measure confirmed versus actual delivery dates.
- Reserve capacity where justified.
- Establish approved alternate sources.
- Use supplier audits and corrective-action plans.
- Consider framework agreements or selective buffers.
- Share engineering releases earlier.

Incoming inspection must not be relaxed merely to accelerate flow. Any risk-based change requires formal assessment, approval, validation, and documented effectiveness.

# 5. Quality protection system

## 5.1 Quality-gate register

For each product family, document:

| Gate element | Required definition |
|---|---|
| Gate type | Hold, witness, review, or in-process control |
| Requirement | Inspection, test, or approval required |
| Acceptance criteria | Approved limits and method |
| Evidence | Records, signatures, traceability, and equipment identity |
| Authorized approver | Named qualified individual or function |
| Planned queue limit | Maximum intended wait |
| Escalation | Action when the limit is exceeded |

A mandatory hold point cannot be converted into a witness point for schedule reasons.

## 5.2 Traceability and calibration

All pilot and scaled processes must preserve:

- Serial, lot, heat, and batch identity.
- Material certificates and supplier identity.
- Drawing and revision status.
- Operator and process records.
- Equipment and test-station identity.
- Inspection and test results.
- NCR and disposition references.
- Calibration status.

No equipment may be used outside its calibration status or approved operating range. Suspect equipment requires quarantine and impact assessment.

## 5.3 First-article, first-piece, and change control

Require applicable first-article or first-piece approval for:

- New or changed designs.
- New suppliers, materials, tooling, fixtures, or routings.
- Significant process changes.
- Changes affecting fit, function, safety, reliability, or certification.
- Restart after a significant interruption.

Every change must document:

- Affected product and process.
- Technical, quality, safety, regulatory, and warranty risk.
- Required validation or FAI.
- Revised documents and training.
- Implementation date.
- Rollback plan.
- Post-change effectiveness review.

## 5.4 NCR and disposition discipline

NCRs must remain within the formal system:

1. Contain suspect product.
2. Record the issue promptly.
3. Assess impact.
4. Obtain authorized disposition.
5. Verify rework or repair.
6. Repeat required inspection or testing.
7. Close with objective evidence.
8. Feed recurring causes into corrective action.

Schedule pressure cannot justify informal repair, undocumented concession, or shipment with incomplete records.

# 6. Controlled pilot

## Pilot selection

Select a product family with:

- Meaningful volume or lost-deal impact.
- A repeatable route.
- Sufficient historical data.
- Manageable quality risk.
- A clear suspected delay mechanism.
- Available Production, Planning, Engineering, and Quality support.

## Pilot structure

The pilot must define:

- Baseline period and comparison group where practical.
- Exact interventions.
- Affected orders and process version.
- Required training and document updates.
- Inspection and test capacity.
- Traceability method.
- Primary measures and quality guardrails.
- Stop conditions and rollback plan.

### Example experiments

| Hypothesis | Pilot change | Primary measure | Quality guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inspection waiting is excessive | Pre-book capacity and enforce readiness | Inspection queue hours | Repeat inspections, escapes |
| Incomplete orders create early delay | Mandatory completeness gate | Order-to-release time | Post-release changes |
| Shortages drive the critical path | Buffer selected common parts | Shortage hours | Inventory aging, supplier defects |
| Priority changes create queues | Freeze near-term schedule | Queue time, adherence | Customer-risk exceptions |
| Large batches delay completion | Smaller controlled lots | Order-to-ship time | FPY, setup time, traceability |
| Rework is critical-path loss | Correct top defect cause | Rework hours, FPY | NCR severity, warranty signals |

## Pilot exit criteria

Scale only if the pilot demonstrates:

- Lower median and P90/P95 lead time.
- Lower relevant queue, shortage, or rework time.
- Stable or improved first-pass yield.
- No increase in serious NCRs, escapes, repeat tests, or early-life failures.
- Complete quality records and traceability.
- No missed mandatory gates.
- Acceptable inventory, overtime, expedite, and quality costs.
- Successful layered-process audits.

A single fast order is not evidence of a capable process.

# 7. Alignment of Sales, Production, and Quality

## Shared operating model

| Function | Primary accountability |
|---|---|
| Sales | Customer communication and commercially complete order |
| Planning | Integrated schedule and finite-capacity promise date |
| Production | Execution, capacity feedback, and process adherence |
| Quality | Independent hold, inspection, test, release, and audit authority |
| Engineering | Technical release, design integrity, and approved changes |
| Procurement | Supplier commitments, recovery, and risk mitigation |
| Reliability | Warranty analysis and field-performance monitoring |

### Conflict-resolution rules

- Planning owns production sequencing.
- Sales cannot directly reprioritize the shop floor.
- Quality can stop or hold work when requirements are unmet.
- Engineering owns technical release and design changes.
- Procurement owns supplier recovery.
- The steering team resolves tradeoffs within the quality boundaries.
- One promise date is visible to every function and to the customer.

## Shared performance measures

Avoid isolated incentives such as Sales maximizing orders, Production maximizing utilization, or Quality maximizing inspection throughput. Use shared measures:

- Order-to-ship lead time.
- P90/P95 lead time.
- Quote accuracy.
- On-time delivery.
- First-pass yield.
- Rework and NCR rate.
- Warranty and escape rate.
- Material-shortage hours.
- Supplier performance.
- Expedite cost and frequency.
- WIP aging.

### Governance cadence

**Daily:** orders at risk, shortages, aged WIP, inspection queue, NCRs, rework, and constraint capacity.

**Weekly:** delay Pareto, pilot performance, quality guardrails, supplier recovery, and unresolved decisions.

**Monthly:** quote accuracy, competitive win/loss results, P90/P95 lead time, warranty, inventory, cost, audits, and scale decisions.

# 8. Phased implementation roadmap

## Phase 0 — Immediate containment and diagnosis: Weeks 0–2

- Establish governance and no-bypass rule.
- Define timestamps, statuses, and quality gates.
- Analyze order history and map the value stream.
- Investigate the previous warranty increase.
- Start daily reviews of shortages, inspection queues, NCRs, rework, and aged WIP.

**Output:** verified delay causes, constraint analysis, baseline metrics, risk register, and pilot charter.

## Phase 1 — Remove visible waiting: Weeks 3–6

- Enforce order completeness.
- Pre-book inspection capacity.
- Implement ready-for-inspection criteria.
- Improve shortage visibility and pre-staging.
- Stabilize the near-term schedule.
- Remove duplicate administrative queues where permitted.
- Correct the largest recurring rework cause.

**Gate:** measurable flow improvement with no deterioration in quality, reliability, traceability, or record completeness.

## Phase 2 — Controlled pilot: Weeks 7–12

- Use finite-capacity scheduling.
- Release only complete orders.
- Pilot a focused cell, smaller lots, postponement, or selective inventory.
- Implement supplier recovery plans.
- Use formal change control, training, and layered audits.

**Gate:** sustained improvement in flow and schedule performance with stable or improved quality and reliability.

## Phase 3 — Scale and revise quotes: Months 4–6

- Standardize proven changes by product family.
- Update routings, control plans, work instructions, and planning parameters.
- Formalize supplier agreements and inventory policies.
- Integrate demonstrated P90/P95 capability into quoting.
- Publish differentiated lead times for standard, configured, engineered-to-order, and special-risk products.

## Phase 4 — Sustain: Ongoing

- Review lead-time distributions monthly.
- Refresh inventory and capacity parameters.
- Monitor warranty and early-life reliability by product family and revision.
- Continue defect prevention and design-for-manufacture work.
- Audit adherence across shifts and sites.
- Retire temporary controls only after effectiveness is verified.

# 9. Measures of success

## Flow and commercial metrics

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Actual lead time | Shipment date minus commercially complete date |
| Quoted lead time | Promised shipment date minus agreed start date |
| P90/P95 lead time | High-percentile actual performance |
| Quote accuracy | Actual shipment date versus promise date |
| On-time delivery | Shipped on or before committed date |
| Lost deals due to delivery | Sales loss rate attributed to lead time |
| WIP aging | Time open orders remain in a status |
| Complete-release rate | Orders released with all required inputs |

## Delay metrics

- Queue hours by operation.
- Material-shortage hours.
- Supplier late hours.
- Inspection queue hours.
- NCR disposition age.
- Rework hours per unit.
- Priority changes per order.
- Expedites per order.
- Constraint downtime.
- Setup and changeover time.

## Quality and reliability metrics

- First-pass yield.
- NCRs per unit or order.
- Rework hours per unit.
- Repeat inspection and test rate.
- Escaped defects.
- Early-life failure rate.
- Warranty claims per defined exposure base.
- Calibration-related interruptions.
- Record and traceability completeness.
- Audit findings.

### Mandatory stop conditions

Pause an affected change and contain product if:

- A mandatory gate is skipped or lacks evidence.
- Traceability cannot be reconstructed.
- Calibration or measurement control is compromised.
- A serious NCR or customer escape occurs.
- First-pass yield, rework, repeat testing, or defect rates exceed approved limits.
- A new failure mode or adverse warranty signal appears.
- Unauthorized documents, materials, software, or process revisions are used.
- Required qualified personnel are unavailable.
- Records are incomplete at shipment.
- The improvement depends on unsustainable expediting, overtime, or manual workarounds.

Restart requires containment, root-cause analysis, impact assessment, corrective action, retraining, Quality and Engineering approval, and a controlled re-run.

---

## Final recommendation

The most likely route to a materially shorter quote is to remove non-value-adding elapsed time: incomplete order release, material shortages, supplier variability, internal queues, batch waiting, inspection scheduling gaps, unstable priorities, rework, and documentation delays.

The company should first improve flow around certified quality gates, not reduce the rigor of those gates. A six-week quote should be introduced only for product families that repeatedly demonstrate the required lead time at the appropriate percentile while maintaining certified compliance, traceability, first-pass yield, warranty performance, and complete release records.
