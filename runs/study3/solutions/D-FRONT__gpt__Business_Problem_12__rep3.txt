# Sustainable Tourism Development Plan  
## A Conservation-First Framework for International Visitor Growth

## Executive policy position

The region should pursue international tourism as a **managed outcome of conservation, community readiness, and infrastructure capacity—not as an objective that overrides them**.

Growth must remain within ecological, cultural, climate, water, housing, transport, and resident-wellbeing limits. The government should prioritize longer stays, higher locally retained spending, off-season travel, community ownership, and high-quality experiences rather than maximizing arrivals.

Because the region’s specific geography, laws, communities, visitor volumes, and infrastructure are not yet defined, this plan does not invent numerical carrying capacities or market forecasts. Permanent thresholds and targets must be established through a baseline program. Until that work is complete, precautionary controls apply.

---

# 1. Objectives and guiding principles

## Strategic objectives

The tourism agency should:

1. **Protect natural and cultural assets** and improve their condition where tourism has caused damage.
2. **Attract appropriate international visitors** through specialist nature, heritage, educational, cultural, and soft-adventure products.
3. **Increase local economic benefits** through employment, procurement, ownership, skills, and transparent benefit sharing.
4. **Protect resident wellbeing**, including housing, water access, public services, cultural rights, and access to customary resources.
5. **Reduce tourism’s resource use, emissions, and climate vulnerability.**
6. **Provide safe, accessible, respectful, and high-quality visitor experiences.**
7. **Create an accountable destination-management system** that can slow, redirect, suspend, or reverse growth when limits are reached.

## Guiding principles

- **Conservation and rights first:** Tourism cannot override ecological limits, heritage protection, customary rights, or essential resident services.
- **Precaution:** Irreversible development will not proceed where significant impacts are unknown.
- **Quality over volume:** Success is measured by visitor value, local benefit, and asset condition—not arrivals alone.
- **Community authority:** Affected rights-holders must have defined decision rights, not merely consultation opportunities.
- **Equity and inclusion:** Women, youth, workers, persons with disabilities, minorities, low-income residents, and customary users must be included.
- **Adaptive management:** Access, prices, marketing, investment, and operating conditions must change when evidence shows deterioration.
- **No greenwashing:** Sustainability, carbon, authenticity, and community-benefit claims must be evidence-based and verified.
- **Transparency and remedy:** Decisions, concessions, revenue, impacts, data, complaints, and corrective actions must be publicly reported.

# 2. Evidence and baseline program

A comprehensive destination baseline must be completed within **12 months**, with rapid assessments of pressured sites within **six months**.

## Required assessments

### Environment and climate

- Ecosystems, protected areas, ecological corridors, watersheds, coastlines, and scenic landscapes.
- Threatened species and breeding, nesting, spawning, or migration areas.
- Erosion, pollution, invasive species, wildlife disturbance, noise, and light.
- Water availability, ecological flows, seasonal scarcity, and climate hazards.
- Tourism-related emissions, including international and domestic transport where measurable.

### Heritage, culture, and rights

- Archaeological, architectural, religious, historical, and cultural assets.
- Cultural landscapes, living heritage, sacred sites, and restricted-access places.
- Heritage condition and visitor-related damage.
- Land tenure, Indigenous and customary rights, private ownership, and access rights.
- Cultural protocols governing access, photography, recording, interpretation, research, and commercial use.

### Communities and economy

- Tourism employment, wages, working conditions, seasonality, and management participation.
- Local procurement, ownership, revenue leakage, and business survival.
- Housing costs, rents, land prices, short-term rentals, second homes, and displacement.
- Access to water, sanitation, public space, health services, transport, and customary resources.
- Resident attitudes, crowding, safety, accessibility, and quality-of-life impacts.

### Visitors and markets

- Arrivals, visitor-nights, length of stay, spending, transport modes, seasonality, and geographic distribution.
- Accommodation capacity, occupancy, and service quality.
- Visitor satisfaction, complaints, online reputation, and safety incidents.
- International connectivity, visa and entry conditions, insurance, payment systems, language capacity, and market dependence.

## Evidence standards

The agency must use repeatable ecological and heritage methods, household and business surveys, water and wastewater testing, waste measurement, accessibility audits, emissions accounting, and independent review.

Communities and rights-holders must be able to review data affecting them. Indigenous and cultural information must be governed according to applicable rights, confidentiality, and data-sovereignty requirements.

Where data are incomplete or conflicting, the stricter precautionary control applies.

# 3. Governance and responsibility

## Destination Tourism Council

Within six months, government must establish a legally recognized **Destination Tourism Council**.

### Representation

The Council must include:

- tourism, environment, protected-area, heritage, planning, water, waste, housing, transport, labor, health, finance, and emergency authorities;
- local governments;
- Indigenous peoples, customary owners, and other rights-holders;
- community organizations;
- women, youth, workers, persons with disabilities, and marginalized groups;
- tourism businesses;
- conservation, heritage, research, and civil-society organizations.

Representatives must be selected through transparent processes. Government appointment alone must not be treated as community representation.

### Functions

The Council must:

- approve the destination strategy and annual implementation plan;
- approve site-management plans and destination visitor ceilings;
- coordinate tourism, land-use, infrastructure, housing, water, conservation, and heritage decisions;
- review major projects and concessions;
- oversee the Destination Benefit Fund;
- approve the official marketing calendar;
- authorize capacity reductions, marketing pauses, closures, restoration, and reopening;
- publish annual sustainability and financial reports.

The enabling legislation or delegation must clearly state which Council decisions are binding, delegated, or advisory.

## Destination Management Unit

The tourism agency must establish and fund a professional **Destination Management Unit** responsible for:

- visitor and business data;
- site-capacity management;
- product development;
- community and enterprise support;
- international marketing;
- operator standards;
- monitoring and reporting;
- finance coordination;
- crisis communications;
- coordination of inspections and enforcement.

## Responsibility framework

| Function | Lead responsibility | Required safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| Visitor limits and closures | Protected-area or heritage authority under Council policy | Published site plan, thresholds, permits, and closure rules |
| Major development approval | Statutory planning authority | Environmental, heritage, climate, water, housing, and social assessments |
| Water allocation | Water authority | Seasonal water budget and capacity-linked permits |
| Waste and wastewater | Utility or local authority | Service standards, maintenance plans, testing, and inspections |
| Concessions | Land, protected-area, or heritage authority | Transparent, time-limited agreements and performance conditions |
| Labor standards | Labor authority | Licensing conditions, inspections, and worker remedies |
| Housing and short-term rentals | Local government or housing authority | Zoning, registration, rental controls, and displacement monitoring |
| Visitor fees and levies | Finance authority | Fee regulation, public reporting, and audited fund |
| Marketing | Tourism agency and Council | Capacity- and emissions-linked marketing protocol |
| Complaints and remedy | Independent grievance office | Investigation, mediation, appeal, and remedy |
| Data governance | Management Unit with rights-holders | Privacy, cultural-data protection, validation, and public methodology |

Within nine months, government must publish the jurisdiction-specific legal basis, budget holder, enforcement authority, appeal route, and reporting obligation for each function.

# 4. Interim controls

Until the baseline and permanent thresholds are approved:

1. No major project may be approved in mapped sensitive, disputed, water-stressed, housing-stressed, or high-climate-risk areas.
2. International promotion of unassessed secondary sites is prohibited.
3. Pressured sites must operate under temporary visitor caps.
4. Fragile sites must use group-size, vehicle, route, seasonal, or guiding restrictions where risks are known.
5. New accommodation is prohibited where it could reduce reliable resident access to water, sanitation, housing, customary land, or essential services.
6. Major projects require preliminary environmental, heritage, climate, water, housing, and community screening.
7. Resident and customary access must be maintained.
8. Mass international promotion is prohibited until the progression gate in Section 11 is met.
9. Official claims such as “sustainable,” “nature-positive,” “community-based,” “low-impact,” or “carbon neutral” are prohibited until verification procedures operate.

### Provisional limits

Within six months, every pressured site must publish a provisional hourly and daily cap based on the lowest safe capacity indicated by:

- ecological or heritage stress;
- safe trail, road, facility, and evacuation capacity;
- water, wastewater, and waste capacity;
- resident and customary access;
- acceptable crowding;
- available ranger and enforcement coverage.

Each cap must identify its evidence, responsible authority, and review date. It may be tightened immediately and may not be relaxed without published evidence, independent review where appropriate, and Council approval.

# 5. Spatial planning and development controls

Within 18 months, authorities must map:

- no-go areas for tourism infrastructure;
- ecological corridors;
- breeding, nesting, spawning, and migration zones;
- water-source protection zones;
- sacred, private, fragile, and restricted cultural sites;
- sensitive heritage areas and viewsheds;
- low-, moderate-, and higher-intensity tourism zones;
- existing settlements and gateways suitable for infrastructure.

All projects must follow the mitigation hierarchy:

1. avoid impacts;
2. minimize unavoidable impacts;
3. restore damage;
4. use biodiversity offsets only as a last resort, never for irreplaceable habitats, sacred landscapes, or critically threatened species.

A **major project** must be defined by regulation using bed capacity, land area, capital value, resource use, proximity to sensitive assets, or environmental and social risk.

Major projects require:

- environmental impact assessment;
- heritage impact assessment;
- social and human-rights assessment;
- climate-risk assessment;
- alternatives and cumulative-impact analysis;
- water, wastewater, waste, housing, and land-access assessment;
- rights-holder participation and consent where required;
- monitoring, maintenance, restoration, and decommissioning plans;
- financial guarantees for restoration or decommissioning where risk warrants.

Infrastructure should be concentrated in existing settlements and gateways rather than dispersed into sensitive landscapes.

# 6. Visitor management

## Site-management plans

Every priority natural or heritage site must have an approved management plan within 18 months. A priority site is one selected using published criteria such as ecological or heritage importance, vulnerability, visitor pressure, community significance, or strategic value.

Each plan must define:

- conservation, heritage, and community objectives;
- ownership and governance;
- public, restricted, and no-access zones;
- maximum hourly and daily visitors;
- group-size and vehicle limits;
- routes, dwell times, and operating seasons;
- water, wastewater, waste, and emergency capacity;
- acceptable crowding;
- resident and customary access;
- monitoring, funding, maintenance, enforcement, and closure procedures.

## Traffic-light system

- **Green:** Normal access and approved promotion.
- **Amber:** Reduced marketing, timed entry, peak pricing, smaller groups, reduced hours, or other demand controls.
- **Red:** Closure, restoration, investigation, or mandatory demand reduction.

Triggers may include ecological or heritage deterioration, wildlife disturbance, water or wastewater failure, unsafe crowding, traffic or noise, resident-wellbeing deterioration, emergency-service overload, climate hazards, or public-health risks.

The tourism agency must reduce or suspend promotion when amber or red conditions apply.

## Destination ceiling

Within 18 months, the Council must establish a destination-level ceiling based on:

- total accommodation beds;
- seasonal visitor-nights;
- water and wastewater capacity;
- waste systems;
- housing and labor conditions;
- transport and emergency capacity;
- absolute tourism emissions;
- cumulative environmental and cultural impacts.

Visitor growth is permitted only while relevant indicators remain within approved limits.

# 7. Environmental, climate, and resource safeguards

## Biodiversity

The government must:

- prohibit wildlife feeding, harassment, extraction, and unauthorized handling;
- restrict drones, lighting, noise, off-road access, and wildlife proximity;
- apply seasonal closures;
- control invasive species through equipment-cleaning protocols;
- manage wildlife-vehicle collisions;
- prohibit damaging captive-wildlife encounters;
- fund rangers and repeat ecological monitoring;
- require licensed and trained operators in sensitive areas.

Protected-area tourism revenue must supplement, not replace, core public conservation funding.

## Water

Within 12 months, the water authority must prepare a seasonal water budget covering residents, agriculture, customary uses, ecological flows, drought scenarios, existing tourism, and proposed capacity.

New tourism capacity is prohibited where it would reduce reliable water access for residents or ecosystems.

Large facilities must:

- meter water use;
- meet water-use limits per visitor-night;
- manage leaks;
- use efficient fixtures;
- reuse suitable greywater;
- maintain drought plans;
- provide wastewater treatment and peak-season capacity;
- report consumption and treatment performance.

## Waste and wastewater

The destination must adopt this hierarchy:

1. eliminate;
2. reduce;
3. reuse and refill;
4. repair;
5. recycle and compost;
6. safely treat and dispose of residual waste.

Dumping, open burning, and uncontrolled disposal are prohibited. Recycling requirements must correspond to actual collection, treatment, markets, and safe final disposal.

Remote facilities must have a named operator, funded maintenance plan, inspection schedule, and contingency arrangements.

## Climate and emissions

Within 18 months, the agency must publish a tourism emissions inventory and climate-action plan covering, where measurable:

- international and domestic transport;
- accommodation;
- food;
- attractions;
- construction;
- waste;
- staff travel and supply chains.

The plan must establish:

- an absolute tourism-emissions ceiling or reduction pathway;
- emissions targets per visitor-night;
- transport and energy targets;
- climate-risk requirements for major projects.

International aviation emissions must be reported separately. Marketing must avoid incentives for short-break, high-frequency travel and must be reduced if the absolute emissions limit is exceeded.

The region must not claim carbon neutrality through offsets. Any offsets must be supplementary, independently verified, transparent, and never replace direct reductions.

Facilities made unsafe by climate change must be relocated, retired, or subject to managed retreat.

# 8. Heritage, rights, and cultural representation

## Rights and consent

Within 12 months, the agency must complete a rights and tenure assessment identifying Indigenous peoples, customary owners, private landholders, tenants, women’s rights, minority rights, and other affected rights-holders.

Where rights are affected, free, prior, and informed consent must be obtained where required by applicable law and standards. The process must provide:

- information in appropriate languages;
- independent legal and technical advice;
- adequate deliberation time;
- no coercion;
- documented decisions;
- benefit-sharing terms;
- dispute resolution and remedy;
- agreed review or withdrawal conditions.

Benefit sharing cannot substitute for consent or rights protection.

## Decision and conflict protocol

Within 12 months, the Council must publish a protocol distinguishing decisions requiring:

- FPIC;
- rights-holder approval;
- community approval;
- consultation only;
- statutory determination.

It must also provide for representative selection, inclusion of marginalized groups, internal disagreement, disputes between customary and elected authorities, cross-community impacts, independent mediation, appeal, remedy, and protection for dissenting voices. Consent must not be inferred from attendance, silence, or a single signature.

## Cultural control

Rights-holders may determine whether cultural sites, stories, designs, knowledge, ceremonies, and practices are:

- public;
- restricted;
- seasonal;
- paid;
- private;
- prohibited from tourism use.

Operators must obtain permission for photography, recording, livestreaming, research, and commercial use. Compulsory performances, staged sacred ceremonies, removal of sacred objects, illegal antiquities trading, and degrading representations are prohibited.

## Heritage conservation

Priority heritage assets must have conservation and visitor-management plans covering condition, preventive maintenance, visitor limits, archaeology, viewsheds, cultural landscapes, qualified restoration, disaster preparedness, security, and enforcement.

Inappropriate reconstruction, unauthorized alteration, “facadism,” and excessive commercialization are prohibited.

---

# 9. Community wellbeing and economic benefit

## Resident wellbeing and housing

The Council must establish thresholds for:

- housing affordability and rent increases;
- water reliability;
- sanitation and waste;
- traffic and noise;
- public-space access;
- safety;
- cost of living;
- access to customary lands and resources;
- resident satisfaction.

Tourism expansion must be restricted when these indicators exceed approved limits.

Local governments must regulate short-term rentals, second homes, land transfers, and resort development where they threaten resident housing or customary access.

## Fair work

Licensed businesses must comply with labor standards covering:

- fair wages;
- safe conditions;
- social protection;
- predictable scheduling where practicable;
- freedom from harassment and discrimination;
- child protection;
- prevention of forced labor and trafficking.

Monitoring must distinguish total local jobs from local skilled, management, permanent, fairly paid, and retained jobs.

## Local ownership and participation

The agency must support:

- community-owned visitor centers;
- guiding and transport cooperatives;
- homestays and small ecolodges;
- local food, farming, fishing, craft, and cultural enterprises;
- community-managed concessions;
- joint ventures with defined local equity or control;
- local booking and marketing platforms;
- management buy-in and long-term asset ownership.

Major concessions must include beneficial-ownership disclosure, maximum terms, local employment and procurement requirements, community revenue or equity, transparent subcontracting, audit rights, performance conditions, termination provisions, limits on exclusive access, and data-sharing obligations.

Within 18 months of baseline completion, the Council must set progressive targets for resident employment, skilled and management positions, local procurement, local ownership or equity, local value added, cultural payments, and locally retained spending.

## Destination Benefit Fund

Government must establish a publicly audited Benefit Fund financed through:

- entry and activity fees;
- accommodation or destination levies;
- concessions and licensing;
- parking and shuttle charges;
- government appropriations;
- conservation, heritage, and climate grants;
- voluntary contributions.

The allocation formula must be approved with community and rights-holder participation and published annually. Eligible uses include conservation, heritage maintenance, community water and sanitation, climate resilience, enterprise finance, training, and ranger services.

The Fund is additional to government responsibility for essential public services.

# 10. Products, markets, and marketing

## Priority products

Potential products include:

- guided nature and wildlife observation;
- managed hiking, cycling, kayaking, diving, and other soft adventure;
- heritage and archaeology circuits;
- museums and historical interpretation;
- local food and agricultural experiences;
- craft and storytelling activities;
- educational and research visits;
- multi-day nature-and-heritage itineraries.

No product may be internationally marketed until it meets legal, safety, environmental, heritage, cultural, accessibility, and local-benefit standards.

## Market selection

Specific markets must be selected after research assessing:

- transport connectivity and emissions;
- length of stay;
- local spending and leakage;
- seasonal complementarity;
- interest in nature and heritage;
- safety and insurance;
- visa and entry conditions;
- foreign-language and payment capacity;
- market diversification;
- compatibility with capacity and emissions limits.

The agency should prioritize specialist, educational, cultural, nature, soft-adventure, slow-travel, and responsible segments. It should avoid incentives for short-break, high-frequency aviation.

## Responsible marketing

The agency must:

- operate a multilingual official website;
- publish permits, capacity, prices, site conditions, transport, safety, accessibility, customs, and conservation rules;
- promote longer stays and off-season travel only where capacity permits;
- feature verified local and community-owned businesses;
- disclose conservation and community fees;
- use accurate imagery and local voices;
- provide visitor-conduct information before booking;
- link campaigns to the traffic-light system.

Official marketing must stop for sites that lack basic capacity, safety, water, waste, cultural safeguards, or monitoring.

Claims such as “sustainable,” “eco,” “nature-positive,” “community-based,” “authentic,” “low-impact,” and “carbon responsible” require documented evidence. Independent verification is mandatory for official certification and material claims.

# 11. Infrastructure, mobility, safety, and accessibility

## Investment priorities

Public investment must first address:

1. drinking water and sanitation;
2. waste collection and treatment;
3. electricity and digital connectivity;
4. health, emergency, and communications systems;
5. signage, toilets, shade, and visitor information;
6. low-impact transport and trails;
7. accessibility and accommodation upgrades.

Infrastructure should be concentrated in existing settlements and gateways.

## Mobility

Transport authorities must develop, where justified by evidence:

- public connections between gateways, towns, sites, and communities;
- shuttles at congested attractions;
- walking and cycling routes;
- low-emission vehicles;
- parking and vehicle-access controls;
- coordinated arrival schedules;
- accurate seasonal travel information.

## Accessibility

New public tourism infrastructure must meet universal-design standards. Exceptions must be written, time-limited, independently reviewed, publicly reported, and accompanied by an accessible alternative where full access is impossible.

Standards must cover physical, sensory, cognitive, and information access; websites and booking; transport; accommodation; toilets; paths; interpretation; emergency communications; and service animals.

Every promoted product must publish verified accessibility information, including limitations. Accessibility must be included in licensing, public funding, official marketing, and audits.

## Safety and public health

Every priority site and major operator must maintain an approved plan covering:

- evacuation routes and capacity;
- severe-weather and wildfire closure;
- communications redundancy;
- medical referral;
- visitor and staff accountability;
- outbreak and public-health response;
- insurance;
- business continuity;
- post-incident review.

---

# 12. Financing and investment safeguards

Within 12 months, the agency must prepare a costed investment program covering:

- baseline and monitoring;
- rangers and enforcement;
- heritage conservation;
- water, sanitation, and waste;
- transport and accessibility;
- skills and enterprise support;
- climate adaptation;
- digital systems and marketing;
- recurring maintenance;
- restoration and decommissioning.

Each project must identify:

- capital cost;
- annual operating and maintenance cost;
- expected revenue;
- confirmed and prospective funding;
- funding gap;
- responsible asset owner;
- lifecycle and decommissioning arrangements.

Core conservation, monitoring, enforcement, emergency response, and essential public infrastructure must have guaranteed public funding. Visitor revenue may supplement but must not be the sole source.

Green bonds, public-private partnerships, sustainability-linked finance, and impact investment may be used only where scale, repayment capacity, risk allocation, transparency, and public safeguards are demonstrated. Financing must include a reserve for closures, climate events, health emergencies, restoration, and revenue decline.

# 13. Phased implementation

## Phase 1: Foundation and protection — first 100 days to Year 2

**Lead:** Tourism agency and cross-government task force.

- Begin environmental, cultural, socioeconomic, rights, and visitor baselines.
- Establish interim visitor and development controls.
- Identify priority sites and pilot communities.
- Establish the Council, Management Unit, and independent grievance channel.
- Publish the project, concession, subsidy, and approval register.
- Audit water, wastewater, waste, housing, transport, emergency, accessibility, and revenue leakage.
- Publish the legal responsibility matrix.
- Prepare the costed financing program.
- Adopt visitor, operator, labor, accessibility, and claims standards.
- Complete rapid site assessments and provisional caps.
- Conduct market research without mass promotion.

**Progression gate:** No major promotional expansion or high-impact infrastructure may proceed until capacity controls, safety and basic services, rights-holder processes, funded monitoring, benefit sharing, grievance systems, accessibility information, and enforcement authority are operational.

## Phase 2: Pilots and capability — Years 2–4

- Approve site-management and conservation plans.
- Launch community-owned or community-equity products.
- Introduce reservations, permits, quotas, timed entry, and shuttles where required.
- Upgrade water, sanitation, waste, trails, visitor facilities, accessibility, and emergency systems.
- Implement labor, local-content, and concession requirements.
- Establish the Benefit Fund.
- Begin targeted international marketing in compliant markets.
- Publish the emissions inventory and climate-action plan.
- Regulate short-term rentals and high-risk development areas.

## Phase 3: Controlled expansion — Years 4–7

- Expand only products and sites meeting all thresholds.
- Improve public and low-emission transport.
- Develop multi-site and shoulder-season itineraries.
- Increase local ownership in accommodation, transport, booking, and guiding.
- Conduct independent audits every two to three years.
- Adjust prices, quotas, investment, and marketing based on evidence.

## Phase 4: Maturity and review — Years 7–10

- Review and revise capacity thresholds.
- Restore, restrict, or close deteriorating sites.
- Increase community equity and higher-value local participation.
- Expand conservation and climate finance.
- Relocate or retire unsafe infrastructure.
- Complete a ten-year evaluation and renew the strategy.

# 14. KPI framework

Numerical targets must be approved within 12 months after the baseline. Each target must identify a baseline, target year, responsible agency, measurement method, reporting frequency, funding source, and corrective action.

| Area | Priority indicators |
|---|---|
| Ecosystems | Condition of priority ecosystems; wildlife disturbance; erosion; invasive species; environmental violations; restoration outcomes |
| Heritage | Condition of priority sites; preventive maintenance; unauthorized access or damage; approved conservation plans |
| Water | Water use per visitor-night; resident water reliability; ecological flows; wastewater compliance |
| Waste | Waste per visitor-night; reduction, reuse, recycling, composting, and safe disposal; remote-system coverage |
| Climate | Absolute tourism emissions; emissions per visitor-night; international aviation emissions; renewable energy; climate-risk compliance |
| Community | Resident satisfaction; housing and rent trends; customary access; local employment, wages, skilled jobs, management, ownership, and procurement |
| Economy | Visitor-nights; average length of stay; off-season share; local spending retained; business survival; revenue leakage |
| Visitor experience | Crowding; satisfaction; safety incidents; accessibility compliance; responsible-operator use |
| Governance | Complaints and resolution time; published concessions and revenues; inspections; violations; corrective-action completion |

## Data governance and enforcement

The Management Unit must publish methodologies, data-quality standards, uncertainty ranges, and annual results. Cultural and Indigenous data must be governed according to rights-holder requirements.

Within 12 months, authorities must publish an enforcement plan covering staffing, geographic coverage, inspections, licensing, evidence standards, penalties, restoration orders, appeals, whistleblower protection, and annual costs.

When a threshold is exceeded, the responsible authority must:

1. publish the evidence;
2. identify the cause;
3. reduce access, capacity, or marketing;
4. fund mitigation or restoration;
5. consult affected rights-holders;
6. set a recovery timetable;
7. report whether and when normal operations can resume.

Reopening requires evidence of recovery and functioning monitoring and enforcement.

# 15. Intended outcome

Within ten years, the region should have a tourism system in which:

- ecosystems and heritage assets are stable or improving;
- visitor-nights remain within enforceable limits;
- residents retain housing, essential services, land, and cultural access;
- local people hold a larger share of tourism employment, ownership, and income;
- tourism work is safe and fairly compensated;
- visitors receive safe, accessible, respectful, and uncrowded experiences;
- tourism emissions, water use, waste, and climate vulnerability are declining;
- communities and government can redirect, suspend, or reduce tourism growth when limits are reached.

The central policy commitment is clear: **international tourism may grow only when the region’s environmental, cultural, social, infrastructure, and emissions limits allow it—and growth must stop or reverse when those limits are exceeded.**
