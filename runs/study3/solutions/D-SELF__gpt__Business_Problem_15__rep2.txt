# Global Future-of-Work Framework

## Executive summary

A global company should create a flexible work system that supports business performance, customer service, employee wellbeing, inclusion, and resilience without imposing one universal attendance model.

The framework should establish:

- **Global minimum standards** for rights, inclusion, accessibility, safety, privacy, security, fair opportunity, and employee voice.
- **Local implementation** that reflects country law, culture, infrastructure, labor markets, and business requirements.
- **Task- and outcome-based work design** rather than reliance on job titles, visibility, or office attendance.
- **Meaningful flexibility for all employees**, including site-based and shift workers.
- **Purposeful offices** designed for collaboration, learning, customer work, specialized activities, and employees without suitable home environments.
- **Transparent decisions**, supported by evidence, documented criteria, review dates, and appeal routes.
- **Responsible technology and data use**, including safeguards for monitoring and artificial intelligence.
- **Evidence-based measurement and continuous improvement**, with minimum standards that cannot be traded away for cost or productivity gains.

The objective is not to maximize remote work or office attendance. It is to design work deliberately around tasks, outcomes, people, customers, and local realities.

---

# 1. Strategic objectives

The company should design work to:

1. Improve customer, operational, and financial performance.
2. Provide flexibility in location, schedule, predictability, autonomy, and temporary adjustments.
3. Ensure fair opportunity regardless of country, role, work location, disability, language, caregiving status, or employment arrangement.
4. Protect health, safety, privacy, security, and sustainable working hours.
5. Enable effective collaboration across time zones, cultures, and languages.
6. Use offices, technology, and real estate intentionally.
7. Attract and retain talent while strengthening resilience and local capability.
8. Hold leaders accountable without relying on surveillance or presenteeism.

---

# 2. Global minimum standards

All countries, business units, and teams must uphold these standards:

1. Equal opportunity and non-discrimination.
2. Accessibility and reasonable accommodation.
3. Health, safety, rest, and sustainable working hours.
4. Privacy, data minimization, and lawful monitoring.
5. Cybersecurity and information protection.
6. Fair, outcome-based performance management.
7. Equivalent access to development, recognition, promotion, and leadership exposure.
8. Transparent work-model decisions.
9. Employee consultation, review, and appeal.
10. Protection from retaliation.
11. Responsible use of workplace technology and artificial intelligence.
12. Appropriate dignity, support, and protection for remote, hybrid, site-based, mobile, shift-based, contingent, and office-based workers.

Local policies may provide stronger protections where required by law or local practice, but may not weaken these standards.

Each requirement should be classified as:

- **Mandatory global standard.**
- **Mandatory local implementation requirement.**
- **Required process with locally adjustable content.**
- **Recommended practice.**
- **Optional example.**

# 3. Guiding principles

## 3.1 Design work around outcomes and tasks

Employees should be assessed on agreed outcomes, quality, customer impact, judgment, collaboration, risk management, learning, and capability-building—not office visibility, online status, webcam use, or keystroke activity.

Work arrangements should be based on actual tasks rather than job titles alone. A role may contain both location-dependent and location-independent activities.

Each role or team should define:

- Core tasks and expected outcomes.
- Activities requiring physical presence.
- Service coverage and response requirements.
- Collaboration and time-zone dependencies.
- Security and confidentiality needs.
- Equipment and workplace requirements.
- Development and relationship-building needs.
- Available flexibility.
- Review date and accountable owner.

Measures must reflect the nature of the work. Volume or activity metrics must not substitute for quality, judgment, risk prevention, relationships, or long-term contribution.

## 3.2 Flexibility is broader than remote work

Flexibility may involve:

- **Location:** home, office, approved hub, customer site, or another permitted location.
- **Schedule:** start and finish times, compressed hours, or shifts where lawful.
- **Predictability:** advance notice and control over schedules.
- **Autonomy:** discretion over how work is organized.
- **Temporary adjustments:** support for disability, health, caregiving, religious, safety, or personal circumstances.

For site-dependent and shift-based employees, flexibility may include:

- Predictable schedules.
- Advance notice of changes.
- Shift swaps or self-scheduling.
- Adjusted or compressed hours.
- Part-time or job-share options.
- Predictable overtime.
- Paid time for mandatory training.
- Remote completion of eligible administrative work.
- Equivalent access to learning and advancement.

## 3.3 Global consistency with local implementation

Countries and regions may adapt implementation to reflect:

- Employment, tax, immigration, payroll, and social-security law.
- Working-time and right-to-disconnect requirements.
- Works councils, unions, and employee-representation obligations.
- Data-residency and privacy rules.
- Local infrastructure and connectivity.
- Cultural and religious practices.
- Customer and operational requirements.
- Local labor-market, office, and community conditions.

Local custom may shape implementation but may not justify presenteeism, excessive availability, discrimination, inaccessible systems, or unequal career opportunity.

## 3.4 Decisions must be transparent and reviewable

No employee should gain or lose flexibility solely because of manager preference, seniority, personal influence, or informal norms.

Every material work-model decision must document:

- The specific task, risk, customer requirement, or operational dependency.
- Why a particular location or schedule is necessary.
- The minimum frequency, duration, and timing required.
- Evidence supporting the requirement.
- Alternatives considered.
- Accessibility, employee, and equity impacts.
- Approval authority.
- Review date and success criteria.

Employees must receive a clear explanation and have access to confidential review, accommodation, and appeal channels.

## 3.5 Offices should enable valuable activities

Offices should be used intentionally for:

- Team formation and relationship-building.
- Coaching, mentoring, and learning.
- Innovation and creative problem-solving.
- Customer and partner engagement.
- Confidential or highly secure work.
- Specialized equipment and controlled environments.
- Community and social connection.
- Employees without suitable home-working conditions.

Attendance should have a clear purpose and should not be required simply to demonstrate commitment or enable supervision.

## 3.6 Inclusion and accessibility are design requirements

Universal design should apply to offices, digital platforms, documents, meetings, authentication, learning, and social activities.

Individual accommodations should supplement—not replace—accessible default systems. Employees should not be required to disclose more medical information than necessary to assess a work-related need.

## 3.7 Equivalent opportunity, not identical arrangements

Employees will not always have identical work arrangements, benefits, or flexibility. They should nevertheless have equivalent access to:

- Information and decision-making.
- Development and mentoring.
- Recognition and sponsorship.
- Strategic assignments.
- Promotion and compensation progression.
- Leadership exposure.
- Safe and dignified working conditions.
- Employee voice.

The company must actively monitor and address proximity bias.

## 3.8 Collaboration must be time-zone and culturally fair

Teams should use asynchronous work for routine coordination and reserve live meetings for activities that genuinely require synchronous interaction.

Global teams should:

- Define only essential overlap hours.
- Rotate inconvenient meeting times.
- Protect local rest periods.
- Publish agendas and materials in advance.
- Record decisions, owners, and deadlines.
- Use plain language.
- Translate critical communications.
- Provide captions, transcripts, and accessible materials.
- Prevent important decisions from occurring only in informal conversations.
- Give regional teams sufficient authority to act without constant headquarters approval.

## 3.9 Security and privacy must be risk-based

Security controls should reflect the sensitivity of work and information, not simply whether someone works remotely.

Employee monitoring must be lawful, necessary, proportionate, transparent, and purpose-limited. Continuous webcam use, keystroke monitoring, emotion detection, and online-status surveillance are not default performance tools.

## 3.10 Sustainable performance requires healthy boundaries

Teams must define:

- Normal working hours.
- Urgent and non-urgent communication.
- Response-time expectations.
- Escalation routes.
- Meeting-free periods.
- Rules for after-hours contact.
- Coverage and handoff arrangements.

Coverage must be achieved through staffing, handoffs, rotation, and escalation design—not assumed employee availability outside lawful working hours.

# 4. Global-versus-local design rules

## 4.1 Global responsibilities

Global functions set:

- Principles and minimum standards.
- Core privacy, security, accessibility, and AI controls.
- Approved technology architecture.
- Measurement definitions and minimum floors.
- Enterprise governance and escalation routes.
- Common templates and employee protections.

## 4.2 Country and regional responsibilities

Countries and regions determine:

- Permitted work locations.
- Working-time and rest practices.
- Expense and reimbursement arrangements.
- Cross-border work rules.
- Consultation and collective-labor processes.
- Local benefits and support.
- Data and employment-law requirements.
- Infrastructure and office solutions.
- Site-based flexibility commitments.

## 4.3 Team and business responsibilities

Business units and teams determine:

- Customer and operational coverage.
- Task and role design.
- Collaboration rhythms.
- Required in-person activities.
- Response times and handoffs.
- Onboarding and development practices.
- Local office scheduling.

## 4.4 Local variation process

Every material local variation must include:

- A documented rationale.
- Legal and employee-relations review where required.
- An accessibility and equity impact assessment.
- An accountable owner.
- An implementation date.
- A review date.
- Communication to affected employees.

Disputes between global and local authorities should escalate from the manager to the business unit, country HR/legal, regional governance, and the global Future of Work Council.

# 5. Work-model architecture

## 5.1 Work Design Passport

Each role or team should maintain a **Work Design Passport** containing:

- Primary work model.
- Core tasks and expected outcomes.
- Task-level location requirements.
- Required in-person activities.
- Schedule and coverage requirements.
- Collaboration and time-zone dependencies.
- Security classification.
- Equipment and technology needs.
- Local legal constraints.
- Accessibility considerations.
- Available flexibility.
- Approval authority.
- Evidence supporting the arrangement.
- Review date and success criteria.

Use:

- A **lightweight passport** for standard, low-risk roles.
- A **detailed passport** for regulated, site-dependent, mobile, security-sensitive, cross-border, or highly interdependent roles.

The Passport is an operational design document, not a permanent employment-status label.

## 5.2 Work-model categories

| Model | Description |
|---|---|
| **Remote-capable** | Most work can be completed away from a company site, subject to country, security, and team requirements. |
| **Hybrid** | Work is divided among home, office, customer sites, hubs, or other approved locations. |
| **Site-dependent** | Regular physical presence is required for equipment, safety, operations, customers, confidentiality, or regulation. |
| **Mobile or distributed** | Work occurs across customer, field, regional, or multiple company locations. |
| **Shift or coverage-based** | Work is organized around service coverage, fixed schedules, or operational handoffs. |
| **Mixed-task** | Different activities within the role have different location or schedule requirements. |

These categories describe the primary operating model and must not prevent task-level flexibility.

## 5.3 Evidence test for attendance or location requirements

Any non-voluntary office or site-attendance requirement must:

1. Identify the specific task, risk, customer requirement, or operational dependency.
2. Explain why remote, asynchronous, local-hub, or alternative arrangements are insufficient.
3. Define the minimum frequency, duration, and timing required.
4. Provide relevant operational, safety, security, quality, or customer evidence.
5. Distinguish evidence from assumptions.
6. Consider accessibility, caregiving, commute, infrastructure, and equity effects.
7. Evaluate alternatives and reasonable accommodations.
8. Obtain approval from the designated authority.
9. Set a review date, success measures, and reassessment conditions.

Tradition, visibility, managerial preference, or general assumptions about culture are not sufficient evidence.

## 5.4 Decision test for competing needs

When principles conflict:

1. Identify all legal, rights, safety, accessibility, security, operational, and employee factors.
2. Eliminate unlawful, unsafe, or discriminatory options.
3. Seek reasonable and accessible alternatives.
4. Apply necessity and proportionality.
5. Select the least burdensome effective arrangement.
6. Document residual impacts and mitigation.
7. Obtain the required approval.
8. Review the outcome against agreed success measures.

Accommodation and inclusion obligations remain active throughout the process.

# 6. Recommendations for work models

## Remote-capable knowledge work

Use remote-first or hybrid arrangements where tasks, infrastructure, security, and team needs permit.

Requirements should include:

- Clear outcomes and decision rights.
- Deliberate onboarding and mentoring.
- Purposeful in-person connection.
- Documented decisions.
- Protection against proximity bias.
- Suitable equipment, connectivity, and support.

## Global and distributed teams

Use:

- Limited core overlap.
- Asynchronous status reporting.
- Rotating meeting times.
- Written decision records.
- Regional decision authority.
- Translation and accessible communications.
- Defined handoffs and response times.

## Site-dependent, laboratory, manufacturing, and specialist roles

Require physical presence only for activities that genuinely depend on:

- Equipment.
- Safety.
- Controlled environments.
- Customer or operational coverage.
- Confidentiality.
- Regulation.

Allow remote completion of eligible analysis, documentation, training, planning, or administrative tasks.

## Shift and coverage-based work

Provide locally defined standards for:

- Schedule notice.
- Last-minute changes.
- Shift swaps.
- Overtime predictability.
- Adjusted schedules.
- Rest periods.
- Mandatory training.
- Development and promotion access.

## Mobile, field, sales, and customer-facing work

Provide:

- Secure mobile technology.
- Approved-location and travel guidance.
- Customer-site safety standards.
- Expense and commuting policies.
- Flexible administrative work.
- Local autonomy within security and legal requirements.

## New hires and early-career employees

Combine distributed flexibility with deliberate connection through:

- Preboarding.
- Structured introductions.
- Buddy or mentor support.
- Early team connection.
- Documented learning plans.
- Regular feedback.
- Access to informal and formal development.

# 7. Offices and workplace strategy

## 7.1 Portfolio decisions

Office closures, consolidations, or redesigns must assess:

- Utilization by site, day, and workspace type.
- Financial, capital, and lease implications.
- Customer and government relationships.
- Local recruitment and talent access.
- Business continuity and resilience.
- Accessibility and safety.
- Infrastructure reliability.
- Specialized facilities.
- Community and supplier effects.
- Employee travel and transition impacts.
- Environmental performance.
- Capacity for employees without suitable home-working conditions.

Office decisions should not be based solely on occupancy or short-term cost.

## 7.2 Office design

Offices should provide an appropriate mix of:

- Quiet and focused work areas.
- Team project spaces.
- High-quality hybrid meeting rooms.
- Training and learning facilities.
- Confidential rooms.
- Accessible workstations.
- Sensory-considerate spaces.
- Reliable connectivity and power.
- Secure areas for sensitive work.
- Community and social spaces.

Booking systems must be simple, transparent, accessible, and separate from performance monitoring. Capacity should be reserved for accommodations, essential work, confidential activities, and employees without suitable home conditions.

## 7.3 Home and alternative workplaces

Where remote or hybrid work is approved, local standards should address:

- Equipment and technical support.
- Connectivity.
- Ergonomics and safe working conditions.
- Confidentiality and data handling.
- Reimbursement or allowances where required.
- Approved coworking or local-hub access.
- Low-bandwidth and offline alternatives.

The company should not assume that employees have a private room, reliable electricity, high-speed internet, or suitable furniture.

## 7.4 Workplace investment priorities

Investment should be prioritized in this order:

1. Legal, safety, accessibility, privacy, and security requirements.
2. Critical operational continuity.
3. Technology and infrastructure reliability.
4. Employee inclusion and equitable access.
5. Collaboration and workplace improvements.
6. Cost and environmental optimization.

# 8. Collaboration tools and digital workplace

The company should maintain a controlled, accessible tool ecosystem for:

- Messaging.
- Meetings.
- Documents.
- Project management.
- Knowledge management.
- Employee communications.
- HR, IT, facilities, and support services.

Minimum standards should include:

- Shared documents and searchable records.
- Advance agendas and materials.
- Decision records.
- Captions and transcripts where appropriate.
- Accessible documents.
- Low-bandwidth alternatives.
- No default continuous-video requirement.
- Support in relevant languages.
- User testing with employees using assistive technologies.
- Interoperability for approved local tools.

Technology adoption should be measured by reliability, accessibility, user experience, and business effectiveness—not login volume.

## Local technology exceptions

Local tools may be approved where required by law, infrastructure, language, accessibility, government restrictions, or market availability. Each exception must assess:

- Security.
- Privacy and data transfer.
- Accessibility.
- Retention.
- Interoperability.
- Vendor reliability.
- Support capacity.
- Exit or migration arrangements.

# 9. Employee experience, inclusion, and wellbeing

## 9.1 Employee journey

The work model should be integrated into:

- Recruitment and realistic job previews.
- Equipment and technology setup.
- Onboarding.
- Team integration.
- Learning and development.
- Performance and feedback.
- Recognition and reward.
- Internal mobility.
- Leave and life events.
- Return from extended leave.
- Exit.

Job descriptions should state actual location expectations, travel, core hours, flexibility boundaries, equipment requirements, and country eligibility.

## 9.2 Accessibility and accommodation

Provide:

- Accessible physical and digital workplaces.
- Assistive technology.
- Accessible authentication.
- Captions, transcripts, and interpretation.
- Screen-reader-compatible systems.
- Quiet and sensory-considerate environments.
- Confidential specialist support.
- Interim arrangements where possible.
- Defined decision timelines.
- Appeal and non-retaliation protections.

Accommodation decisions should be based on functional work needs and should not require unnecessary medical disclosure.

## 9.3 Caregiving and life-stage needs

Support should address:

- Childcare.
- Eldercare.
- Disability-related family support.
- New parenthood.
- Emergency care.
- Religious obligations.
- Return from extended leave.
- Other significant life events.

Managers must not assume caregivers are less committed or that constant availability demonstrates performance.

## 9.4 Language and cultural inclusion

Critical policies, safety instructions, accommodation processes, benefits information, major workplace changes, mandatory training, and incident communications should be available in relevant local languages.

Communications should use:

- Plain language.
- Accessible formats.
- Advance materials.
- Local validation.
- Version control.
- A process for correcting translation errors.

Employees should not be disadvantaged because they operate in a second or third language.

# 10. Performance, careers, rewards, and employment status

## 10.1 Performance management

Performance systems should focus on:

- Agreed outcomes.
- Quality and reliability.
- Customer and stakeholder impact.
- Collaboration and inclusion.
- Risk and compliance.
- Learning and adaptability.
- Leadership behaviors where relevant.

Managers should not use individual attendance, activity, webcam, or online-status data as default performance evidence.

## 10.2 Career equity

The company should monitor whether work location affects:

- Performance ratings.
- Promotion.
- Compensation progression.
- Access to mentoring.
- High-visibility assignments.
- Strategic projects.
- Leadership exposure.
- Informal sponsorship.

## 10.3 Compensation, expenses, and benefits

The company should maintain transparent principles for:

- Location-based pay.
- Relocation and cross-border work.
- Remote-work allowances.
- Equipment and connectivity reimbursement.
- Commuting and travel costs.
- Site-based premiums or schedule compensation.
- Country-specific benefits.
- Payroll and tax treatment.
- Notice and consultation before material changes.

Approved flexibility or accommodations should not adversely affect pay, performance evaluation, promotion, or benefits unless a lawful and transparent reward policy requires a difference.

Material location-related changes require appropriate legal review, employee communication, and consultation where required.

## 10.4 Contractors and contingent workers

The framework applies, as appropriate under local law and contractual arrangements, to:

- Contractors.
- Agency workers.
- Temporary employees.
- Interns.
- Outsourced service personnel.
- Third parties accessing company systems.

Relevant workers must receive appropriate standards for:

- Health and safety.
- Cybersecurity.
- Privacy.
- Accessibility.
- Required equipment and support.
- Training.
- Non-discrimination.
- Reporting concerns.

Supplier contracts must define responsibility for equipment, accessibility, incident response, privacy, security, and compliance.

# 11. Governance, employee voice, and accountability

## 11.1 Decision rights

| Level | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Global** | Principles, minimum standards, core tools, privacy, security, AI governance, metrics, and equity requirements |
| **Region/country** | Lawful local policies, consultation, tax, immigration, working time, data, infrastructure, and employee relations |
| **Business unit** | Operating needs, role profiles, customer coverage, workforce planning, and site-based flexibility commitments |
| **Team** | Collaboration rhythms, in-person activities, coverage, onboarding, and response expectations |
| **Manager** | Applying approved arrangements, setting outcomes, supporting inclusion, documenting decisions, and escalating issues |
| **Employee** | Following agreed work, security, safety, and communication practices and raising constraints promptly |

## 11.2 Future of Work Council

The council should include HR, employee relations, legal, privacy, cybersecurity, technology, facilities, finance, procurement, risk, accessibility, regional leadership, and employee representatives where applicable.

It should:

- Approve global standards.
- Review local variations.
- Oversee office, technology, monitoring, and AI changes.
- Sponsor pilots.
- Monitor equity and business outcomes.
- Publish major decisions and rationale.
- Escalate disputes.
- Trigger event-based reviews.

## 11.3 Employee voice and appeals

Major changes should involve appropriate combinations of:

- Surveys.
- Focus groups.
- Employee-resource groups.
- Accessibility and caregiver networks.
- Unions.
- Works councils.
- Employee advisory groups.
- Representative pilots.
- Anonymous reporting.

The company must distinguish among informing, consulting, negotiating, and obtaining consent. Local law and collective agreements determine the applicable requirement.

For material changes, publish:

- The rationale.
- Evidence considered.
- Feedback received.
- Changes made in response.
- Recommendations not adopted and why.
- Available review and appeal routes.

# 12. Privacy, security, and responsible technology

## 12.1 Security controls

Controls may include:

- Multifactor authentication.
- Managed, encrypted devices.
- Identity-based access.
- Secure configuration and patching.
- Data-loss prevention.
- Access segmentation.
- Secure printing and physical-document practices.
- Incident reporting.
- Proportionate monitoring.

Security controls must be tested for accessibility, low bandwidth, field use, and local infrastructure constraints.

## 12.2 Data classification and restrictions

The company should distinguish among:

- Security and compliance data.
- Facilities-planning data.
- Operational data.
- Employee-experience data.
- Restricted individual data.
- Prohibited surveillance data.

Planning and experience data should be aggregated with minimum group-size thresholds, role-based access, defined retention periods, audit logs, and clear employee notice.

The company must not:

- Rank employees by activity.
- Infer commitment from presence.
- Use meeting attendance as a proxy for performance.
- Export individual activity data to managers for informal evaluation.
- Repurpose data without renewed legal, privacy, employee-relations, and governance approval.

## 12.3 AI and algorithmic systems

Any AI or algorithmic system used for recruitment, scheduling, performance, workforce planning, sentiment analysis, promotion, meeting analysis, or productivity measurement must undergo:

- Privacy and legal review.
- Bias and disparate-impact testing.
- Accessibility testing.
- Human oversight.
- Explainability and employee-notification review.
- Data-retention assessment.
- Vendor and cross-border data review.
- Defined approved and prohibited uses.
- Correction and challenge processes.

Prohibited or highly restricted uses include:

- Emotion inference from faces, voices, or keystrokes.
- Automated ranking of commitment or productivity from activity data.
- Fully automated accommodation decisions.
- Fully automated disciplinary recommendations.
- Unreviewed AI-generated performance ratings.
- Use of unapproved public AI tools with confidential company or employee data.

AI must not be the sole basis for hiring, promotion, discipline, termination, accommodation, or performance decisions.

Maintain an AI use-case register with risk classification, approval authority, review date, and reassessment triggers.

# 13. Business continuity and emergency flexibility

The company should maintain contingency procedures for:

- Natural disasters.
- Civil unrest.
- Public-health emergencies.
- Cyber incidents.
- Power or internet outages.
- Office closures.
- Border restrictions.
- Regional safety threats.

Plans should include:

- Alternate hubs and worksites.
- Temporary remote-work permissions.
- Offline and phone-based processes.
- Employee safety checks.
- Crisis communication channels.
- Emergency equipment and connectivity support.
- Rapid approval pathways.
- Minimum security controls.
- Payroll, insurance, immigration, and tax escalation.
- Post-event review and remediation.

Emergency exceptions must have:

- An approving authority.
- Minimum controls.
- A maximum duration.
- A reassessment date.
- Employee communication requirements.
- A documented post-event review.

# 14. Service levels and accountability

Each country should publish service standards for:

| Process | Required information |
|---|---|
| Standard work-model approval | Owner, approval authority, response target, escalation route |
| Accommodation request | Confidential owner, interim support, decision target, appeal route |
| Cross-border work review | Legal, tax, immigration, security, and insurance owners |
| Technology support | Severity-based response and workaround process |
| Work-model appeal | Independent reviewer and response target |
| Country-playbook update | Review owner, legal review date, publication process |
| AI or monitoring review | Privacy, legal, security, accessibility, and employee-relations approval |
| Employee communication | Owner, language requirements, and publication timeline |

Global functions should define minimum service expectations; countries may provide more protective or responsive standards.

Managers must document material decisions, participate in calibration, complete required training, and correct inequitable patterns identified through audits.

# 15. Measurement and success measures

No single metric should determine policy. Measures should be segmented by comparable role, country, work model, and relevant employee groups, subject to privacy law.

Every metric must have:

- A definition.
- A data owner.
- A baseline.
- A target or acceptable range.
- A minimum floor where relevant.
- A reporting frequency.
- Privacy limitations.
- An investigation trigger.
- An accountable decision-maker.
- A remediation deadline.

Minimum floors should apply to safety, accessibility, privacy, security, legal compliance, employee rights, and equity. Strong financial results must not compensate for failure against these standards.

## Business and operational outcomes

- Customer satisfaction.
- Quality and error rates.
- Delivery and cycle times.
- Service-level performance.
- Project and innovation outcomes.
- Decision speed.
- Absence and regretted attrition.
- Business-continuity performance.

## Employee experience

- Clarity of priorities.
- Trust and manager effectiveness.
- Belonging.
- Workload sustainability.
- Technology satisfaction.
- Onboarding quality.
- Intent to stay.
- Confidence in raising concerns.

## Equity and inclusion

- Promotion and pay progression.
- Access to learning and strategic assignments.
- Performance-rating patterns.
- Attrition.
- Accommodation timeliness and outcomes.
- Leadership exposure.
- Meeting participation.
- Remote versus office career outcomes.
- Access to flexibility by role and demographic group.

## Workplace and technology

- Occupancy by site and day.
- Utilization by workspace type.
- Accessibility performance.
- Booking reliability.
- Technical-support resolution.
- System availability.
- Tool duplication.
- Security and privacy incidents.
- Travel, energy, and emissions.

## Sustainable work

- Aggregated working-hour patterns.
- After-hours communications.
- Schedule volatility for site-based workers.
- Burnout and workload indicators.
- Sick leave trends.
- Use and quality of support resources.

Metrics should trigger inquiry and improvement, not automatic punishment. High office attendance may indicate inadequate home infrastructure, while low meeting attendance may reflect effective asynchronous work.

# 16. Implementation roadmap

## Phase 1: Diagnose

Establish a baseline through:

- Task and role analysis.
- Employee research and representative consultation.
- Country legal, tax, immigration, and employee-relations review.
- Office utilization and infrastructure data.
- Technology, accessibility, privacy, and security audits.
- Workload and wellbeing measures.
- Career-equity analysis.
- Financial and environmental assessment.

## Phase 2: Design

Create:

- Enterprise policy and global minimum standards.
- Country playbooks.
- Work Design Passports.
- Team-agreement templates.
- Manager decision guides.
- Accommodation and appeal processes.
- Workplace and technology standards.
- Privacy, data, and AI governance.
- Compensation and expense principles.
- Measurement definitions and service levels.
- Emergency-flexibility procedures.
- Communications plans.

## Phase 3: Pilot

Test varied models across:

- A distributed knowledge team.
- A site-dependent operation.
- A lower-connectivity market.
- A highly regulated country.
- A location with significant accessibility or caregiving needs.
- A redesigned office or regional hub.
- A contingent or mixed-workforce environment.

Each pilot should define success measures, employee safeguards, baseline data, review points, and criteria for scaling, modifying, or ending the pilot.

## Phase 4: Scale

Scale through:

- Local implementation plans.
- Required consultation.
- Manager enablement.
- Technology and workplace readiness.
- Employee champions.
- Accessible support channels.
- Published decisions and privacy notices.
- Audits of manager application and equity outcomes.

## Phase 5: Review and adapt

Review at least annually and after:

- Material legal or regulatory changes.
- Security or privacy incidents.
- New monitoring or AI deployments.
- Major office closures or consolidations.
- Significant equity disparities.
- Acquisitions or restructuring.
- Major infrastructure or public-health disruption.
- Material changes in technology, workforce composition, or customer requirements.

# Recommended enterprise policy position

> The company will maintain globally consistent standards for inclusion, accessibility, privacy, security, health, safety, fair opportunity, sustainable work, responsible technology, and employee voice. Within those standards, work will be designed according to the tasks performed, customer and operational requirements, local law, infrastructure, team interdependence, and individual circumstances. Flexibility may involve location, schedule, predictability, autonomy, or workload—not remote work alone. Decisions must be evidence-based, documented, reviewable, and free from proximity bias. Local adaptation is expected where it improves legal compliance, inclusion, business resilience, or customer effectiveness, but local custom may not override global rights and standards.

This framework provides a common global foundation without imposing a uniform attendance model. It gives leaders and employees practical decision tools, protects opportunity and wellbeing, recognizes meaningful regional and job-type differences, and establishes clear mechanisms for local adaptation, accountability, governance, and continuous improvement.
