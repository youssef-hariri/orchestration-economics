# Global Future-of-Work Framework

## Executive position

The company will design work around the activities, outcomes, dependencies, risks, and people involved—not around a universal office-attendance target.

Flexible work will be supported where compatible with role requirements, customer commitments, collaboration needs, security, wellbeing, and applicable local requirements. Where remote work is not practical, the company will provide meaningful alternative flexibility and comparable access to information, development, recognition, and career opportunity.

**Global standards establish the minimum expectations. Countries, regions, businesses, and teams may adapt implementation to legitimate legal, cultural, operational, and workforce conditions, but may not lower core safeguards for dignity, inclusion, accessibility, privacy, security, fair performance management, or employee voice.**

This framework is an enterprise operating model covering workforce planning, workplaces, technology, management, employee experience, risk, and performance.

---

# 1. What the framework is designed to achieve

The future-of-work model should strengthen:

- Business and customer performance
- Employee wellbeing, autonomy, and belonging
- Inclusion and accessibility
- Collaboration, innovation, and learning
- Talent attraction, development, and retention
- Security, privacy, and business resilience
- Effective and purposeful use of workplaces and technology
- Fair access to opportunity across countries, roles, and work patterns

Success is not defined by where employees work or how often they are visible. It is defined by sustainable business performance, equitable employee experience, effective collaboration, and responsible risk management.

# 2. Global guardrails and local adaptation

## 2.1 Global standards

The following are global minimum standards and apply across the company:

### Fairness and inclusion

- No employee will be disadvantaged in performance assessment, promotion, compensation, development, recognition, or strategic assignments because of an approved work arrangement.
- Work decisions must be evidence-based, transparent, consistently applied, and reviewable.
- Employees in office-, site-, field-, and frontline-based roles must receive comparable access to information, development, recognition, support, and career opportunity.
- Accessibility and reasonable-adjustment processes must be confidential, timely, funded, and accessible.

### Performance and leadership

- Performance will be managed through outcomes, quality, customer impact, collaboration, and role-relevant behavior.
- Office presence, camera use, message volume, keystrokes, or perceived availability must not be used as substitutes for performance evidence.
- Leaders and managers are accountable for preventing proximity bias and applying work arrangements consistently.

### Privacy, security, and technology

- Workforce data will be collected proportionately, transparently, securely, and for defined purposes.
- Covert productivity surveillance, emotion inference, and individual ranking based on digital activity are prohibited.
- AI and algorithmic tools affecting employees require appropriate privacy, security, accessibility, bias, legal, and human-oversight controls.
- Core information-security and data-protection requirements apply regardless of work location.

### Employee voice and review

- Employees must receive clear information about role classification, available flexibility, decision criteria, and review routes.
- Material work-model, workplace, and technology changes require appropriate employee consultation and representative engagement.
- Employees must be able to raise concerns, request adjustments, and appeal decisions without retaliation.

### Health, safety, and sustainable performance

- Work design must protect health, safety, reasonable working-time boundaries, workload sustainability, and psychological wellbeing.
- The company must not transfer material business costs or unreasonable risks to employees without appropriate support and local review.

## 2.2 Local and regional adaptation

Local or regional leaders may adapt:

- Attendance and scheduling practices
- Working-time arrangements
- Office opening hours and facilities
- Employee consultation methods
- Communication channels, languages, and cadence
- Cultural, religious, and public-holiday considerations
- Local benefits, expenses, and wellbeing resources
- Customer, operational, labor, and regulatory practices

Each material local variation must document:

1. The business or employee need being addressed  
2. Evidence supporting the variation  
3. Consultation undertaken  
4. Groups that may be advantaged or disadvantaged  
5. Confirmation that the approach does not rely on cultural or nationality-based stereotypes  
6. Applicable legal, tax, labor, privacy, immigration, safety, and regulatory review  
7. A review date and success measures  

Local adaptation may change how a standard is implemented; it may not remove core protections.

# 3. Principles for designing work

## Principle 1: Start with work, not location

For each role and activity, determine:

- Required outcomes and service levels
- Activities requiring physical presence
- Activities benefiting materially from co-location
- Activities suitable for remote or asynchronous work
- Dependencies across teams, customers, facilities, and time zones
- Security, safety, regulatory, and continuity requirements

Terms such as “collaboration,” “culture,” or “visibility” must not be used as general reasons for office attendance without identifying the specific work requirement and considering viable alternatives.

## Principle 2: Flexibility must be equitable

Remote work is only one form of flexibility. Depending on the role, options may include:

- Predictable schedules and rosters
- Shift choice or shift swapping
- Staggered start and finish times
- Compressed or part-time schedules
- Job sharing
- Flexible leave
- Phased returns
- Greater control over breaks where operationally feasible
- Occasional location flexibility
- Reduced or more predictable travel

Fairness does not require identical arrangements. It requires legitimate, transparent decisions and comparable access to opportunity and support.

## Principle 3: Manage for outcomes, not proximity

Managers should set clear objectives, review progress, provide timely feedback, and address performance concerns directly. They should not equate office attendance with commitment or remote work with reduced contribution.

Talent processes should explicitly test for proximity bias by examining:

- Access to high-value assignments
- Participation in important meetings
- Sponsorship and mentoring
- Recognition and visibility
- Quality of performance evidence
- Promotion and succession outcomes

## Principle 4: Make collaboration intentional

In-person time is most valuable for:

- Relationship and trust building
- Onboarding and team formation
- Coaching and development
- Complex problem-solving
- Innovation and creative work
- Sensitive discussions
- Cross-functional alignment
- Community and culture building

Remote or asynchronous work is often effective for:

- Focused individual work
- Analysis and preparation
- Documentation
- Routine coordination
- Distributed collaboration
- Work requiring proximity to customers or local markets

Teams should select the format that best supports the activity. A fully virtual or fully in-person session may sometimes be more effective and inclusive than a poorly designed hybrid meeting.

## Principle 5: Design for inclusion and accessibility by default

Accessibility should be built into:

- Offices, transportation, and workplace services
- Collaboration platforms and meeting rooms
- Recruitment, onboarding, learning, and communications
- Scheduling across time zones
- Assistive technology
- Home-working support
- Emergency and health-related arrangements
- AI and workforce analytics

Employees should not have to repeatedly disclose sensitive information or rely solely on an individual manager’s discretion to obtain an enduring adjustment, where a lawful and practical alternative exists.

## Principle 6: Protect trust, privacy, and security

Controls should be proportionate to the sensitivity of information, criticality of systems, work location, and threat environment.

The company will favor:

- Strong identity and access management
- Managed devices and secure connectivity
- Encryption and data classification
- Secure collaboration channels
- Physical privacy controls
- Data-loss prevention
- Training and support
- Transparent, privacy-conscious analytics
- Risk-based controls for sensitive work

## Principle 7: Preserve belonging and sustainable performance

Flexible work must not create isolation, excessive working hours, fragmented culture, or reduced learning. Teams should deliberately provide:

- Connection and belonging
- Onboarding and informal learning
- Mentoring and sponsorship
- Knowledge sharing
- Recognition
- Career conversations
- Conflict resolution
- Workload reviews
- Clear availability and response-time expectations

# 4. Work-pattern architecture

Work-pattern categories describe work requirements, not employee value or permanent status.

| Category | Definition | Typical considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **Remote-compatible** | Core activities can generally be completed away from company premises | Approved location, security, connectivity, time zone, customer needs |
| **Hybrid** | The role benefits from both remote and in-person work | Specific activities requiring co-location, team dependencies, customer needs, local context |
| **Office-, site-, or field-based** | Regular physical presence is required | Equipment, customers, physical materials, safety, regulated environments, operations |
| **Location- or time-zone-constrained** | Work must be performed in a particular jurisdiction, approved location, or time zone | Tax, immigration, licensing, customer, security, regulatory, or operational requirements |
| **Individually adjusted** | An alternative arrangement is required for a documented accessibility, health, caregiving, religious, safety, or other relevant need | Confidential process, reasonable adjustment, review and escalation |

Classifications should be reviewed when technology, customers, team structures, regulations, security risks, or work activities materially change.

# 5. Work-arrangement decision framework

Every major role or team decision should use a documented assessment.

## 5.1 Decision criteria

| Area | Questions to answer |
|---|---|
| **Business and customer effectiveness** | Can required outcomes, quality, responsiveness, and service levels be achieved? |
| **Work dependencies** | Are equipment, physical materials, secure environments, customer contact, or operational handoffs required? |
| **Collaboration** | Which activities require real-time interaction, co-location, coaching, onboarding, or innovation? |
| **Employee experience** | Is the arrangement predictable, manageable, healthy, and understandable? |
| **Inclusion and accessibility** | Does it provide equitable access to information, opportunity, meetings, technology, and adjustments? |
| **Security, privacy, and resilience** | Are data, systems, customers, and continuity appropriately protected? |
| **Legal and regulatory feasibility** | Have local employment, labor, tax, immigration, privacy, safety, consultation, and sector requirements been reviewed? |
| **Cost and resources** | What are the workplace, travel, equipment, connectivity, support, and environmental implications? |
| **Talent strategy** | What are the effects on attraction, retention, internal mobility, skills access, and career equity? |

Employee preference should be considered seriously but balanced against role requirements, team needs, fairness, risk, and customer commitments.

## 5.2 Required decision record

Each decision should identify:

- Proposed work pattern
- Specific activities and evidence supporting it
- Acceptable alternatives
- Required controls and conditions
- Inclusion and accessibility impacts
- Risks and mitigations
- Accountable decision owner
- Review date
- Circumstances that would trigger reconsideration

Employees should be told how their role was classified, what flexibility is available, who made the decision, and how to request review, appeal, or adjustment.

## 5.3 Decision hierarchy

When factors conflict, decisions should generally be considered in this order:

1. Legal, safety, security, regulatory, and critical customer requirements  
2. Role and operational requirements  
3. Team coordination and service needs  
4. Employee wellbeing, accessibility, and preference  
5. Cost and workplace-utilization considerations  

This hierarchy does not remove the obligation to seek less restrictive alternatives, mitigate burdens, or explain decisions transparently.

# 6. Equity across the workforce

## 6.1 Office-, site-, field-, and frontline-based roles

Each business with site-dependent work should maintain an **equivalent flexibility and opportunity plan** covering:

- Schedule predictability and notice periods
- Shift choice and shift swapping
- Breaks and recovery
- Transportation and commuting safety
- Equipment and facilities
- Paid access to training and consultations
- Career development and internal mobility
- Recognition and leadership access
- Communication for employees without regular desk or email access
- Employee voice and escalation
- Feasible forms of occasional flexibility

These provisions must address practical needs rather than provide symbolic alternatives to remote work.

## 6.2 Remote and hybrid roles

The company should define responsibility for:

- Equipment and connectivity
- Ergonomic and health-and-safety support
- Approved work locations
- Secure handling of information
- Travel and office-access costs
- Business continuity during power or connectivity disruption
- Confidentiality in shared or public environments
- Access to suitable company premises when home working is not practical

Material business costs must not be transferred to employees without appropriate support and local review.

## 6.3 Contingent and partner workforces

Where the company controls the work environment or materially affects working conditions, it should establish appropriate baseline expectations for contractors, agency workers, vendor staff, seasonal workers, and other contingent populations, including safety, dignity, information access, and reasonable scheduling.

# 7. Cross-cultural and global collaboration

The company should not assume that one model of professionalism, engagement, communication, hierarchy, punctuality, or relationship-building is universal.

## Global non-negotiables

- Respectful conduct and dignity
- No discrimination or retaliation
- Clear accountability
- Accessible information
- Reasonable response-time expectations
- Transparent decision records
- Safe and lawful working conditions
- Effective routes for disagreement and escalation

## Locally adaptable practices

- Meeting etiquette and formality
- Use of local languages
- Feedback and communication style
- Social and relationship-building practices
- Holiday and religious-calendar planning
- Manager communication cadence
- Consultation methods
- Team rituals

Managers should be trained to distinguish cultural or communication differences from performance concerns. Silence, disagreement, camera use, directness, punctuality, office presence, and after-hours availability should not be interpreted through stereotypes.

## Time-zone standards

Global teams should:

- Establish limited, predictable overlap windows
- Rotate inconvenient meeting times
- Document decisions and actions
- Use asynchronous updates and shared workspaces
- Record or summarize important meetings
- Distinguish essential from optional attendance
- Track recurring out-of-hours meetings
- Provide alternatives when time-zone burdens become uneven or unsustainable

Accessibility and wellbeing should be treated as design constraints requiring mitigation, not merely as employee preferences.

# 8. Team and leadership practices

## 8.1 Team work charters

Each team should maintain a concise, co-created charter covering:

- Team outcomes and responsibilities
- Role and location dependencies
- Collaboration windows
- Response-time expectations
- Meeting and documentation norms
- Purpose of in-person activities
- Focus-time and availability expectations
- Inclusion and accessibility practices
- Escalation routes
- Review date

## 8.2 Manager accountability

Managers are accountable for:

- Clear objectives and priorities
- Consistent application of work arrangements
- Outcome-based feedback
- Equitable access to visibility and development
- Inclusive meetings and information sharing
- Workload and wellbeing monitoring
- Appropriate handling of adjustment requests
- Privacy-conscious management
- Early resolution of conflict and performance concerns

Manager capability should be assessed through practical training, team outcomes, employee experience, workload, fairness, and inclusion measures. Repeated inconsistent treatment, retaliation, covert surveillance, or proximity-based disadvantage should trigger escalation and corrective action.

## 8.3 Performance and talent controls

Talent processes should include checks on whether:

- Employees had comparable access to strategic assignments
- Important meetings and decisions were accessible
- Feedback was based on documented outcomes
- Location affected sponsorship or recognition
- Approved flexibility influenced ratings, promotion, pay, or succession decisions

# 9. Workplace strategy

## 9.1 Define the purpose of each workplace

Each office or site should have an explicit purpose, such as:

- Customer and stakeholder engagement
- Collaboration and innovation
- Onboarding and learning
- Community and culture
- Specialized, secure, or operational work
- Employee services
- Flexible workspace

Office investment should be based on purpose and business, employee, resilience, accessibility, and sustainability outcomes—not attendance alone.

## 9.2 Inclusive workplace design

Workplaces should provide an appropriate mix of:

- Focus and quiet areas
- Team and hybrid meeting rooms
- Confidential spaces
- Accessible workstations
- Sensory-friendly areas
- Learning and social spaces
- Storage and lockers
- Secure or specialized environments
- Locally relevant caregiving, prayer, reflection, or wellbeing facilities

## 9.3 Major workplace decisions

Before relocation, consolidation, or closure, the company should assess:

- Customer and operational continuity
- Accessibility and commuting impact
- Transportation safety
- Employee consultation and local requirements
- Talent and socioeconomic effects
- Cost, capital, and lease implications
- Environmental impact
- Alternative workspace availability
- Transition support

Each major site decision should have a documented business case showing alternatives considered, peak demand, explicit site purpose, employee impact, investment requirements, and measures of success unrelated to attendance alone.

# 10. Technology, privacy, security, and AI

## 10.1 Technology standards

The company should provide an integrated, supported ecosystem for:

- Secure access and identity
- Video, audio, and messaging
- Document and knowledge management
- Project and workflow management
- Digital whiteboarding
- Accessibility and assistive technology
- Language and translation support where appropriate
- Device management and technical support

Minimum technology standards should address:

- Reliability and availability
- Local support hours and escalation
- Regional platform access
- Accessibility conformance
- Low-bandwidth and offline alternatives
- Data residency and cross-border transfer
- Recording and transcript controls
- Retention and deletion
- Information classification
- Vendor resilience

## 10.2 Hybrid meetings

Important meetings should include:

- Reliable audio and video
- Visible remote participants
- Shared digital materials
- An identified facilitator
- Clear turn-taking norms
- Captions or other accessibility features where appropriate
- Documented decisions and actions
- No material side conversations unavailable to remote participants

## 10.3 Remote-work security

Remote-work standards should define:

- Approved location categories
- Device and network requirements
- Household and public-space privacy expectations
- Secure printing, storage, and disposal
- Power and connectivity disruption procedures
- Incident reporting
- Confidentiality requirements
- Ergonomic and health-and-safety support

Where an employee cannot safely or securely work from home, the company should provide an appropriate alternative rather than assuming the burden rests with the employee.

## 10.4 AI and workforce analytics

The company should maintain a permitted-use and prohibited-use matrix for employee-related AI and analytics.

### Prohibited or unacceptable uses

- Covert productivity surveillance
- Emotion or sentiment inference for employment decisions
- Individual ranking based on keystrokes, message volume, camera use, or similar activity
- Automated employment decisions without meaningful human review
- Use of employee data to train external systems without appropriate authorization and safeguards

### Uses requiring formal review

- Role classification
- Flexibility approval
- Performance or promotion recommendations
- Workforce planning
- Meeting transcription and summaries
- Workplace analytics
- Employee sentiment or wellbeing analysis

Reviews should assess privacy, security, bias, accessibility, explainability, data retention, human accountability, employee notice, and challenge or appeal rights.

---

# 11. Employee voice, adjustments, and appeals

The company should provide accessible channels for deskless, shift-based, multilingual, remote, office-based, and disabled employees.

Employee-voice standards should include:

- Consultation before material work-model, workplace, or technology changes
- Paid participation time where appropriate
- Confidential escalation and non-retaliation protections
- Publication of major feedback themes and management responses
- Representation from affected workforce segments
- A documented explanation where material feedback is not adopted

Adjustment processes should include:

- Clear request channels
- Acknowledgment and decision timelines
- Interim support where appropriate
- Confidential information handling
- Independent escalation
- Defined funding responsibility
- No repeated disclosure of enduring needs where lawful and practical
- Periodic review of systemic accessibility barriers

# 12. Governance and accountability

| Level | Primary accountability |
|---|---|
| **Global Future-of-Work Council** | Principles, minimum standards, enterprise metrics, material exceptions, major investments, cross-functional trade-offs |
| **Enterprise program owner** | Integrated implementation plan, dependencies, risks, reporting, and escalation |
| **Regional and country bodies** | Local feasibility, consultation, legal and regulatory readiness, local implementation |
| **Business leaders** | Work-activity analysis, customer and operational requirements, resources, and outcomes |
| **Managers** | Team charters, consistent application, outcome management, inclusion, workload, and employee support |
| **Employees** | Following agreed practices, protecting information, raising risks, and participating in reviews |

The Global Future-of-Work Council should include HR, business operations, workplace and real estate, technology, security, privacy, legal, tax, finance, risk, inclusion and accessibility, regional leadership, and employee or representative voices where appropriate.

## Mandatory local-readiness review

Before rollout in a country or region, the accountable local owner must obtain documented review of:

- Employment and labor requirements
- Collective arrangements and representative engagement
- Tax, payroll, social insurance, and benefits
- Immigration and work authorization
- Privacy and cross-border data transfers
- Health and safety
- Sector-specific regulation
- Workplace relocation or closure requirements
- Expenses, equipment, and recordkeeping

Unresolved issues must have an owner, mitigation, risk acceptance authority, and implementation decision.

# 13. Implementation priorities

## Phase 1: Establish the foundation

- Appoint an executive sponsor and enterprise program owner
- Confirm global non-derogable standards
- Establish governance and decision rights
- Map work activities, roles, dependencies, and workforce segments
- Baseline business, employee, workplace, technology, inclusion, and risk measures
- Identify high-risk legal, tax, privacy, accessibility, and security issues

## Phase 2: Build operating standards

- Implement the role-classification methodology
- Create decision records and review processes
- Define remote-work, frontline-equivalent, accessibility, technology, AI, and employee-voice standards
- Establish local-readiness and cross-border review processes
- Create team-charter and hybrid-meeting templates
- Define equipment, connectivity, travel, and workplace-cost responsibilities
- Develop manager capability and accountability measures

## Phase 3: Pilot representative models

Pilot across differences in:

- Countries and regulatory environments
- Time zones and languages
- Job types and work patterns
- Office, site, field, and deskless populations
- Accessibility needs
- Customer and operational requirements
- Employee-representative contexts

Evaluate customer outcomes, quality, workload, inclusion, retention, cost, risk, and learning—not attendance alone.

## Phase 4: Scale with controls

- Publish employee and manager guidance
- Complete local consultation and readiness reviews
- Implement approved technology and workplace changes
- Provide equipment, connectivity, travel, and transition support
- Monitor disparities and unintended consequences
- Escalate issues through defined governance channels

## Phase 5: Review and improve

- Review quarterly during initial implementation and at least annually thereafter
- Reassess role classifications and local adaptations
- Review workplace and technology investments
- Audit proximity bias, accessibility, employee voice, and manager consistency
- Retire ineffective practices
- Share effective local innovations while maintaining global guardrails

# 14. Success measures

Use a balanced scorecard. Metrics should be segmented by role, region, work pattern, level, and legally appropriate demographic categories, with privacy-preserving aggregation.

| Outcome | Illustrative measures |
|---|---|
| **Business and customer performance** | Service levels, customer satisfaction, quality, rework, project delivery, innovation, continuity |
| **Collaboration and culture** | Decision speed, meeting effectiveness, documentation quality, onboarding, belonging, psychological safety |
| **People and sustainability** | Engagement, retention, recruitment, mobility, learning access, workload, overtime, after-hours communication, absence |
| **Fairness and accessibility** | Flexibility approval patterns, adjustment processing times, promotion, pay, ratings, recognition, strategic assignments, accessibility defects |
| **Workplace and technology** | Utilization by purpose and peak period, workplace suitability, technology reliability, support resolution, tool duplication, connectivity gaps |
| **Risk and compliance** | Security and privacy incidents, AI errors and challenges, local-review completion, health and safety, audit findings, exceptions |
| **Manager effectiveness** | Team outcomes, fairness indicators, workload, inclusion, employee experience, completion of practical capability requirements |

For every metric, define:

- Owner
- Purpose
- Data source
- Frequency
- Privacy threshold
- Baseline
- Target or tolerance
- Escalation trigger
- Corrective action

Metrics must not be used to rank individuals by office presence, keystrokes, message volume, camera use, or other weak proxies for contribution.

# 15. Executive approval priorities

The first-year priorities should be to:

1. Approve the global position and non-derogable standards.
2. Appoint accountable executive and enterprise program owners.
3. Establish the evidence-based role-classification and decision-record process.
4. Define equivalent flexibility and opportunity standards for frontline and site-based roles.
5. Implement accessibility service levels and confidential adjustment processes.
6. Establish remote-work cost, security, and approved-location standards.
7. Create employee-voice, consultation, review, and appeal mechanisms.
8. Integrate the framework with workforce planning, budgeting, real estate, technology, compensation, talent, and resilience planning.
9. Establish AI, analytics, surveillance, and meeting-data safeguards.
10. Launch representative pilots and measure business, employee, inclusion, cost, and risk outcomes.
11. Publish quarterly executive reporting and conduct an annual framework review.

## Enterprise policy statement

> The company enables flexible work where compatible with the activities, outcomes, dependencies, risks, customer commitments, and applicable local requirements of the work. Work arrangements are not determined by a universal attendance quota. Decisions must be transparent, evidence-based, consistently applied, inclusive, accessible, and reviewable. Where remote work is not practical, the company will provide other meaningful forms of flexibility and comparable access to opportunity. Approved work arrangements will not disadvantage employees in performance assessment, development, recognition, compensation, or promotion. Technology and workforce data will be used proportionately, transparently, securely, and with appropriate human oversight. Global standards provide the foundation, while local teams adapt implementation to legal, cultural, operational, and workforce conditions.
