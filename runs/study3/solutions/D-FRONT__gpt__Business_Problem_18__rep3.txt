# Heritage Repositioning Strategy  
## Earned Trust, Made Useful Now

## 1. Executive recommendation

The brand should **not try to look young**. It should make 70 years of earned trust valuable in a specific contemporary situation.

### Strategic direction

> **Preserve the product promise, quality, and distinctive recognition that earned trust. Change the product experience, access, occasions, and expression that make the brand feel outdated.**

This is a **meaning expansion**, not a wholesale identity replacement.

The brand’s history is not the consumer benefit. It matters when it proves a capability that creates value now—such as reliable performance, category expertise, consistent quality, or responsible improvement.

### What the brand will do

- Target a specific younger growth audience rather than “youth” generally.
- Lead with a present-day consumer benefit.
- Make at least one meaningful product, format, access, or service improvement before making broad relevance claims.
- Use heritage as supporting proof, not nostalgia.
- Protect the loyal customer’s trusted experience while modernizing delivery and expression.
- Test the full experience—product, packaging, creative, retail, service, and economics—before scaling.

### What the brand will not do

- Mimic youth slang, memes, creators, or internet behavior.
- Make older customers the joke or portray them as obsolete.
- Use younger casting as the primary evidence of relevance.
- Present a logo, color, or campaign style as innovation.
- Use heritage without a current consumer benefit.
- Make unsupported claims about sustainability, inclusion, innovation, or cultural relevance.
- Optimize for engagement while trial, repeat, margin, or trust deteriorate.

### Internal platform

> **Proven by time. Useful for life now.**

This is an organizing platform—not yet a validated consumer-facing line.

---

## 2. What is known—and what remains to be proven

### Confirmed facts

- The brand is approximately 70 years old.
- It has high awareness, strong distribution, and trust among older customers.
- Consumers under 35 perceive it as outdated.
- Under-35 purchase share is declining.
- Previous modernization campaigns were mocked as inauthentic.
- Loyal customers were briefly alienated.
- The brand has a genuine heritage story.

### Working assumptions

The following are hypotheses, not findings:

1. The principal issue is contemporary relevance rather than price, product, category decline, access, or distribution.
2. Younger consumers know the brand but lack a current reason to choose it.
3. A meaningful product, format, service, or access improvement can be delivered.
4. Loyal customers are more attached to quality and performance than to every existing visual asset.
5. The earlier backlash was mainly caused by performative communications.
6. The brand’s heritage includes a distinctive capability that remains relevant today.
7. Existing distribution can become contemporary discoverability and purchase access.
8. Younger occasional users and aware non-users are more winnable than broad youth culture.

These assumptions must be tested before final positioning, national packaging changes, or broad rollout.

## 3. Audience strategy

“Under 35” is a planning boundary, not a sufficient target. Phase 0 must validate audience size, category participation, unmet need, conversion, repeat, channel access, and profitability.

### Provisional primary audience

> **Younger occasional users and aware non-users who value dependable category performance but need the brand to fit current routines, formats, expectations, or occasions.**

This is the first-wave hypothesis because it builds on existing brand permission rather than asking the brand to become a general youth-culture participant.

### Supporting audiences

| Audience | Need or tension | Strategic role |
|---|---|---|
| Younger occasional users | Want dependable performance but need easier, more relevant use in current routines | Primary growth audience |
| Aware younger non-users | Know the brand but associate it with another generation | Recruitment |
| Lapsed younger users | Left for convenience, value, format, or relevance | Win-back |
| Users of newer competitors | Want current benefits without sacrificing performance | Selective conquest only |
| Loyal customers | Want quality, familiarity, and trusted performance preserved | Retention, advocacy, and validation |
| Retail and channel partners | Need evidence of recruitment potential without damage to velocity or margin | Commercial enabler |
| Employees and service teams | Need clarity on what is changing and what is not | Delivery and credibility |

### Working consumer tension

> “I want something that works and fits my life now, but I do not want to trade reliability for novelty or spend time figuring out whether a newer option will deliver.”

This remains a hypothesis.

### Phase 0 audience decision

Select one primary and one secondary audience based on:

- Size and growth potential.
- Category participation.
- Severity of unmet need.
- Brand permission.
- Expected conversion and repeat.
- Channel accessibility.
- Incremental margin potential.

## 4. Positioning

### Approved strategic territory

The brand should seek to own the intersection of:

1. **Earned trust**
2. **Practical current usefulness**
3. **Demonstrable improvement**

### Positioning architecture

The final position must specify:

- **Target:** Which younger segment?
- **Situation:** In which occasion or need state?
- **Tension:** What is inadequate about current choices?
- **Promise:** What specific benefit will the brand deliver?
- **Heritage permission:** What capability has the brand earned?
- **Current proof:** What product or experience improvement makes the promise true?
- **Competitive contrast:** Why choose this brand over relevant alternatives?

### Draft positioning statement

> For **[validated younger segment]** in **[priority occasion]**, **[Brand]** provides **[specific current benefit]** by combining its proven **[heritage capability]** with **[specific current product, format, service, or access improvement]**. Unlike **[relevant alternative or category convention]**, it delivers **[distinctive choice advantage]** without asking consumers to trade away reliability.

The bracketed elements cannot be filled responsibly until research identifies the actual opportunity.

### Candidate territories for testing

These are hypotheses, not approved claims:

1. **Dependable performance, made easier**  
   Reliable results in a more convenient or accessible experience.

2. **A proven choice for a current occasion**  
   Bringing the brand into a relevant use case it does not currently own.

3. **Experience-earned improvement**  
   Applying accumulated expertise to a meaningful current standard or need.

Select one only when it produces a clear choice advantage and is supported by the product or experience.

### Messaging hierarchy

1. **Current consumer benefit:** Why this matters now.
2. **Product or experience delivery:** What makes the benefit real.
3. **Heritage proof:** Why the brand is credible.
4. **Continuity reassurance:** What loyal customers can expect to remain.
5. **Action:** What to buy, how to use it, where to find it, and what changed.

Younger audiences should receive current-need and product-fit messaging. Loyal customers should receive continuity, quality, and improvement reassurance. The brand truth should be shared; executions do not need to be identical.

## 5. Reasons to believe

Final proof must use verified facts. Candidate proof areas include:

### Heritage proof

- A documented founding innovation.
- A distinctive production or quality capability.
- A relevant standard maintained over time.
- Longstanding expertise in solving the category problem.
- A genuine guarantee or service commitment.
- Evidence of continuous improvement.

### Current utility proof

- A new or improved format.
- Greater convenience or portability.
- A product improvement tied to the priority occasion.
- Easier product navigation.
- Better retail or digital access.
- A more suitable price-pack architecture.
- A demonstrable performance improvement.

### Proof-before-promise map

| Claim | Required evidence | Consumer-visible proof | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proven performance | Current or historical performance evidence | Demonstration, testing, review, certification | Product/quality |
| Fits current routines | Product, format, access, or service improvement | Pack, tutorial, use case, retail execution | Product/marketing |
| Thoughtful improvement | Specific change and rationale | Plain explanation of what changed | Innovation/brand |
| Responsible behavior | Verified operational action | Documentation and product evidence | Operations/legal |
| Continuity | Stable quality, product, or service standards | “What remains” communication and core availability | Brand/service |

### Mandatory heritage rule

> No heritage asset may appear unless it explains or substantiates a current consumer benefit.

Archive material must answer: **What does this prove about the brand today?**

## 6. Preserve, evolve, test, and retire

The following are decisions about asset types. Actual brand-asset decisions require recognition and equity research.

| Element | Preserve | Evolve | Test | Retire or reduce |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product promise | Core quality, safety, performance, signature experience | How benefits are delivered and explained | New formats, features, occasions | Unsupported superiority claims |
| Name and master mark | Name and distinctive recognition assets | Digital flexibility and application | Limited motion and packaging treatments | Wholesale replacement without evidence |
| Signature visual cues | High-recognition colors, shapes, symbols, pack cues | Cleaner hierarchy and composition | Secondary palettes, type, imagery, motion | Decorative clutter that blocks choice |
| Heritage | Specific, truthful origin and capability | Link directly to current benefit | Archive-led versus current-proof-led stories | Generic anniversary nostalgia |
| Tone | Trustworthiness and characteristic personality | More conversational accessibility | Humor and informality | Slang, memes, forced youth language |
| Packaging | Recognition, core-product continuity, quality cues | Navigation, benefit hierarchy, format clarity | New sizes, bundles, limited editions | Changes that reduce recognition |
| Portfolio | Core products that drive trust and volume | Assortment clarity and architecture | Innovations under the master brand | Variants without a clear role |
| Retail presence | Core shelf presence and availability | Findability and occasion-based merchandising | Secondary displays and new contexts | Displays unsupported by product or stock |
| Partnerships | Genuine expertise and relevant relationships | Broader credible representation | Creators, experts, communities | Reach-only celebrity or influencer deals |
| Customer relationships | Service, loyalty, advocacy | Digital feedback and onboarding | Referral and community programs | Treating loyal customers as an obstacle |

### Asset audit

Before major identity or packaging change, score each asset on:

- Recognition.
- Distinctiveness.
- Trust contribution.
- Age coding.
- Relevance contribution.
- Commercial importance.
- Replacement risk.
- Legal or operational necessity.

Only the product promise, quality and safety standards, signature experience, truthful heritage, and a small number of proven distinctive assets should be presumed non-negotiable.

Every other asset should receive a formal decision:

- Preserve unchanged.
- Preserve but modernize expression.
- Test in isolation.
- Retire or reduce.
- Replace through a defined transition.

## 7. Product, packaging, retail, and access

### Product

Before broad communication, identify the one or two largest barriers to younger trial or repeat. Investigate:

- Pack size and household fit.
- Portability and convenience.
- Price and entry point.
- Format, feature, or sensory expectations.
- Current usage occasions.
- Ease of choosing and understanding the range.
- Quality or performance concerns.
- Availability in relevant channels.
- Responsibility or ingredient expectations where relevant.

The brand must deliver at least one meaningful, validated improvement that makes the new promise true. It should not pursue an undifferentiated innovation checklist.

### Packaging

- Retain proven recognition cues.
- Make the current benefit immediately legible.
- Simplify variant navigation.
- Test shelf and digital-thumbnail recognition.
- Introduce new sizes or formats under the master brand where credible.
- Explain changes plainly.
- Avoid a youth sub-brand unless evidence shows the master brand cannot stretch.

### Retail and ecommerce

- Organize navigation around needs or occasions where appropriate.
- Improve search titles, product images, comparisons, reviews, and FAQs.
- Test trial sizes, bundles, and introductory formats where economically sound.
- Use retail media to explain the current benefit at the point of choice.
- Protect familiar core products and shelf presence.
- Map marketplaces, convenience, quick-commerce, subscriptions, and social commerce where relevant.
- Coordinate media with stock and service readiness.

## 8. Channel and content model

### Channel-by-stage roles

| Stage | Younger-audience role | Loyal-customer role | Primary channels | Content requirement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery | Establish current relevance | Maintain visibility without alienation | Short-form video, creators, CTV | Current use and distinctive benefit; minimal heritage |
| Consideration | Prove performance and fit | Reinforce continuity | Product pages, experts, reviews, retail media | Demonstration, comparison, specific proof |
| Trial | Reduce risk and access barriers | Protect familiar purchase path | Retail, ecommerce, CRM | Offer, format, availability, reassurance |
| Repeat | Confirm value and experience | Reinforce trust and habit | CRM, service, loyalty | Usage support, performance, feedback |
| Advocacy | Enable credible recommendation | Mobilize genuine advocates | Community, referrals, earned media | Real experience and verifiable evidence |

### Content pillars

1. **Proven, not stuck**  
   Show what the brand has learned and how that capability benefits consumers now.

2. **Useful in real life now**  
   Demonstrate current routines, constraints, and occasions.

3. **Better by design**  
   Explain specific product, format, service, or access improvements.

4. **Trusted across generations**  
   Show younger and older users as current people with agency—not stereotypes.

5. **Real proof, real questions**  
   Use demonstrations, reviews, expert explanation, and transparent answers.

### Creator and community approach

Select creators for:

- Genuine category use.
- Relevant expertise or lived experience.
- Credibility with the priority audience.
- Ability to demonstrate or explain the product.
- Brand and conduct fit.
- Transparent disclosure.

Use a portfolio of practical creators, experts, younger users, loyal users, and relevant community voices. A partnership must make sense without its reach number.

Build community participation only where the brand can sustain a relationship through feedback, beta groups, product-use support, or visible “you said, we changed” action.

## 9. Authenticity and backlash protections

### Do

- Speak plainly and conversationally.
- Lead with utility.
- Show younger consumers as real people, not a demographic code.
- Let creators use their own credible voice within standards.
- Use humor only where the brand has earned it.
- Treat older customers as respected current users.
- Connect every major claim to visible action.
- Explain what changed and why.
- Use current references only where naturally connected to the product.

### Do not

- Borrow slang, memes, or trends to signal youth.
- Make being old the joke.
- Use younger casting as the main proof of relevance.
- Present design change as product innovation.
- Over-script supposedly authentic content.
- Use activism, sustainability, or cultural partnerships as decoration.
- Depend on heritage without current utility.
- Let engagement substitute for product understanding, trial, repeat, or margin.
- Make modest improvements sound revolutionary.

### Full-experience authenticity audit

Before launch, review:

- Product and formulation.
- Packaging.
- Price and promotion.
- Availability and distribution.
- Retail and ecommerce.
- Customer service.
- Claims and substantiation.
- Creator fit and disclosure.
- Employee understanding.
- Corporate and responsibility behavior.

### Five-question authenticity gate

1. Would the brand still say this if the trend disappeared tomorrow?
2. Is the behavior supported by a product, service, or operational action?
3. Does the partnership make sense without its reach number?
4. Would a loyal customer recognize the brand’s character?
5. Can the contemporary benefit be explained in one sentence?

### Backlash response model

| Trigger | Likely diagnosis | Immediate response | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sustained “cringe,” “corporate,” or “trying too hard” response | Creative or partnership mismatch | Pause amplification, remove offending material, acknowledge without defensiveness, retest product-grounded work | Brand/communications |
| False or unsupported claim criticism | Substantiation failure | Correct or withdraw claim, publish evidence, contact affected customers | Legal/quality/brand |
| Loyal customers report abandonment | Continuity or asset failure | Restore familiar cues, protect core range, issue continuity communication | Brand/sales/service |
| High engagement but weak product understanding | Entertainment displaced proposition | Re-edit toward benefit and demonstration; reallocate media | Creative/media |
| Trial rises but repeat does not | Product, value, or onboarding problem | Investigate before adding media | Product/insights |
| Packaging recognition declines | Identity evolution exceeded tolerance | Slow rollout, restore distinctive cues, retest architecture | Design/packaging |
| Creator content works only without the brand | Weak brand fit | End or redesign partnership | Partnerships |
| Earned coverage centers on the rebrand | Change appears cosmetic | Shift PR toward product, service, and operational evidence | Communications |
| Core sales decline beyond tolerance | Base damage, supply issue, or cannibalization | Protect core SKUs, review rollout and media, restore familiar access | Commercial/finance |

Each trigger must have a fixed threshold, duration, decision owner, customer response, inventory implication, budget implication, and re-test condition.

## 10. Transition phases

### Phase 0: Diagnose and align — months 0–3

Conduct:

- Quantitative funnel and equity research by age, usage, loyalty, product, market, and channel.
- Qualitative work with younger users, occasional users, aware non-users, lapsed users, newer-brand users, and loyal customers.
- Product, price, format, occasion, availability, packaging, retail, and digital audits.
- Forensic review of the prior campaign’s creative, comments, earned media, complaints, sales, and retention.
- Heritage proof inventory.
- Distinctive-asset recognition testing.
- Younger purchase-journey mapping.
- Brand, commercial, sentiment, and operational baselines.

**Gate 1: Proceed only if the team can state:**

- The primary younger audience.
- The priority occasion or need.
- The principal barrier to trial or repeat.
- The heritage capability providing permission.
- The current product or experience proof.
- The competitive choice advantage.

If not, continue diagnosis.

### Phase 1: Validate product and proposition — months 3–6

- Test the highest-priority product, pack, format, price, access, or service response.
- Develop two or three differentiated positioning territories.
- Compare utility-led, heritage-proof-led, and product-specific propositions.
- Measure relevance, credibility, distinctiveness, choice, expected value, and trial intent.
- Establish whether the proposed improvement changes expected behavior.

No broad identity change should precede a credible product or experience proposition.

### Phase 2: Controlled creative, packaging, and market pilots — months 6–9

- Test current versus evolved identity expression.
- Test packaging in controlled stores and digital environments.
- Use a small portfolio of credible creators and experts.
- Pilot retail and ecommerce execution.
- Launch in selected markets, channels, or product lines.
- Maintain adequate stock and service readiness.
- Measure younger response and loyal-customer reassurance separately.

**Gate 2: Scale, modify, narrow, or stop** against fixed brand, behavioral, operational, and economic criteria.

### Phase 3: Integrated rollout — months 9–15

Scale only the strongest tested platform.

- Align packaging, retail, ecommerce, media, CRM, creators, and service.
- Expand validated product or format improvements.
- Train employees, retailers, creators, and service teams.
- Explain what changed and what remains.
- Maintain core SKUs and familiar purchase paths.

Calendar timing is subordinate to evidence and operational readiness.

### Phase 4: Expansion and institutionalization — months 15–24

- Expand proven occasions, formats, channels, and markets.
- Continue the product and access pipeline.
- Optimize for recruitment, repeat, margin, and retention.
- Conduct annual equity and asset reviews.
- Reassess audience progression.
- Narrow the platform if durable growth occurs only in one occasion or product range.

At the 18–24-month gate, decide whether to broaden the platform, narrow it, retain product-led work while pausing identity change, shift to lapsed-user win-back, or redirect investment to product, price, category, or access solutions.

## 11. Loyal-customer protections

### Protect

- Core product performance and signature experience.
- Quality and service standards.
- Familiar master-brand recognition.
- Availability of core products.
- Preferred purchase paths and formats.
- Existing loyalty benefits.
- Clear explanations of material changes.

### Communicate differently, not inconsistently

**Younger consumers:**  
Why the product fits a current need and what problem it solves.

**Loyal customers:**  
What remains the same, what has improved, and how quality is protected.

### Involve without granting veto power

- Test proposed changes with loyal customers before rollout.
- Ask what must not change and what improvements they welcome.
- Use loyal users as current advocates, not nostalgia props.
- Give customer service continuity scripts and escalation paths.
- Provide advance notice of material product or packaging changes.

Set numerical tolerances for trust, recognition, retention, complaints, and core sales after baselines are established and before pilots begin.

## 12. Governance and decision rights

### Core/evolution charter

**Core**

- Product promise.
- Quality and safety.
- Signature experience.
- Truthful heritage.
- Service and legal commitments.
- A limited set of proven distinctive assets.

**Evolution zone**

- Formats and pack sizes.
- Product occasions.
- Packaging hierarchy.
- Visual expression.
- Tone.
- Digital and retail experience.
- Partnerships.
- Content formats.
- Access and availability.

### Cross-functional growth council

Include brand, strategy, product, quality, legal, design, packaging, sales, retail, ecommerce, media, customer service, finance, and research. Younger and loyal customers should advise, not hold veto power.

### Proof-before-promise gate

No major claim proceeds without confirmation from the relevant product, quality, operations, legal, or service owner.

### Pilot scorecard

Every pilot uses one scorecard covering:

- Target relevance.
- Consideration and trial.
- Product understanding.
- Recognition.
- Authenticity.
- Loyal-customer response.
- Repeat.
- Margin and cannibalization.
- Operational readiness.

## 13. Success measures and decision criteria

### Leading indicators

**Younger audience**

- Contemporary relevance.
- Fit with current life.
- Consideration.
- Product understanding.
- Intent to try.
- Authenticity.
- Distinctiveness.
- Search and product-page engagement.
- Trial conversion.
- Early repeat signals.

**Loyal customers**

- Recognition.
- Trust.
- Quality perception.
- Perceived continuity.
- Packaging recognition.
- Recommendation.
- Complaint volume and themes.
- Sentiment toward specific changes.

**Execution**

- Qualified reach and effective frequency.
- Search lift.
- Product-page engagement and conversion.
- Shelf findability.
- In-stock rate.
- Retail compliance.
- Creator comment quality.
- Customer-service contact reasons.

### Lagging indicators

- Under-35 penetration and purchase share.
- New-to-brand recruitment.
- Trial and repeat.
- Purchase frequency and retention.
- Retail velocity.
- Incremental sales versus matched control.
- Customer lifetime value.
- Incremental margin.
- Promotion dependence.
- Cannibalization.
- Core-customer retention.
- Core-product sales.
- Brand preference and recommendation.
- Share relative to competitors and substitutes.

### Objective-setting protocol

Because no baseline data is available:

1. Establish baselines in Phase 0.
2. Define minimum detectable effects and reliability standards with research and finance.
3. Approve numerical thresholds before pilot results are reviewed.
4. Lock them in the pilot charter.
5. Do not relax them retrospectively without documented executive approval.

Thresholds must cover:

- Minimum consideration lift.
- Minimum trial lift.
- Minimum repeat signal.
- Minimum recognition level.
- Maximum trust decline.
- Maximum loyal-customer retention decline.
- Maximum complaint or negative-sentiment increase.
- Minimum incremental margin.
- Maximum promotion dependence.
- Maximum cannibalization.
- Required payback period.

### Scale

Scale only when:

- Target consideration and trial improve versus baseline or control by the approved threshold.
- Product understanding and recognition meet threshold.
- Repeat shows a credible positive signal over an adequate observation period.
- Trust and loyal-customer retention remain within approved tolerances.
- Incremental economics meet the approved margin and payback case.
- Stock, product, service, and retail systems can deliver the promise.

### Modify or narrow

Modify when:

- Relevance rises but trial does not.
- Trial rises but repeat or margin does not.
- Younger response rises while recognition weakens.
- Results are limited to one occasion, format, channel, or subgroup.
- Engagement is high but product comprehension is weak.

### Stop

Stop or withdraw when:

- Trust, recognition, or core retention breaches pre-agreed limits.
- The product fails to deliver the communicated benefit.
- Backlash is broad and sustained.
- Results depend mainly on discounting or novelty.
- Incremental economics are negative after realistic costs.
- Packaging or identity materially reduces findability or recognition.

## 14. Immediate next actions

### Leadership

1. Approve the strategic direction: **credible evolution, not youth imitation**.
2. Approve the core/evolution charter and decision rights.
3. Confirm that no national rebrand or broad relevance campaign proceeds before evidence gates are passed.
4. Align on the requirement to protect trust, recognition, retention, and economics—not merely awareness or engagement.

### Strategy and insights

1. Commission the Phase 0 diagnostic.
2. Validate the provisional audience and priority occasion.
3. Establish brand, commercial, operational, and sentiment baselines.
4. Complete the prior-campaign backlash audit.
5. Produce two or three category-specific positioning territories.

### Product, packaging, retail, and service

1. Identify the largest barriers to younger trial and repeat.
2. Define and validate the minimum product or experience improvement.
3. Audit packaging, shelf recognition, ecommerce findability, availability, and customer-service readiness.
4. Build the proof inventory and claims map.

### Brand, creative, media, and partnerships

1. Develop product-grounded creative territories.
2. Define channel roles and audience-specific journeys.
3. Establish creator credibility and disclosure standards.
4. Build the authenticity monitoring and escalation protocol.

### Finance and governance

1. Set the pilot economics framework.
2. Define incremental margin, cannibalization, promotion, and payback requirements.
3. Lock scale, modify, and stop thresholds before pilot results are reviewed.

## Recommended strategic direction

**Keep what earned trust. Change what limits usefulness. Prove the difference before claiming it.** The brand should pursue credible evolution—anchored in a specific younger consumer need, a meaningful product or experience improvement, and disciplined pilots that protect loyal-customer trust while building contemporary relevance.
