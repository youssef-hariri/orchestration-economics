# International Innovation and Experimentation Strategy

## Executive summary

The company should build a **globally consistent innovation system, not a single global innovation culture**. Differences in employee initiative across countries may reflect leadership behavior, trust, workload, decision rights, language, resources, labor arrangements, regulation, or previous experience—not differences in creativity.

The strategy should give employees five conditions for responsible innovation:

1. **Permission** to question assumptions and raise concerns without retaliation.
2. **Capacity** to participate during paid working time, with competing work removed or reassigned.
3. **Capability** to frame problems, test ideas, assess evidence, and manage risk.
4. **Authority and access** to customers, data, tools, funding, specialists, and decision-makers.
5. **A credible pathway** from local insight to a decision, experiment, implementation, or documented learning.

Senior leaders should establish common global standards for safety, ethics, fairness, privacy, cybersecurity, non-retaliation, transparency, and risk management. Branches should adapt how those standards are delivered, including language, meeting formats, participation channels, recognition, local challenge areas, and consultation practices.

Success should be measured by improved business responsiveness, higher-quality experimentation, broader and fairer participation, stronger psychological safety, and sustained operational or customer value—not by idea volume alone.

---

## 1. Global principles

These principles apply in every country and branch:

1. **Innovation is everyone’s responsibility**, not only R&D’s.
2. **Innovation includes three types of work:**
   - Continuous improvement of existing operations
   - Experiments involving uncertainty and evidence-based testing
   - Strategic innovation involving higher-impact or potentially scalable changes
3. **Local insight is valuable.** Branches may address locally important customer, market, workforce, and operational problems.
4. **Ideas are judged on evidence and potential value**, not seniority, country, language fluency, or proximity to headquarters.
5. **Small, controlled experiments should precede major commitments** wherever feasible.
6. **Employees may challenge assumptions and raise concerns without retaliation.**
7. **Responsible failure is acceptable; negligence is not.**
8. **Participation must occur within paid working time** and be reflected in workload planning.
9. **Successful local innovations should receive a fair opportunity to be shared and scaled**, subject to validation in the receiving location.
10. **Innovation must not create unacceptable harm** to employees, customers, suppliers, communities, or the environment.
11. **Decisions, funding, and contribution credit should be transparent.**
12. **Fundamental protections cannot be weakened for cultural or operational reasons.**

Where “risk-taking” has negative connotations, use terms such as **disciplined testing**, **controlled learning**, and **evidence-based improvement**.

## 2. Diagnose before intervening

Before launching a global campaign, conduct a branch-level diagnostic. The purpose is to identify the actual constraint rather than assume that employees lack creativity.

### Assess

- Psychological safety and fear of retaliation
- Management openness to dissent
- Decision-making authority and centralization
- Workload and available innovation time
- Access to customers, data, tools, funding, and specialists
- Results of previous suggestion programs
- Language, disability, accessibility, and digital barriers
- Labor, regulatory, privacy, cybersecurity, and industry constraints
- Concerns about job displacement, surveillance, or restructuring
- Participation by frontline, shift, remote, temporary, contracted, and outsourced workers

### Use multiple sources

- Anonymous surveys in local languages
- Confidential interviews and focus groups
- Review of historical ideas and decisions
- Customer and frontline feedback
- Workload and capacity data
- Meeting observation
- Grievance, turnover, performance, promotion, and contract-renewal data

Classify the main barriers as:

- **Permission:** employees do not feel safe speaking up
- **Capacity:** employees lack time or resources
- **Capability:** employees need problem-solving or experimentation skills
- **Authority:** employees cannot act on relevant decisions
- **Opportunity:** employees lack access to customers, tools, data, or decision-makers
- **Motivation:** employees see little value in participating

Do not rank countries or branches publicly as “innovative” or “uninnovative.” Compare progress with local baselines and similar operating contexts.

## 3. Leadership and management practices

Innovation depends primarily on management behavior.

### Expected leadership behaviors

Leaders and managers should:

- Ask, “What are we missing?” and “Who sees this differently?”
- Invite alternatives before decisions are finalized.
- Speak last where hierarchy may inhibit dissent.
- Ask for evidence rather than polished presentations.
- Acknowledge employees who identify problems early.
- Explain why ideas are accepted, declined, or deferred.
- Share credit transparently.
- Discuss appropriate mistakes and lessons learned.
- Support teams that stop weak experiments early.
- Protect employees from blame when approved experiments fail responsibly.
- Challenge their own processes.
- Remove or reprioritize work when experimentation is approved.
- Make decisions within published service levels.

### Leadership routines

Each branch should select a small number of practical routines, such as:

- Monthly problem-solving sessions
- Quarterly experiment reviews focused on learning
- Leader listening hours or frontline visits
- Pre-mortems for significant initiatives
- Structured post-experiment reviews
- Frontline advisory groups or reverse mentoring

In high-hierarchy environments, use anonymous pre-meeting input, small-group discussion, written alternative views, and local facilitators. In lower-hierarchy environments, use structured turn-taking and independent written input to prevent outspoken employees from dominating.

### Leadership accountability

Performance and leadership reviews should consider:

- Timeliness and quality of responses to employee ideas
- Psychological-safety results
- Cross-branch collaboration
- Fair credit allocation
- Responsible risk management
- Inclusion of frontline and less-visible contributors
- Removal of workload and approval barriers
- Successful implementation and learning
- Contribution to business responsiveness

Do not reward managers for idea volume alone.

## 4. Participation, psychological safety, and inclusion

Psychological safety means employees can speak up, report problems, question assumptions, and propose alternatives without humiliation or retaliation. It does not mean that every idea is approved or that standards are lowered.

### Global safeguards

Provide:

- An enforceable non-retaliation policy
- Confidential reporting routes
- Independent investigation options outside the direct management chain
- Interim protection for employees raising serious concerns
- Protection for safety, legal, ethical, customer, and data concerns
- Meeting norms that prevent senior voices from dominating
- Manager training in receiving dissent
- Transparent decision and contribution records

Monitor whether dissenters experience adverse effects in:

- Performance ratings
- Promotion
- Work allocation
- Contract renewal
- Development access
- Turnover or grievance patterns

Retaliation claims should be investigated by an independent function such as ethics, employee relations, ombuds, or internal audit.

### Multiple participation channels

Provide an appropriate combination of:

- Local-language submissions
- Mobile-friendly and low-bandwidth tools
- Accessible digital tools
- Paper or physical channels where necessary
- Facilitated team discussions
- Voice, video, or messaging submissions
- Anonymous problem and concern reporting
- Confidential follow-up for employees developing ideas
- Paid sessions for shift, field, production, warehouse, and customer-facing employees

Assess ideas on substance, not presentation quality, language fluency, digital access, or self-promotion.

### Inclusion and influence

Track who:

- Identifies problems
- Receives support and funding
- Participates in experiments
- Receives credit
- Makes scaling decisions
- Bears additional workload or operational consequences

Include frontline, technical, commercial, operational, support, remote, part-time, temporary, contracted, and disabled employees, as well as employee representatives where relevant.

Translation, facilitation, cultural explanation, and inclusion work should be treated as formal work and recognized accordingly.

## 5. Capacity, funding, and capability

Innovation must not become unpaid additional work.

Each branch should document:

- What work will be stopped, simplified, automated, or reassigned
- How normal duties will be covered
- How shift and frontline employees will participate
- How innovation time is recorded
- How approved experiments affect performance expectations
- What backfill or specialist support is required

Managers should not be penalized for short-term operational trade-offs caused by formally approved and properly resourced experimentation.

Annual workforce and budget plans should identify:

- Protected innovation hours
- Local experimentation budgets
- Specialist support capacity
- Training and facilitation requirements
- Expected implementation workload
- Trade-offs with existing initiatives

### Funding model

Use staged funding:

1. **Discovery:** understand the problem, users, and context.
2. **Experiment:** run a defined test.
3. **Validation:** investigate promising evidence.
4. **Scale:** implement after agreed criteria are met.

Every branch should have a modest local experimentation budget. Enterprise funding should support strategic, cross-border, or infrastructure-intensive work.

Allocation should consider:

- Branch size
- Number of eligible employees
- Operational complexity
- Resource disadvantage
- Regulatory constraints
- Strategic importance
- Quality of approved experiments

Use separate accounting codes so funds are not silently diverted to ordinary operations. Provide a transparent appeal or review route.

### Capability support

Offer practical training in:

- Problem framing
- Customer and employee research
- Experiment design
- Data interpretation
- Risk and impact assessment
- Inclusive facilitation
- Business-case development
- Scaling and change management

Under-resourced branches should receive regional coaches, shared legal and technology services, translation, facilitation, and staff backfill.

## 6. Idea-to-experiment system

Use a risk-proportionate process rather than one global approval model.

### Pathway A: Rapid improvement

For low-risk internal process, workflow, service, quality, or productivity changes.

Requirements:

- Clearly stated problem
- Proposed change
- Small test
- Named owner
- Simple measure
- Short review

Approval should normally remain local.

### Pathway B: Controlled experiment

For changes involving customers, systems, data, moderate financial exposure, or material operational impact.

Requirements:

- Hypothesis
- Target users or process
- Expected benefit
- Success and failure criteria
- Budget and duration
- Risk controls
- Data-collection plan
- Stop conditions
- Decision date
- Relevant operational, technology, privacy, cybersecurity, or legal review

### Pathway C: Regulated or high-impact experiment

For safety-sensitive, employment-related, financial, medical, privacy-sensitive, cross-border, or legally regulated initiatives.

Requirements may include:

- Formal risk and impact assessment
- Legal, compliance, privacy, security, or labor consultation
- Employee or works-council involvement where required
- Independent approval
- Defined monitoring and escalation
- Customer and employee safeguards
- Remediation or compensation arrangements where relevant
- Rollback or termination plans

### Standard cycle

1. Identify a meaningful problem.
2. Gather customer, operational, market, or employee evidence.
3. Define the proposed solution and assumptions.
4. Screen for value, feasibility, harm, and regulatory requirements.
5. Run the smallest safe test.
6. Review evidence against pre-agreed criteria.
7. Stop, modify, retest, scale locally, or recommend broader adoption.
8. Document learning and assign an operational owner if implemented.

Every experiment should identify its owner, scope, budget, duration, measures, risk controls, stop conditions, decision date, and likely effects on employees, customers, suppliers, and communities.

### Responsible failure

Evaluate unsuccessful experiments against standards established before testing:

- Was the hypothesis reasonable?
- Was the test proportionate?
- Were known risks controlled?
- Were stop conditions defined?
- Was relevant expertise consulted?
- Was data reported honestly?
- Were concerns escalated?

Outcome alone should not determine accountability. Negligence, concealment, discrimination, safety violations, regulatory breaches, and reckless conduct remain unacceptable.

## 7. Examples of initiatives

Branches should select initiatives based on their diagnostic findings rather than launch all of these at once.

### For low-trust or high-hierarchy branches

- Anonymous problem-identification channels
- Small-group listening sessions led by trained local facilitators
- Written “alternative view” sections in decision papers
- Manager coaching on receiving dissent
- Rapid implementation of selected low-risk employee suggestions
- Private learning reviews for unsuccessful experiments

### For frontline and shift-based employees

- Paid, scheduled improvement sessions
- Supervisor-assisted submissions
- Mobile, paper, or voice-based idea channels
- Team-based process-improvement reviews at shift changes
- Recognition for implementation and risk identification
- Temporary backfill for participation

### For branches with moderate innovation capability

- Customer co-design workshops
- Cross-functional challenge teams
- Local experimentation microgrants
- Regional communities of practice
- Short problem-solving sprints
- Shared repositories of tested solutions and lessons

### For high-capability branches

- Larger controlled pilots
- Cross-border experimentation teams
- Mentoring of other branches
- Enterprise challenge themes
- Customer, supplier, university, or research partnerships
- Testing of scalable digital, service, or business-model innovations

### For all branches

- Monthly experiment reviews
- Pre-mortems for higher-impact initiatives
- Transparent idea-decision tracking
- Recognition for responsible stopping
- Six- and twelve-month reviews of implemented changes

## 8. Global consistency and local adaptation

### Standards that must be global

Every branch must maintain:

- Non-retaliation and respectful treatment
- Safety, legal, ethical, privacy, and cybersecurity standards
- Fair access to participation
- Transparent decisions
- Proportionate risk review
- Credible response times
- Fair contribution credit
- Basic impact and learning reporting

### Practices that may vary locally

Branches may adapt:

- Language and communication style
- Individual versus team recognition
- Anonymous, confidential, or public participation
- Meeting and facilitation methods
- Local challenge topics
- Training delivery
- Local partnerships
- Scheduling and digital channels
- Approval routes within global controls

Each branch should prepare a local innovation charter covering:

- Priority problems
- Local decision rights
- Available resources
- Boundaries and approval requirements
- Participation channels
- Sharing and scaling arrangements

### Local-variation test

Any proposed adaptation should consider:

- Whether it affects a global right or safeguard
- Legal, safety, privacy, cybersecurity, employment, or fairness risks
- Cross-border consequences
- Reversibility
- Required employee consultation
- Required approval

Adaptation should be based on employee evidence and local requirements, not assumptions about national culture.

### Graduated autonomy

- **Foundation:** low-risk improvements with close coaching
- **Developing:** controlled experiments within defined budgets and safeguards
- **Advanced:** broader local authority, cross-border pilots, and mentoring responsibility

Progression should depend on evidence of trust, capacity, capability, risk control, and implementation performance.

## 9. Collaboration and scaling

Create global and regional networks around business problems as well as functions. Options include:

- Cross-border innovation teams with explicit decision rights
- Shared experiment repositories
- Regional review panels
- Rotating virtual innovation sprints
- Short-term exchanges and secondments
- Internal expertise marketplaces
- Global customer-problem challenges
- Regional hubs for translation and adaptation
- Customer, supplier, university, and community collaboration where appropriate

Design collaboration for inclusion:

- Rotate meeting times across time zones.
- Use asynchronous work by default.
- Translate important materials.
- Use plain language.
- Give remote participants equal access to documents and decisions.
- Credit originating branches and contributors.
- Include local experts early.

Before transferring a solution, the receiving branch should assess:

- Customer needs
- Regulation and labor requirements
- Infrastructure and technology
- Workforce impact
- Privacy and data-transfer rules
- Cultural and operational fit
- Supplier, community, and environmental effects

Use a **core-and-adaptable-components** model. Standardize what must be common; allow local configuration where it improves fit.

Before scaling, require:

- A named receiving function
- A permanent budget owner
- Training and communications
- Technology and process ownership
- Implementation milestones
- Maintenance and support arrangements
- A rollback or exit plan
- Six- and twelve-month follow-up

## 10. Roles and responsibilities

| Role | Primary responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Set ambition; secure resources; remove enterprise barriers; make strategic trade-offs |
| Enterprise innovation council | Maintain global principles, funding criteria, risk standards, measurement definitions, and cross-branch learning |
| Regional or country panel | Support local adaptation; coordinate specialist resources; resolve cross-branch issues |
| Branch leader | Provide paid capacity, local funding, decision authority, safeguards, and workload relief |
| Branch innovation owner | Manage local channels, response times, charters, reporting, and learning reviews |
| Managers | Invite challenge, protect participation, respond constructively, and adjust workloads |
| Experiment owner | Define and run the test, manage evidence and risks, and recommend next steps |
| Risk specialists | Review material safety, legal, privacy, cybersecurity, ethical, financial, and employment risks; retain veto authority over unacceptable risks |
| HR and employee relations | Monitor retaliation, performance implications, workforce impacts, consultation, and fair treatment |
| Finance and procurement | Enable staged funding, rapid low-risk purchasing, and transparent accounting |
| Technology, data, and security teams | Provide secure tools, data access, testing environments, and technical safeguards |
| Employees and employee representatives | Identify problems, contribute evidence, participate in experiments, and raise concerns |

Maintain a responsibility matrix covering diagnosis, communication, funding, consultation, approval, implementation, scaling, measurement, and corrective action.

Suggested service levels:

- Acknowledge submissions within five working days.
- Provide a decision or next step within twenty working days.
- Complete rapid low-risk reviews within ten working days.
- Provide reasons for rejection or deferral.
- Escalate only when risk, scale, or cross-border impact warrants it.

## 11. Data, intellectual property, and technology safeguards

Establish country-appropriate rules covering:

- Employee-created inventions
- Attribution and moral rights
- Financial or career recognition
- Confidentiality
- Third-party intellectual property
- Open-source software
- Contractor and supplier contributions
- Cross-border transfer of technical information
- Dispute resolution and appeals

Credit, legal ownership, and compensation are separate issues and must be explained clearly.

Early screening should cover:

- Personal or sensitive data
- Access permissions
- Cybersecurity vulnerabilities
- Secure testing environments
- Data retention and deletion
- Cross-border data transfer
- Third-party tools
- Algorithmic bias
- Confidential information

Experiments involving automated decisions should also require, where relevant:

- Defined purpose and acceptable-use boundaries
- Bias and disparate-impact testing
- Human oversight
- Appropriate explainability
- User notification
- Appeal or correction mechanisms
- Post-deployment monitoring
- Rollback capability

## 12. Metrics

Use a balanced, context-adjusted scorecard. Do not use raw country rankings or universal idea quotas.

### Climate

- Psychological-safety score
- Confidence in non-retaliation
- Perceived management responsiveness
- Belief that ideas are taken seriously
- Evidence of retaliation or career penalties

### Participation and influence

- Percentage of eligible employees contributing
- Participation by branch, role, function, level, and work pattern
- Frontline and shift-worker participation
- Use of local-language and non-digital channels
- Percentage of submissions receiving a timely response
- Representation in funding, selection, experimentation, and scaling decisions

### Experiment quality

- Time from idea to decision
- Experiments started and completed
- Percentage with hypotheses, success criteria, and stop conditions
- Experiment cycle time
- Percentage stopped or modified based on evidence
- Compliance and safety review completion
- Small-test-to-large-commitment ratio
- Quality of problem evidence and test design

### Business responsiveness

- Time from identified customer or market need to tested response
- Time from validated experiment to implementation
- Percentage of strategic priorities supported by experiments
- Response speed to regulatory, technological, competitive, or customer changes
- Customer problems identified and addressed by frontline employees

### Outcomes

- Customer satisfaction, retention, access, or adoption
- Revenue or margin
- Cost, quality, productivity, or cycle-time improvement
- Safety and risk reduction
- Employee experience
- Sustainability
- Cross-branch adoption
- Six- and twelve-month durability
- Unintended effects on employees, customers, suppliers, and communities

### Measurement cadence

- **Monthly:** submissions, response times, experiments, capacity, incidents
- **Quarterly:** psychological safety, leadership behavior, participation equity, workload
- **Semiannually:** customer, employee, financial, sustainability, and scaling outcomes
- **Annually:** strategic responsiveness, portfolio balance, returns, harms, and system redesign

Maintain a measurement dictionary defining each metric, source, owner, frequency, and validation method. Use sample audits and qualitative case studies to verify reported activity and benefits.

Set targets only after baseline diagnosis. Possible targets include improved response times, stronger psychological safety, broader frontline participation, higher experiment quality, shorter validation-to-implementation times, and measurable customer or operational improvements.

## 13. Phased implementation roadmap

### Phase 1: Align and diagnose — first 30 days

- Appoint the executive sponsor and accountable owners.
- Establish global principles and non-negotiable boundaries.
- Conduct local-language listening and baseline measurement.
- Review previous suggestion programs.
- Map workload, authority, resources, and regulatory constraints.
- Select pilot branches with different cultures and capability levels.
- Identify three to five enterprise or local priority problems.

### Phase 2: Establish the minimum operating model — days 31–90

Each pilot branch should:

1. Complete a local innovation charter.
2. Provide an accessible participation channel and alternatives.
3. Allocate paid participation time.
4. Appoint a branch owner and local facilitator.
5. Provide a local experimentation budget.
6. Select the appropriate experimentation pathway.
7. Publish decision criteria and response deadlines.
8. Establish retaliation, credit, data, and intellectual-property safeguards.
9. Train managers in psychological safety and constructive challenge.
10. Identify work that will be stopped, reduced, or reassigned.

### Phase 3: Test and refine — months 4–9

- Run experiments in pilot branches.
- Hold monthly learning reviews.
- Measure workload, trust, inclusion, response times, and risk.
- Remove unnecessary procurement, legal, technology, or approval barriers.
- Test local adaptations.
- Gather employee and customer feedback.
- Audit experiment quality and reported results.
- Identify solutions suitable for replication.

### Phase 4: Scale selectively — months 10–18

Expand only when pilots demonstrate:

- Timely and transparent responses
- Improved trust or psychological safety
- Realistic paid capacity
- Appropriate experiment quality
- No unresolved material retaliation, safety, legal, privacy, or cybersecurity issues
- Evidence of customer, operational, employee, or strategic value
- Acceptable workload and inclusion outcomes
- Documented local adaptations
- A credible operational owner and implementation plan

Branches that do not meet these conditions should receive remediation rather than automatic expansion.

### Phase 5: Institutionalize — beyond 18 months

- Include innovation leadership in promotion and succession decisions.
- Conduct quarterly executive portfolio reviews.
- Stop or reprioritize existing work to create capacity.
- Refresh strategic challenge areas annually.
- Maintain a balanced portfolio of incremental, adjacent, and transformational work.
- Conduct recurring cultural, workload, and inclusion diagnostics.
- Integrate validated solutions into operating and capital planning.
- Review whether the system remains ethical, inclusive, responsive, and valuable.

## 14. Key risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Innovation becomes unpaid extra work | Provide paid time and remove, reassign, or automate equivalent work |
| Innovation conflicts with operational targets | Adjust workforce plans and performance expectations for approved experiments |
| Idea campaigns create noise | Focus on defined problems and evidence-based screening |
| Employees fear retaliation | Provide independent reporting, investigate claims, and monitor career outcomes |
| Public debate favors senior or fluent employees | Use written, anonymous, facilitated, accessible, and multilingual channels |
| Headquarters appropriates local ideas | Maintain contribution records and credit originating branches |
| High-initiative branches become overloaded | Set portfolio limits and resource mentoring responsibilities |
| Low-initiative branches are stigmatized | Measure progress from local baselines |
| Risk-taking becomes reckless | Use a common risk taxonomy and prohibit unsafe, unlawful, or unethical conduct |
| Failed experiments are concealed | Reward honest learning and early stopping |
| Metrics encourage gaming | Use balanced measures, audits, and qualitative review |
| Innovation harms employees or communities | Require impact assessments and employee-representative involvement |
| Technology experiments create cyber or privacy risks | Use secure environments and specialist review |
| New technology displaces or burdens employees | Assess workload, job impact, consultation, training, and mitigation |
| Successful pilots fail during transfer | Validate local fit and assign operational ownership |
| Local solutions are copied without adaptation | Separate core enterprise requirements from locally configurable elements |
| Governance becomes bureaucratic | Use clear decision rights, limited escalation, and published service levels |
| Inclusion work becomes invisible labor | Allocate time and recognize the contribution |
| Demographic data creates privacy risks | Collect only necessary data, suppress small groups, and protect confidentiality |
| Contractors or suppliers are excluded | Define participation, credit, confidentiality, and protection rules |
| Intellectual-property disputes undermine trust | Publish ownership rules and provide neutral dispute resolution |
| Psychological-safety data are unreliable | Triangulate surveys with interviews, behavior, grievances, and career outcomes |

## Definition of success

The strategy is working when:

- Employees can raise concerns and propose improvements without fear.
- Managers consistently invite and respond to alternative views.
- Participation extends beyond headquarters, senior staff, and highly fluent employees.
- Branches conduct disciplined experiments rather than merely more idea campaigns.
- Weak initiatives are stopped earlier and produce reusable learning.
- Local branches have meaningful authority, paid capacity, and resources.
- Valuable local solutions are shared without forced standardization.
- The company responds faster to customer, market, technological, and regulatory change.
- Customers and employees experience measurable improvements.
- Innovation contributes to revenue, productivity, quality, resilience, safety, sustainability, or risk reduction.
- Successful pilots transition into sustained operational value.
- The company can distinguish responsible experimentation from negligence or misconduct.

> **Create common global safeguards and opportunity, while allowing each branch to choose the culturally and operationally appropriate route to participation, experimentation, and learning.**
