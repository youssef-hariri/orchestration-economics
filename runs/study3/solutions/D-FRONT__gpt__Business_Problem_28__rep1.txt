# Industrial-Services Unit  
## Turnaround-or-Exit Assessment and 12-Month Board Plan

### Decision requested

The Board is asked to authorize:

1. A final, tightly governed 12-month proof-of-viability program—not a third general cost-reduction exercise.
2. A dual-track plan: prove a viable core while preparing sale, carve-out, partnership, asset-disposal, wind-down, and closure alternatives.
3. Immediate controls over uneconomic bidding, loss-making contracts, cash leakage, discretionary capital expenditure, and strategic subsidies.
4. Formal decision gates at Day 30 and Months 2, 5, 8, and 12.
5. Gate-based release of turnaround funding within a Board-approved maximum cash envelope.
6. A final ownership decision based on risk-adjusted value after funding, separation costs, stranded costs, liabilities, and transaction costs.

---

## 1. Executive conclusion

The unit has lost money for three consecutive years despite two restructuring programs that reduced costs without restoring profitability. Its order book is shrinking, high-performing managers are transferring to other group businesses, and some customers are shared with profitable divisions.

The evidence supports a **mixed structural-and-execution hypothesis**:

- Execution losses may arise from underpricing, weak estimating, low utilization, rework, poor change-order recovery, delayed billing, and inadequate contract controls.
- Structural losses may arise from commoditized services, excess capacity, unfavorable contract risk, declining demand, insufficient scale, or weak differentiation.
- Shrinking backlog may reflect deteriorating market position, customer attrition, or deliberate withdrawal from uneconomic work; this must be established.
- Manager transfers are both a symptom of declining prospects and a contributor to weaker execution.
- Shared customers may create group-wide value, but that value must be quantified and captured. It cannot justify indefinite subsidy.

The unit should therefore receive one final year to prove whether a defined combination of customers, service lines, and operating locations can:

1. Generate sustainable contribution after cost-to-serve;
2. Cover its residual fixed-cost base;
3. Win new and renewed work at acceptable economics;
4. Convert earnings into cash;
5. Operate at realistic utilization and capital requirements;
6. Retain or replace critical talent; and
7. Produce greater risk-adjusted value than exit alternatives.

The current portfolio should be treated as unlikely to survive intact. Each material activity must be classified as **invest, fix, harvest, sell, run off, or close**.

## 2. Facts, assumptions, and required evidence

### 2.1 Established facts

- The unit is part of a diversified group.
- It has lost money for three consecutive years.
- Two restructuring programs reduced costs but did not stop the losses.
- Some customers also purchase from profitable group divisions.
- The order book is shrinking.
- High-performing managers are transferring to other group businesses.
- The Board has granted one final year to demonstrate a credible path to profitability or exit.

### 2.2 Working assumptions requiring validation

- Losses may be concentrated in particular customers, contracts, service lines, sites, or operating teams.
- Some work may be underpriced, over-customized, operationally complex, or exposed to unfavorable contract terms.
- Previous restructuring may have removed overhead without addressing revenue quality, pricing, productivity, utilization, or contract selection.
- Group relationships may create customer-retention or cross-selling value, but that value may not accrue to the unit.
- Some assets, licenses, technical capabilities, or customer relationships may be more valuable to another owner.
- Reported losses may include group allocations or legacy costs that would not transfer to a buyer, while other costs may be understated by group support.

### 2.3 Evidence required by Month 2

Management must produce:

- Customer, contract, service-line, site, geography, and team-level economics;
- A contribution waterfall from quoted price to collected cash;
- Break-even utilization and billing-rate analysis;
- Backlog contribution, cash value, and cancellation-risk analysis;
- Fixed, avoidable, shared, and stranded-cost analysis;
- Working-capital and cash-exposure analysis;
- Strategic-customer and cross-sell value cases;
- Buyer, partner, carve-out, and closure assessments;
- A review of why prior restructuring benefits did not restore profitability.

At least 90% of material revenue should be mapped to a customer, contract, service line, and site by Day 30, with 100% of material loss-making contracts assigned to named owners. Any remaining data gaps must carry explicit confidence ratings.

## 3. Diagnosis: structural versus execution-driven loss

### 3.1 Structural-loss indicators

An activity should be considered structurally challenged where, after correcting identifiable execution failures:

- Sustainable market prices remain below fully burdened cost;
- Demand cannot support economic utilization;
- Customers buy primarily on lowest price;
- Differentiation does not create realized pricing power;
- Fixed-price, penalty, warranty, or escalation terms systematically transfer risk to the unit;
- Competitors possess a structural advantage in scale, location, technology, labor model, or procurement;
- The activity requires persistent group subsidy;
- Acceptable returns depend on speculative demand or uncommitted cross-selling.

### 3.2 Execution-loss indicators

Losses are more likely execution-driven where:

- Actual pricing is below market or approved bid thresholds;
- Actual hours, overtime, travel, or subcontracting exceed estimates;
- Rework, defects, claims, or warranty costs are abnormal;
- Utilization is low despite adequate demand;
- Scope increases are not converted into approved change orders;
- Billing is delayed or incomplete;
- Forecasts identify losses only after recovery is unlikely;
- Renewal prices do not reflect actual cost-to-serve;
- Prior restructuring actions were not implemented or benefits were not tracked.

### 3.3 Required counterfactuals

For each material loss pool, management must show:

1. Actual economics;
2. Economics at approved pricing;
3. Economics at target productivity;
4. Economics at realistic utilization;
5. Economics excluding one-time or legacy issues;
6. Economics after resizing capacity; and
7. Cash economics after working capital and maintenance capital expenditure.

Each activity will then be classified as:

- Fixable through execution;
- Fixable through portfolio or capacity reduction;
- Dependent on uncertain commercial recovery; or
- Structurally uneconomic.

## 4. Economic model and portfolio segmentation

### 4.1 Contribution waterfall

For each material customer, contract, service line, site, and geography:

**Revenue**  
less:

- Discounts, rebates, credits, and penalties;
- Direct labor, overtime, materials, and subcontractors;
- Travel, mobilization, accommodation, and site costs;
- Equipment usage and maintenance;
- Warranty, rework, claims, and expected defect costs;
- Unbilled work and unrecovered change-order effort;
- Customer-specific account and contract-management costs;
- Material working-capital financing costs.

= **Contribution after cost-to-serve**

Then separately identify:

- Service-line management;
- Sales and bid costs;
- Facilities and fleet;
- Shared operations and support;
- Corporate or group allocations;
- Standby and excess-capacity costs.

= **Fully loaded operating profit or loss**

Both measures are required:

- Controllable contribution supports short-term contract and portfolio decisions.
- Fully loaded economics supports long-term ownership, investment, and exit decisions.

### 4.2 Utilization and break-even

For labor-intensive services, calculate:

- Available productive hours;
- Billable hours;
- Paid hours;
- Utilization by role, crew, location, and service line;
- Realized billing rate;
- Fully burdened labor cost per productive hour;
- Overtime and subcontractor substitution;
- Break-even utilization at current rates;
- Break-even billing rate at expected utilization.

Available capacity must be adjusted for skills, geography, travel, training, safety, leave, seasonality, customer access, and certification requirements.

A service line that is profitable only at unrealistic utilization is structurally impaired unless capacity can be reduced or demand secured.

### 4.3 Portfolio categories

| Category | Test | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Invest** | Positive or near-positive contribution, defensible capability, attractive demand | Fund selectively and grow |
| **Fix** | Attractive potential impaired by pricing, productivity, utilization, or contract leakage | Execute a defined recovery plan |
| **Harvest** | Cash-generative but low-growth or non-core | Limit investment and extract cash |
| **Sell** | Valuable capability or customer base, but better owned elsewhere | Prepare a sale or carve-out |
| **Run off / close** | Structurally negative with no credible recovery case | Complete, terminate, dispose of, or close |

Potential profit pools to test include recurring maintenance or compliance work, specialized technical services, contract recovery, pricing discipline, utilization improvement, profitable group-account opportunities, and portfolio pruning. None should be treated as proven until supported by contract-level economics and forward demand.

## 5. Shared-customer value

Shared customers must be assessed separately for:

1. Unit standalone contribution;
2. Profitable group revenue at risk;
3. Probability-weighted cross-sell contribution; and
4. Value an external owner could create.

The calculation is:

\[
\text{Net relationship value}
=
\text{Unit contribution}
+
\text{Protected group contribution}
+
\text{Probability-weighted cross-sell contribution}
-
\text{Incremental support and delivery costs}
-
\text{Required subsidy}
\]

For each major shared customer, management must identify:

- Unit and group revenue and contribution;
- Probability that group revenue would be lost if the unit exited;
- Customer renewal and substitution risk;
- Specific cross-sell opportunity, owner, timing, probability, and contribution;
- Incremental account-management and delivery costs;
- Whether the customer would buy from the unit without group affiliation;
- Whether another group division or external provider could serve the need.

A strategic subsidy may be approved only where the benefiting division, objective, amount, accounting treatment, expiry date, milestones, and termination condition are explicit. The subsidy must be reported separately from the unit’s operating result and may not continue solely because the customer is described as strategic.

---

## 6. Backlog, contract, and cash quality

Backlog must be assessed by:

- Signed versus expected work;
- Committed versus cancellable volume;
- Remaining revenue and contribution;
- Customer and service-line concentration;
- Price escalation and labor-cost exposure;
- Delivery schedule and capacity requirements;
- Claims, disputes, and change-order dependence;
- Renewal probability;
- Cash collection profile;
- Termination and liability provisions.

The relevant measure is **backlog contribution and cash value**, not backlog revenue. A shrinking backlog may be positive if loss-making work has been rejected; a stable backlog may be negative if it comprises deteriorating fixed-price contracts.

The cash analysis must include:

- 13-week cash forecast;
- DSO and overdue receivables;
- Unbilled work, retentions, and disputes;
- Contract mobilization and start-up cash;
- Cash trapped in loss-making projects;
- Bonds, guarantees, and collateral;
- Required maintenance and replacement capex.

Working-capital release, claims settlements, and deferred expenditure must not be presented as recurring profit improvement.

## 7. One-year plan and decision gates

### Day 0–30: Mobilize and stop loss

**Actions**

- Appoint a single turnaround director and workstream owners.
- Activate bid and renewal approval controls.
- Review the largest contracts by revenue, loss, cash exposure, and strategic importance.
- Establish weekly cash, loss-contract, backlog, and pipeline reviews.
- Identify critical roles and retention risks.
- Freeze nonessential capex and hiring.
- Begin buyer, partner, separability, and closure analysis.

**Day-30 readiness test**

- At least 90% of material revenue mapped to an economic owner;
- 13-week cash forecast operating;
- All material loss-making contracts assigned to owners;
- Bid controls active;
- Critical roles identified;
- Exit-perimeter work started.

Failure should trigger escalation, possible leadership changes, and accelerated exit preparation.

---

### Months 0–2: Diagnose and stabilize

**Objectives**

- Establish reliable economics;
- Stop incremental losses;
- Protect customers, cash, and critical talent;
- Identify immediate exit candidates.

**Deliverables at Month 2**

- Reconciled baseline P&L and cash bridge;
- Customer/service-line/contract profitability heat map;
- Bid-to-cash margin-leakage bridge;
- Backlog contribution and cancellation analysis;
- Break-even utilization and capacity model;
- Strategic-customer value assessment;
- Initial buyer and partner market sounding;
- Turnaround, sale, and closure value cases.

**Gate rule**

Accelerate exit preparation if reliable data cannot be produced, new losses remain uncontrolled, no viable or recoverable activity can be identified, or cash exposure exceeds the approved limit.

---

### Months 3–5: Reposition the portfolio

**Actions**

- Reprice or renegotiate viable contracts.
- Exit or run off work below approved contribution thresholds.
- Improve scheduling, productivity, billing, change-order recovery, and rework.
- Consolidate sites and reduce excess capacity where justified.
- Focus sales on recurring, specialized, and lower-risk services.
- Establish joint account plans for shared customers.
- Appoint service-line leaders with profit accountability.
- Begin confidential buyer or partner discussions.

**Month-5 evidence**

- At least 80% of new and renewed work meets approved economics;
- Loss-making contracts are recovered, repriced, exited, or under controlled run-off;
- Utilization, productivity, billing, and rework are improving;
- Qualified target-segment pipeline has evidenced timing and contribution;
- Residual fixed costs are quantified;
- Critical-role retention is at least 90%;
- Improvement is recurring rather than driven by one-time settlements or working-capital release.

The numerical thresholds above are **interim management-control thresholds**. Final Board targets must be confirmed against the Month-2 baseline and the unit’s cash and return requirements.

Failure should lead to active divestment, controlled wind-down, or a formally approved narrower core—not an open-ended extension.

---

### Months 6–8: Scale and prepare

**Actions**

- Scale only service lines with repeatable economics.
- Increase recurring and indexed-price work.
- Complete portfolio pruning and dispose of idle assets.
- Institutionalize bid, contract, project, and cash controls.
- Complete standalone reporting and separation planning.
- Test buyer and partner interest.
- Refresh retain, sale, partnership, and closure valuations.
- Stress-test demand, labor, pricing, claims, and customer-loss scenarios.

**Month-8 evidence**

- Underlying contribution improved for at least three consecutive reporting periods;
- New-business margins meet approved targets;
- Backlog contribution and cash quality are improving;
- Residual fixed costs are validated;
- Capacity matches realistic demand;
- Results do not depend on uncommitted pipeline or exceptional claims recovery;
- Credible management team is in place;
- Retain value exceeds the best risk-adjusted exit alternative, or there is a documented strategic reason to continue.

---

### Months 9–12: Prove and decide

**Actions**

- Produce a rolling 12–24-month forecast by customer and service line.
- Separate recurring performance from one-time benefits.
- Compare retain, pivot, whole-unit sale, carve-out, partnership, asset disposal, and closure.
- Complete customer, employee, contract, liability, and communications plans.
- Launch a formal transaction process if selected.
- Prepare controlled run-off or closure authorization if no transaction is viable.

**Month-12 Board package**

- Validated current-year results;
- Recurring contribution and cash performance;
- Quality-adjusted backlog;
- Utilization and capacity evidence;
- Talent and leadership assessment;
- Strategic-customer value realization;
- Buyer or partner indications;
- Separation, stranded-cost, and liability model;
- Wind-down and closure case;
- Risk-adjusted recommendation.

---

## 8. Funding and cash governance

Before the Month-2 gate, management must propose a maximum funding envelope covering:

- Operating cash burn;
- Contract-loss funding;
- Restructuring and severance costs;
- Required maintenance capex;
- Critical-talent retention;
- Transaction preparation;
- Closure or run-off costs;
- Contingency.

Funding should be released in tranches at the formal gates. No increase to the total envelope, indefinite strategic subsidy, or extension beyond Month 12 may occur without Board approval.

The CFO must separately report:

- Recurring operating improvement;
- One-time benefits;
- Working-capital release;
- Restructuring cash costs;
- Capital expenditure;
- Group funding;
- Cash exposure under downside scenarios.

---

## 9. Stop-loss triggers

A trigger requires escalation to the Board sponsor and a written recovery or exit decision within ten business days.

| Trigger | Required response |
|---|---|
| Cash burn exceeds the approved 13-week forecast by more than 10% for two consecutive weeks | Freeze discretionary commitments and reassess funding |
| Cash runway falls below the approved minimum without committed funding | Prepare immediate funding, sale, or wind-down decision |
| More than 20% of new or renewed work is below approved economics after Month 5 | Suspend discretionary growth and review commercial leadership |
| Qualified pipeline falls below 1.25x the next two quarters’ requirement | Resize capacity and accelerate exit assessment |
| Material loss-making contract value increases for two consecutive periods | Escalate recovery, termination, or run-off decision |
| Major-contract forecast variance exceeds ±10% after Month 3 | Independent contract review and corrective action |
| Utilization remains below break-even for two consecutive months without a capacity action | Reduce capacity or classify the activity as structurally impaired |
| Critical-role retention falls below 90% | Activate succession and leadership-replacement plan |
| No service line demonstrates positive or credibly recoverable contribution by Month 5 | Move to active divestment or wind-down |
| Strategic-customer value cannot be evidenced or monetized | Withdraw subsidy unless separately approved |
| Retain value falls more than 10% below the best risk-adjusted exit value | Default to divestment unless the Board approves a documented strategic exception |

These are control thresholds, not substitutes for the full economic assessment. Where a threshold is not supported by the Month-2 baseline, it must be recalibrated by the CFO and approved by the Board sponsor.

## 10. Exit alternatives

| Alternative | Appropriate where | Principal risks |
|---|---|---|
| **Whole-unit sale** | The unit has a coherent capability or customer franchise and a buyer can capture synergies | Depressed value, warranties, retained liabilities, separation complexity |
| **Sale of selected service lines, sites, or geographies** | Specific capabilities, licenses, teams, or customer groups are attractive | Stranded costs, customer disruption, shared systems and contracts |
| **Partnership or joint venture** | Scale, capital, technology, procurement, or market access is the main constraint | Governance complexity, continued subsidy, limited liquidity |
| **Management buyout** | Value depends heavily on current managers and the business can operate independently at smaller scale | Limited financing, vendor support, residual liabilities |
| **Asset disposal and controlled wind-down** | Operating value is limited but assets or working capital have realizable value | Front-loaded closure costs, contract and employee liabilities |
| **Full closure** | No viable core or credible buyer exists and closure value exceeds continued ownership | Redundancy, claims, warranties, regulatory, environmental, and reputational risks |

For each option:

\[
\text{Net value}
=
\text{Expected operating or sale value}
-
\text{Required funding}
-
\text{Cash burn}
-
\text{Separation or closure costs}
-
\text{Stranded costs}
-
\text{Retained liabilities}
-
\text{Transaction and transition costs}
\]

Internal forecasts, market soundings, indicative offers, and binding offers must be shown separately. An optimistic internal forecast must not be compared with a discounted sale value without consistent probability weighting and cost treatment.

## 11. Continuation and exit criteria

### Continue as a focused core only if:

- A defined core has positive contribution after cost-to-serve;
- It covers its residual fixed-cost base under realistic assumptions;
- New and renewed work is consistently priced at approved economics;
- Improvement is recurring and cash-backed;
- Backlog and qualified pipeline support realistic utilization;
- Required capital and working capital are within the approved envelope;
- Critical talent is retained or replaced;
- Strategic group value is measurable and capturable;
- Retained value exceeds the best risk-adjusted exit alternative.

The final minimum economic standard must include:

- Fully loaded operating economics;
- Positive cash flow after maintenance capex;
- Return on incremental turnaround capital;
- A defined break-even date;
- Maximum permitted future group funding;
- Downside performance within the approved cash envelope.

### Pivot to a narrower core if:

- The full unit fails, but a clearly defined subset passes the economic tests;
- The core is operationally and financially separable;
- Non-core operations can be sold, harvested, run off, or closed;
- The transition can be funded within a fixed cash limit;
- A new, final decision date and accountability model are approved.

### Divest if:

- An external owner offers greater risk-adjusted value;
- Separation, liabilities, and stranded costs are appropriately reflected;
- The business is separable within the available time and cash budget;
- Continued ownership requires further funding without superior expected return;
- Customer and employee continuity can be protected.

### Wind down or close if:

- No defined core meets the continuation standard;
- No credible buyer or partner offers superior value;
- Continued operations consume cash beyond the approved limit;
- Closure value exceeds retention and sale value;
- Legal, customer, employee, and regulatory obligations can be managed responsibly.

## 12. Governance and accountability

| Area | Accountable owner |
|---|---|
| Overall turnaround and gate readiness | Turnaround director |
| Unit operating result and customer continuity | Unit managing director |
| Baseline, cash, benefits, and valuation integrity | CFO |
| Pricing, renewals, pipeline, and shared accounts | Chief Commercial Officer |
| Delivery, utilization, productivity, quality, and contracts | COO |
| Critical talent and workforce actions | HR director |
| Contract, employment, regulatory, and liability risks | General Counsel |
| Buyer, partner, carve-out, and transaction readiness | Corporate Development lead |

A weekly steering committee should include these owners and relevant profitable-division leaders for shared accounts. Finance or internal audit must independently validate the baseline, benefits, cash release, contract forecasts, and exit or closure costs.

Each Board gate must have one accountable owner, a defined evidence pack, a decision recommendation, and a funding consequence.

---

## 13. Final Board position

The unit should not be preserved merely because it serves important group customers, nor exited merely because aggregate losses are severe.

It should be retained only if a defined core demonstrates:

- Sustainable contribution;
- Realistic utilization;
- Profitable new and renewed work;
- Positive cash logic;
- Measurable group-wide value;
- A credible management team; and
- Greater risk-adjusted value than the available exit alternatives.

Activities that fail these tests should be repriced, harvested, sold, run off, or closed. The final year is successful only if it produces a reliable ownership decision before further value is destroyed—not merely a smaller reported loss.
