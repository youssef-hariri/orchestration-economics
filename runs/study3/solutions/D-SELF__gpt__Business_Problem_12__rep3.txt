# Regional Sustainable Tourism Development Plan, 2026–2036

## Executive summary

This plan provides a framework for attracting international visitors while protecting nature, respecting local culture, and ensuring lasting benefits for residents.

Because the region’s geography, communities, infrastructure, markets, laws, and tourism baseline have not been specified, the plan uses assumptions that must be validated locally. It recommends beginning with **two or three pilot destinations**, a small number of compatible international markets, and carefully managed visitor segments rather than pursuing unrestricted growth.

The central policy is:

> **Expand tourism only where rights-holders support it, essential services are adequate, ecological and cultural limits can be maintained, and benefits are transparently shared.**

Visitor numbers should be treated as a possible outcome—not the primary objective. Success should instead be measured through local income, quality employment, conservation results, heritage protection, resident wellbeing, and visitor value.

---

# 1. Strategic vision and objectives

## Vision

By 2036, the region will be a distinctive and resilient international destination where protected landscapes, historical places, and living cultures generate fair local prosperity without exceeding ecological, cultural, infrastructure, housing, water, or resident-wellbeing limits.

## Objectives

### 1. Protect natural assets

- Prevent inappropriate development in sensitive habitats and landscapes.
- Establish site-specific visitor limits and closure triggers.
- Reduce water use, waste, pollution, emissions, and wildlife disturbance.
- Fund conservation independently of visitor growth wherever possible.

### 2. Safeguard culture, heritage, and rights

- Protect tangible, intangible, and living heritage.
- Respect Indigenous, customary, religious, and community rights.
- Require consent or co-decision where applicable.
- Protect sacred places, private spaces, traditional livelihoods, cultural knowledge, and public access.

### 3. Generate equitable local prosperity

- Increase local and community ownership.
- Strengthen local supply chains.
- Provide decent employment and career progression.
- Share tourism revenues transparently.
- Prevent displacement, housing loss, loss of customary access, and elite capture.

### 4. Attract compatible international visitors

- Target visitors whose interests fit the region’s assets and capacity.
- Encourage longer stays, shoulder-season travel, and low-impact mobility.
- Promote multi-day itineraries rather than concentrating demand at a few famous sites.

### 5. Build a resilient destination

- Improve water, sanitation, waste, transport, accessibility, safety, and emergency services.
- Prepare for climate hazards, disease outbreaks, market disruption, cyber incidents, energy shocks, and other crises.
- Adjust or reduce tourism when agreed thresholds are exceeded.

---

# 2. Planning assumptions requiring local validation

The region is assumed to have some combination of natural landscapes, biodiversity, historical sites, and living cultural traditions, but no specific regional facts should be presumed.

Before implementation, the government should validate:

- Which natural and cultural assets are genuinely distinctive.
- Which communities and rights-holders may be affected.
- Whether communities want tourism, and in what form.
- Current visitor numbers, spending, seasonality, and local economic retention.
- Water, housing, transport, waste, energy, and emergency-service capacity.
- Heritage condition and biodiversity vulnerability.
- International connectivity, visa requirements, safety conditions, and market demand.
- Existing legal authority, staffing, financing, and enforcement capacity.
- Climate and cumulative impacts from tourism and other sectors.

---

# 3. Destination development framework

The region should be divided into management zones through participatory planning and environmental, heritage, social, and rights assessments.

## Gateway zones

Suitable for transport connections, accommodation, visitor information, food, services, and tourism businesses, subject to housing and infrastructure controls.

## Managed attraction zones

Natural and heritage sites where access is permitted under approved visitor limits, conservation plans, cultural protocols, and monitoring systems.

## Community tourism zones

Areas where communities voluntarily choose to provide tourism services and retain meaningful control over ownership, access, interpretation, pricing, and benefits.

## Restricted zones

Areas requiring permits, seasonal access, small groups, authorized guides, or other controls because of ecological, cultural, safety, privacy, or customary-use concerns.

## Conservation and recovery zones

Areas closed to tourism or subject to restoration until environmental, cultural, infrastructure, or rights conditions improve.

No site should be promoted simply because it is less crowded than an existing attraction.

# 4. International visitor strategy

## 4.1 Priority visitor segments

The agency should select segments based on motivation, behavior, expenditure, seasonality, emissions, and local value—not nationality alone.

| Segment | Potential products | Safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| Nature and biodiversity travelers | Wildlife observation, hiking, birdwatching, conservation visits | Licensed guides, distance rules, seasonal closures |
| Heritage and cultural travelers | Museums, archaeological circuits, historic towns, food and craft | Capacity limits, accurate interpretation, community approval |
| Soft adventure travelers | Walking, cycling, paddling, scenic routes | Route assessments, permits, safety and rescue systems |
| Slow and immersive travelers | Homestays, farms, village walks, cooking, wellness | Host consent, privacy protection, fair payment |
| Educational and specialist visitors | Research, archaeology, conservation, geology | Permits, local partnerships, data protection |
| Diaspora and ancestral visitors | Genealogy, archives, family and religious heritage | Privacy and respectful-access protocols |
| Accessible and multigenerational travelers | Adapted nature and heritage experiences | Verified accessibility and trained staff |

Mass coach, cruise, party, or highly price-sensitive segments should be assessed separately. They should not be actively pursued where they create disproportionate congestion, emissions, public costs, or low local spending.

## 4.2 Source-market selection

Initially select two or three priority source markets using locally validated evidence on:

- Product-market fit
- Air, rail, coach, and internal connectivity
- Visa and entry requirements
- Average stay and visitor spending
- Shoulder-season potential
- Environmental and transport impacts
- Cultural or diaspora connections
- Tour-operator access
- Market diversification
- Accessibility, language, payment, insurance, and safety requirements

The agency should prepare a market annex containing candidate markets, evidence, pilot budgets, expected outcomes, and exit criteria. No market should be prioritized solely because it is large.

## 4.3 Product development

Develop multi-day circuits connecting:

- Gateway towns
- Heritage sites
- Protected landscapes
- Community enterprises
- Food and craft producers
- Accessible attractions
- Low-carbon transport routes

Before a product is marketed, confirm:

- Community approval or applicable legal consent.
- Environmental and cultural suitability.
- Adequate water, sanitation, waste, transport, and emergency capacity.
- Trained local operators.
- Clear visitor rules.
- Local revenue-retention arrangements.
- Monitoring and enforcement capacity.

# 5. Responsible marketing

Marketing should begin only after the relevant destination meets minimum readiness standards.

## Marketing actions

- Create a multilingual, accessible destination website.
- Work with responsible tour operators and specialist travel agencies.
- Use targeted digital campaigns rather than indiscriminate mass promotion.
- Promote longer stays, shoulder seasons, and regional circuits.
- Invite media and content creators only under clear environmental and cultural protocols.
- Provide accurate information on transport, cost, weather, terrain, risks, rules, and accessibility.
- Promote licensed, locally owned, or independently verified operators.

## Marketing safeguards

Marketing must not:

- Use stereotypes or misrepresent communities.
- Describe inhabited places as “untouched.”
- Promote sacred, private, fragile, or restricted areas without authorization.
- Reveal sensitive wildlife or archaeological locations.
- Encourage illegal wildlife contact, artifact purchases, trespassing, or unsafe behavior.
- Claim that a destination is sustainable without evidence.
- Promote a site before capacity systems and community agreements are operational.

Environmental and cultural claims should be site-specific, measurable, current, and independently verified. Marketing, certification, impact assessment, and enforcement should not all be controlled by the same unit.

# 6. Community rights and participation

## 6.1 Rights-holder mapping

Before site selection, investment approval, product design, or marketing, identify:

- Indigenous peoples and representative institutions.
- Customary land and resource users.
- Residents, tenants, workers, and local businesses.
- Cultural and religious custodians.
- Women’s, youth, disability, and minority organizations.
- Farmers, fishers, informal operators, and other affected livelihood groups.

Absence from a formal land register must not be treated as evidence that customary rights do not exist.

## 6.2 Participation requirements

The agency must:

- Provide complete information about proposals, investors, ownership, risks, alternatives, and likely impacts.
- Use local languages and accessible formats.
- Fund reasonable participation costs, including transport, childcare, interpretation, and independent legal or technical advice.
- Use decision-making processes accepted by affected communities.
- Record agreements, objections, dissenting views, and unresolved issues.
- Reconsult when visitor numbers, ownership, infrastructure, or activities materially change.

Lack of objection is not consent.

## 6.3 Consent and community authority

Where Indigenous peoples may be affected, processes must comply with applicable law and relevant international standards concerning free, prior, and informed consent.

Consent processes must be:

- Free from coercion.
- Completed before decisions, construction, or promotion.
- Based on complete and understandable information.
- Conducted in appropriate languages.
- Allowed sufficient time for internal deliberation.
- Transparently documented.
- Renewable when impacts materially change.
- Linked to the ability to modify, suspend, or withdraw approval.

Communities should be able to refuse tourism use of sacred places, ceremonies, stories, knowledge, images, language, designs, or private spaces.

# 7. Environmental protection and carrying capacity

## 7.1 Baseline assessments

The government should map and assess:

- Protected areas and critical habitats.
- Biodiversity corridors and endangered species.
- Wetlands, forests, coastlines, rivers, and water resources.
- Geological features and sacred natural sites.
- Heritage landscapes.
- Current visitor pressure.
- Housing, transport, waste, energy, and emergency capacity.
- Climate and disaster risks.
- Cumulative impacts from agriculture, mining, transport, urbanization, and tourism.

## 7.2 Site-specific capacity

Each major site should assess:

1. Physical capacity and safety.
2. Ecological capacity.
3. Resident and social tolerance.
4. Cultural capacity.
5. Water, wastewater, waste, energy, and service capacity.
6. Emergency and transport capacity.
7. Management and enforcement capacity.
8. Regional cumulative capacity.

Each site plan must define:

- Desired conditions.
- Alert thresholds.
- Corrective-action thresholds.
- Closure or severe-restriction thresholds.
- Responsible authority.
- Monitoring method and budget.
- Required response time.

Where data are incomplete, apply precautionary limits rather than assume additional capacity.

## 7.3 Visitor-management tools

Use a combination of:

- Timed reservations.
- Daily or seasonal quotas.
- Group-size limits.
- Mandatory guides.
- Permit systems.
- Seasonal closures.
- Designated trails and boardwalks.
- Shuttles and park-and-ride systems.
- Quiet, no-drone, no-fire, and no-go zones.
- Wildlife-distance and no-feeding rules.
- Boat-mooring controls.
- Differential pricing with resident and domestic-access safeguards.
- Fines, restoration orders, permit suspension, and closure.

Pricing must not substitute for absolute limits. Permit allocation should prevent large operators from capturing scarce access and should reserve appropriate opportunities for residents, local enterprises, education, cultural use, and accessibility.

# 8. Nature, biodiversity, and climate safeguards

## 8.1 Development controls

Major tourism programs and projects should undergo environmental, cultural, social, heritage, climate, and cumulative-impact assessments covering:

- Construction.
- Operation.
- Maintenance.
- Closure and decommissioning.
- Restoration.
- Public access.
- Housing and displacement.
- Customary land and resource use.

Planning rules should establish:

- No-build zones.
- Setbacks from watercourses, wetlands, coasts, habitats, and heritage sites.
- Building-height and density limits.
- Landscape and architectural standards.
- Water-extraction limits.
- Short-term-rental controls where housing is under pressure.
- Developer contributions to infrastructure and conservation.
- Restoration bonds for major developments.

## 8.2 Water security

Tourism must not reduce reliable household, agricultural, or ecosystem water access.

Require:

- Water-balance assessments before development.
- Metering and reporting of water use per visitor-night.
- Drought restrictions and seasonal caps.
- Limits on pools, golf courses, and irrigated landscaping in water-stressed areas.
- Water reuse and rainwater capture where appropriate.
- Protection of customary water access.
- Suspension provisions during shortages.

## 8.3 Wastewater, waste, and materials

Require:

- Verified wastewater treatment and inspection.
- Maintenance and desludging plans for remote systems.
- Licensed waste collection and disposal.
- Source separation, recycling, and refill systems.
- Reduced single-use packaging.
- Food-waste reduction.
- Sustainable seafood and agricultural procurement.
- Responsible sourcing of timber, stone, and other materials.
- Construction-waste management.
- Light and noise controls.
- Invasive-species and biosecurity measures.

## 8.4 Wildlife and biodiversity

Activity-specific rules should address:

- Viewing distances and encounter frequency.
- Group and vessel limits.
- Seasonal closures.
- No feeding, baiting, touching, or breeding interference.
- Noise, lighting, and drone restrictions.
- Disease and invasive-species prevention.
- Off-road driving.
- Anchoring, diving, and night-tour impacts.
- Sensitive-location data protection.

Wildlife operators should be licensed, insured where appropriate, trained, and required to report incidents. Ecological monitoring should be independently reviewed.

The mitigation hierarchy is:

1. Avoid impacts.
2. Minimize unavoidable impacts.
3. Restore affected areas.
4. Address residual impacts only where legally and scientifically appropriate.

Irreplaceable habitats should not be treated as offsettable.

## 8.5 Climate resilience

Prepare risk assessments for:

- Floods and landslides.
- Wildfires.
- Extreme heat.
- Drought.
- Coastal erosion and sea-level rise.
- Storms and extreme rainfall.
- Biodiversity change.
- Access-route failure.
- Emergency evacuation.

Priorities are to reduce energy and transport demand, improve shared and low-emission mobility, promote longer stays, use renewable energy where feasible, and protect ecosystems that provide climate benefits.

# 9. Cultural heritage and cultural integrity

## Heritage actions

- Inventory tangible, intangible, and living heritage.
- Assess condition and vulnerability.
- Prepare site-specific conservation and visitor-management plans.
- Conduct heritage-impact assessments.
- Monitor archaeological conditions during construction.
- Establish chance-find procedures.
- Secure artifacts and verify provenance.
- Prevent illicit excavation and trafficking.
- Prepare emergency plans for fire, floods, conflict, and climate hazards.
- Use local languages and multiple perspectives in interpretation.
- Establish appropriate procedures for contested ownership, restitution, or repatriation where relevant.

Some sites should remain closed, remotely interpreted, or accessible only to authorized users.

## Cultural-product requirements

Every cultural tourism product must identify:

- Who controls the cultural content.
- Whether the practice is suitable for tourism.
- Visitor behavior, dress, photography, filming, and recording rules.
- Group size and timing.
- Practitioner compensation.
- Intellectual-property ownership.
- Data sovereignty for Indigenous knowledge.
- Privacy and sacred-site protections.
- Procedures for modification, suspension, or withdrawal.

Communities must be able to refuse the commercialization of cultural practices and knowledge. The strategy should use **community-defined cultural integrity**, not generic claims of authenticity.

---

# 10. Housing, land, and displacement protection

Before approving major tourism development, conduct a displacement and cumulative-impact assessment covering:

- Housing affordability and availability.
- Long-term rental conversion.
- Short-term-rental growth.
- Land-price and ownership changes.
- Customary land and livelihood access.
- Fishing, farming, grazing, and gathering.
- Resident and worker displacement.
- Public access to coasts, trails, water, and heritage places.

Potential safeguards include:

- Short-term-rental licensing and caps.
- Primary-residence requirements.
- No-net-loss rules for long-term rental housing.
- Community land trusts and cooperative ownership.
- Restrictions on sale or leasing of communal or customary land.
- Resident right-of-first-refusal provisions.
- Prohibition on privatizing essential public access.
- Independent review of acquisition or resettlement.

Any unavoidable displacement must comply with applicable human-rights law, include fair compensation and alternatives, and be independently monitored.

---

# 11. Local economic participation and decent work

## 11.1 Community benefit agreements

Before public support or promotion, projects must have written, enforceable agreements specifying:

- Ownership and management.
- Revenue-sharing formulas.
- Payment dates.
- Local employment and procurement.
- Community funds.
- Cultural and environmental obligations.
- Reporting requirements.
- Grievance and remedy procedures.
- Suspension and termination conditions.

The government should define “local” for ownership, employment, procurement, and tax retention.

## 11.2 Community funds

Community funds should have:

- Independent oversight.
- Public eligibility and allocation rules.
- Conflict-of-interest controls.
- Audited accounts.
- Public reporting of recipients and expenditure.
- Representation of women, youth, Indigenous peoples, marginalized groups, and affected livelihood sectors.

Funds should support both enterprises and non-commercial public benefits for communities bearing tourism costs or restrictions.

## 11.3 Local enterprise support

Establish a program providing:

- Small grants and low-interest loans.
- Business, accounting, and legal support.
- Digital and offline booking assistance.
- Language, hospitality, guiding, safety, and accessibility training.
- Product-development support.
- Cooperative development.
- Low-cost, proportionate licensing and verification.
- Mentoring and incubation.

Compliance systems must not exclude small, informal, or community enterprises. Requirements should be proportionate and supported through grants or technical assistance.

## 11.4 Workforce standards

Tourism work should provide:

- Fair wages.
- Safe conditions.
- Non-discrimination.
- Protection from harassment and exploitation.
- Predictable scheduling.
- Seasonal-worker protections.
- Training and career progression.
- Worker grievance and whistleblower channels.
- Protection for informal, migrant, seasonal, and platform-based workers.
- Recognition of traditional and community knowledge.

---

# 12. Infrastructure, mobility, and accessibility

## Infrastructure priorities

Public investment should first address:

- Water and sanitation.
- Waste management.
- Emergency and medical services.
- Safe public transport and access routes.
- Public toilets.
- Reliable electricity and renewable energy.
- Broadband and mobile connectivity.
- Visitor information.
- Accessible trails, buildings, and transport.
- Climate-resilient drainage, bridges, paths, and buildings.

Major projects should include lifecycle, maintenance, decommissioning, environmental, heritage, social, climate, and cumulative-impact assessments.

## Low-impact mobility

The government should:

- Improve connections from international gateways to priority destinations.
- Establish shuttles at congested sites.
- Support rail, coach, walking, cycling, and shared transport.
- Introduce electric or low-emission vehicles where feasible.
- Develop park-and-ride systems.
- Promote longer stays and regional circuits.
- Publish transport-emissions information.
- Reduce unnecessary internal travel.

## Accessibility and inclusion

Adopt universal-design standards and require:

- Site-by-site accessibility audits.
- Accessible booking, payment, and information systems.
- Step-free routes and accessible toilets.
- Tactile, audio, captioned, and plain-language interpretation.
- Sign-language services where feasible.
- Accessible emergency evacuation.
- Assistance-animal policies.
- Cognitive and sensory accommodations.
- User testing by disabled people.
- Disability representation in governance.
- Protection against discrimination and harassment.
- Accessible consultation and complaints procedures.
- Affordable resident access to public natural and cultural assets.

# 13. Governance and institutional responsibilities

## Sustainable Tourism Council

Create a legally mandated council including:

- Tourism, environment, culture, planning, housing, transport, finance, labor, and public-health authorities.
- Local governments.
- Protected-area and heritage managers.
- Indigenous and local community representatives.
- Tourism businesses and workers.
- Conservation and civil-society organizations.
- Universities.
- Accessibility organizations.
- Emergency services.

The Council should:

- Approve regional and site-level plans.
- Review major projects and cumulative impacts.
- Coordinate fees, quotas, closures, and standards.
- Publish annual results.
- Resolve cross-sector conflicts.
- Recommend suspension or modification of harmful activities.

Membership, mandates, budgets, conflicts of interest, meeting records, and decisions should be public.

## Program-management office

Create a cross-government delivery unit responsible for:

- Annual workplans.
- Budget and procurement tracking.
- Legislative dependencies.
- Monitoring deadlines.
- Risk escalation.
- Interagency coordination.
- Public reporting.

Every action should have a lead body, supporting bodies, budget, deadline, indicator, and risk owner.

## Destination Management Units

Each pilot destination should have an adequately funded unit responsible for:

- Visitor flows and reservations.
- Site maintenance.
- Conservation and heritage monitoring.
- Community engagement.
- Fee collection.
- Waste and mobility management.
- Operator licensing and inspections.
- Emergency coordination.
- Enforcement.
- Incident reporting.
- Public information.

## Independent oversight

Create an independent ombudsperson or complaints office for:

- Displacement.
- Rights violations.
- Cultural misuse.
- Unpaid community benefits.
- Greenwashing.
- Corruption.
- Discrimination.
- Unsafe operations.
- Environmental damage.

Complaints should have response deadlines, remedies, appeal routes, and public aggregate reporting.

# 14. Financing and fiscal safeguards

## 14.1 Costed financial framework

Before expanding beyond the pilot phase, prepare a ten-year financial plan covering:

- Baseline and assessment studies.
- Infrastructure capital expenditure.
- Site-management staffing.
- Conservation and heritage operations.
- Maintenance and replacement.
- Enforcement and monitoring.
- Community participation.
- Enterprise finance.
- Accessibility upgrades.
- Digital systems.
- Marketing.
- Emergency and maintenance reserves.

The plan must include conservative, central, and downside scenarios for visitor numbers, fees, grants, exchange rates, and private investment. Actual figures should be established through local feasibility studies rather than invented assumptions.

Essential conservation, heritage protection, and public infrastructure should not depend solely on volatile visitor revenue.

## 14.2 Revenue sources

Potential sources include:

- Protected-area and heritage fees.
- Accommodation or visitor levies.
- Concession fees.
- Developer-impact charges.
- Parking charges.
- High-impact activity charges.
- Grants and biodiversity or climate finance.
- Carefully structured public-private partnerships.

Fees should be placed in legally protected accounts with a published allocation formula for:

- Conservation and ecological monitoring.
- Heritage maintenance.
- Community-controlled funds.
- Local infrastructure.
- Indigenous cultural stewardship.
- Visitor management and enforcement.
- Independent auditing.

Communities bearing significant restrictions or impacts should receive benefits even where visitor numbers are low.

## 14.3 Private investment

Require disclosure of:

- Beneficial ownership.
- Land acquisition terms.
- Public subsidies and tax concessions.
- Infrastructure obligations.
- Local employment and procurement commitments.
- Environmental and cultural conditions.
- Performance bonds.
- Penalties and termination provisions.

Public-private partnerships should undergo independent value-for-money, affordability, fiscal-risk, and public-interest assessments.

# 15. Phased implementation

## Phase 1: Foundations — months 0–12

### Lead stakeholders

Tourism agency or ministry, environment and protected-area authorities, culture and heritage authorities, local governments, housing and planning agencies, transport authorities, Indigenous and community representatives, universities, and civil society.

### Actions

- Establish the program-management office and Sustainable Tourism Council.
- Select two or three pilot destinations using published criteria.
- Freeze active promotion of fragile or unassessed sites.
- Map rights-holders, customary areas, sacred sites, habitats, livelihoods, and vulnerable communities.
- Complete baseline assessments for tourism, markets, water, housing, displacement, climate, biodiversity, heritage, infrastructure, and resident attitudes.
- Establish legal authority for quotas, closures, fees, licensing, inspections, land-use controls, and benefit agreements.
- Identify urgent sanitation, waste, safety, transport, and accessibility needs.
- Draft consent, cultural-protocol, visitor, accessibility, data-protection, and marketing-claims standards.
- Establish an independent complaints and remedy mechanism.
- Prepare a costed Phase 2 investment plan.
- Publish the strategy for public comment and document responses.

### Decision gate

No major international campaign, large tourism development, or sensitive-site promotion should proceed until minimum readiness criteria are independently confirmed.

## Phase 2: Controlled pilots — years 1–3

- Upgrade basic services at pilot destinations.
- Implement reservations, quotas, permits, or closures where required.
- Introduce conservation and heritage fees with public reporting.
- Establish community benefit agreements and funds.
- Train guides, workers, site managers, inspectors, and emergency personnel.
- Provide enterprise finance and local-procurement support.
- Improve accessibility and low-emission mobility.
- License and inspect operators.
- Test selected international markets and shoulder-season products.
- Monitor ecological, cultural, economic, housing, water, and resident indicators quarterly.
- Conduct an independent evaluation at the end of Year 3.

### Decision gate

Expand only where pilot destinations demonstrate:

- Threshold compliance.
- Resident support.
- Delivered community benefits.
- Adequate infrastructure and enforcement.
- Stable or improved ecological and heritage conditions.
- Financial and institutional viability.

## Phase 3: Managed expansion — years 3–5

Expand only successful products and destinations that meet the decision gate.

Actions may include:

- Developing multi-day regional circuits.
- Restoring additional heritage sites.
- Improving public transport and inter-destination links.
- Scaling local procurement and workforce programs.
- Strengthening housing and short-term-rental controls.
- Requiring sustainability and community-benefit plans for larger developments.
- Expanding international trade partnerships selectively.
- Revising visitor limits, fees, and marketing based on evidence.

## Phase 4: Resilience and review — years 5–10

- Review the strategy every three years.
- Adjust marketing, fees, quotas, and closures when thresholds are approached.
- Expand ecosystem restoration and climate adaptation.
- Strengthen conservation and heritage finance.
- Maintain community authority to modify, suspend, or withdraw tourism products.
- Publish independent annual performance reports.
- Maintain emergency and maintenance reserves.

# 16. Monitoring, evaluation, and stop-growth rules

## 16.1 Monitoring framework

Each indicator must specify:

- Definition and unit.
- Baseline year.
- Target or threshold.
- Target date.
- Data source.
- Responsible institution.
- Reporting frequency.
- Independent verification method.
- Corrective action.

At least 12 months of baseline data should be collected before major promotional expansion where feasible.

## 16.2 Core indicators

### Economic value

- Spending per visitor and visitor-night.
- Share of spending retained locally.
- Local and community ownership.
- Local procurement.
- Local employment and median tourism wage.
- Job stability and progression.
- Community revenue received.
- Urban-rural distribution of benefits.
- Shoulder-season spending.

### Environmental performance

- Water use per visitor-night.
- Household and agricultural water reliability.
- Energy use and renewable-energy share.
- Waste per visitor-night.
- Wastewater compliance.
- Transport emissions.
- Habitat condition.
- Key-species condition and disturbance.
- Trail erosion.
- Invasive-species incidents.
- Sites exceeding ecological thresholds.

### Heritage and culture

- Condition of priority heritage sites.
- Conservation funding generated and spent.
- Unauthorized access, theft, or damage.
- Community-approved interpretation.
- Local-language coverage.
- Cultural-practitioner income.
- Unauthorized photography or recording.
- Products modified or withdrawn at community request.

### Community and rights

- Resident support for tourism.
- Housing affordability.
- Long-term rental availability.
- Short-term-rental conversion.
- Households displaced.
- Loss of customary access.
- Participation in binding decisions.
- Projects completing applicable consent processes.
- Communities modifying or refusing proposals.
- Grievances and remedy times.

### Governance and compliance

- Licensed-operator compliance.
- Inspection coverage.
- Fee collection and allocation.
- Public availability of contracts and assessments.
- Sustainability claims independently verified.
- Greenwashing complaints and sanctions.
- Projects suspended or modified for non-compliance.

## 16.3 Evaluation

Use:

- Pilot and comparison destinations.
- Before-and-after assessments.
- Resident, visitor, and business surveys.
- Ecological and heritage-condition monitoring.
- Distributional analysis by gender, age, disability, ownership, income, and community.
- Independent evaluations at the end of Years 3 and 5.

## 16.4 Adaptive management

Apply a formal decision rule:

- **Expand** when benefits increase and thresholds remain stable.
- **Modify** when impacts approach alert levels, benefits are uneven, or resident support declines.
- **Stop or reduce promotion** when ecological, cultural, rights, housing, water, safety, or resident-wellbeing thresholds are exceeded.

If monitoring is unavailable or unreliable, apply precautionary restrictions rather than permitting additional growth.

1. Appoint the program director and establish the cross-government delivery unit.
2. Establish the Sustainable Tourism Council with legitimate rights-holder representation.
3. Select pilot destinations through published environmental, cultural, rights, readiness, and market criteria.
4. Freeze promotion of fragile or unassessed sites.
5. Begin Indigenous, community, resident, worker, business, and civil-society engagement.
6. Commission baseline, capacity, water, housing, displacement, climate, biodiversity, and heritage studies.
7. Map customary land, sacred places, livelihoods, critical habitats, and public access.
8. Audit urgent infrastructure, safety, sanitation, waste, mobility, and accessibility gaps.
9. Draft consent, cultural, visitor, accessibility, data, and marketing-claims protocols.
10. Establish the community benefit-sharing and fund-governance framework.
11. Create a public register of tourism contracts, concessions, subsidies, and compliance actions.
12. Develop the ten-year costed financing plan, including maintenance and downside scenarios.
13. Establish an independent complaints and remedy mechanism.
14. Prepare pilot market research and responsible-operator criteria.
15. Publish the strategy for public comment and formally respond to submissions.

## Conclusion

The region should pursue **managed, community-authorized, conservation-led tourism**, not unrestricted visitor growth.

International visitors can be attracted responsibly by offering distinctive nature, heritage, food, outdoor, educational, and community-approved experiences; improving basic services and accessibility; targeting compatible markets; promoting longer stays and shoulder seasons; and providing clear, honest visitor information.

Long-term success depends on enforceable safeguards:

- Communities and rights-holders must have meaningful authority.
- Indigenous and customary rights must be protected.
- Water, housing, infrastructure, biodiversity, and heritage limits must override growth targets.
- Local workers and enterprises must receive fair and lasting benefits.
- Sustainability claims must be independently verified.
- Public finance and private investment must be transparent.
- Tourism must be reduced, redirected, or suspended when harm exceeds agreed thresholds.

This approach offers the strongest basis for attracting international visitors while protecting nature, respecting local culture, and delivering durable benefits to the communities that sustain the destination.
