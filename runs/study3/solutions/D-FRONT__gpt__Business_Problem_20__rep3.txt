# Operations Improvement Plan  
## Reduce Quoted Lead Time Without Compromising Certified Quality

## Executive summary

The company quotes **14-week lead times**, while a competitor quotes **6 weeks**. Sales reports losing approximately **one in three delivery-speed-sensitive opportunities**. The product requires certified inspections at multiple stages, and a prior schedule-compression effort was followed by increased warranty claims.

The company should not pursue six weeks by shortening, bypassing, or informally changing certified inspections, testing, traceability, nonconformance controls, calibration, or independent Quality release. The immediate objective is to determine where the 14 weeks is actually spent, remove avoidable waiting and variation, and demonstrate a safer flow on one standard product family.

The plan will:

1. Establish a validated, order-level lead-time baseline by product family.
2. Separate touch time from queues, batching, shortages, approvals, supplier delays, schedule losses, rework, and quality holds.
3. Investigate the previous warranty increase and formalize non-negotiable quality controls.
4. Prioritize interventions based on measured delay, frequency, customer impact, quality risk, cost, effort, and reversibility.
5. Pilot flow, material-readiness, scheduling, rework, information-flow, and inspection-queue improvements without reducing mandatory controls.
6. Introduce differentiated promises for standard, configured, and engineered-to-order products.
7. Align Sales, Production, Quality, Engineering, Planning, Procurement, Service, Finance, and IT through one order-status process and shared scorecard.

**Current authorization should cover diagnostic work and pilot preparation only.** No shorter customer promise—including six weeks—should be approved until a representative pilot demonstrates stable delivery, quality, reliability, supplier capacity, and economics at the selected service level. Lead-time commitments should be reset only after pilot evidence demonstrates stable delivery and quality performance.

# 1. Current-state diagnosis

## 1.1 Confirmed facts

- Standard quoted lead time is 14 weeks.
- A competitor quotes 6 weeks.
- Sales reports losing approximately one-third of delivery-speed-sensitive opportunities.
- Certified inspections are required at multiple stages.
- A prior schedule-compression effort was followed by increased warranty claims.
- Production, Quality, and Sales disagree about the causes of delay.

## 1.2 Hypotheses requiring validation

Potential contributors include:

- Production, inspection, or test queues.
- Large batches and changeover delays.
- Material shortages or late supplier receipts.
- Engineering clarification and approval delays.
- Priority changes and schedule resequencing.
- Rework, retest, and nonconformance disposition.
- Incomplete inspection documentation.
- Limited inspector or test-equipment availability.
- Supplier quality holds.
- Excessive commercial risk allowance in the 14-week quote.
- Product-family mix masking faster performance for standard configurations.

These are hypotheses, not established causes.

## 1.3 Common lead-time definition

Use one primary customer measure:

> **Order acceptance to physical shipment of a complete, quality-released unit.**

The metric dictionary must define:

- The order-acceptance event.
- Calendar versus working days.
- Weekends, holidays, and shutdowns.
- Customer-caused holds.
- Order changes, cancellations, split orders, and reopened orders.
- Partial shipments.
- Shipment versus customer receipt.
- Parallel and overlapping activities.

Customer-caused holds should be reported separately, not silently excluded. Performance against the **original promise date** is the primary delivery measure; revised-promise performance is reported separately.

## 1.4 Required data and order population

Analyze all eligible orders from the previous **12–18 months**, supplemented by direct observation. Segment by:

- Standard, configured, and engineered-to-order.
- Product family and configuration.
- Fast, median, and late orders.
- Orders with shortages, supplier delays, quality holds, rework, engineering changes, or expedites.
- Orders produced before, during, and after the prior compression effort.
- Orders associated with warranty or early-life service events.

Link the following records for each order and relevant subassembly:

- Quote and order acceptance.
- Engineering request, review, approval, and release.
- BOM, drawing, routing, and revision release.
- Purchase order, supplier promise, receipt, and usable receipt.
- Material-shortage start and resolution.
- Kitting completion.
- Queue entry, operation start, completion, and release.
- Inspection/test request, start, result, disposition, retest, and release.
- Nonconformance creation, containment, disposition, rework, retest, and closure.
- Packing and shipment.
- Original and revised promise dates.
- Serial, lot, batch, supplier, and process genealogy.

ERP timestamps must be reconciled against physical observation. Missing, negative, overlapping, or implausibly long intervals require data-quality review.

## 1.5 Time classification

Each interval must be classified as:

- Value-adding processing.
- Mandatory certified inspection or test execution.
- Waiting for material, labor, equipment, information, approval, or inspector.
- Production, inspection, or test queue.
- Batch or calendar delay.
- Supplier transit or supplier waiting.
- Engineering approval or change.
- Rework, retest, or correction.
- Nonconformance disposition.
- Downtime or missed start.
- Priority-induced schedule loss.
- Administrative delay.
- Unclassified or missing time.

The lead-time waterfall must distinguish:

- Critical-path time.
- Parallel activity.
- Additive delay.
- Overlapping activity.

Functional durations must not simply be added when activities occur concurrently.

## 1.6 Baseline measures

Report overall and by product family:

| Measure | Required statistics |
|---|---|
| Order-to-ship lead time | Median, P75, P80, P90, P95 |
| Quote and promise | Quoted, original promised, revised promised, actual |
| Quote accuracy | Median and P90 absolute error |
| Touch time | Median and P90 by operation |
| Queue and waiting time | Median and P90 by operation |
| Material delays | Frequency, days per affected order, P90 |
| Engineering delays | Cycle time, aging, revision count |
| Rework | Incidence and added days/hours |
| Inspection/test | Queue, execution, hold, retest, disposition |
| Scheduling | Adherence, priority changes, frozen-window violations |
| Suppliers | OTIF, usable-receipt time, defects, quality holds |
| Quality | FPY, nonconformance rate, test failures, escapes |
| Reliability | Warranty and early-life events by product, process, supplier, revision |

Produce a lead-time waterfall for each major product family:

| Lead-time component | Median days | P90 days | Orders affected |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Order and engineering release | — | — | — |
| Material and supplier readiness | — | — | — |
| Production queues | — | — | — |
| Production touch time | — | — | — |
| Inspection/test queues | — | — | — |
| Inspection/test execution | — | — | — |
| Rework and disposition | — | — | — |
| Packing and shipment | — | — | — |
| Unclassified time | — | — | — |
| Total order-to-ship | — | — | — |

No benefit should be credited until the baseline is sufficiently complete, reconciled, and shown to reduce complete-unit shipment time.

# 2. Warranty and quality diagnosis

## 2.1 Review of the prior compression effort

The **Quality Director** is accountable, supported by Service, Engineering, Supplier Quality, Production, and Reliability.

Compare products made before, during, and after the prior compression effort, including:

- Warranty failure mode and severity.
- Product, component, supplier, process, shift, and revision.
- Nonconformances, rework, retests, deviations, and test escapes.
- Overtime, staffing, training, and qualification changes.
- Material substitutions and supplier changes.
- Inspection and test content, duration, and sequence.
- Process capability and critical-parameter records.
- Installation and field-use conditions.
- Corrective-action effectiveness.

The review must identify:

- Affected serial and lot populations.
- Existing containment.
- Root cause or remaining uncertainty.
- Residual risk.
- Corrective-action effectiveness.
- Whether unresolved high-severity failure modes block related acceleration.

The company must not assume that speed alone caused the warranty increase. However, the timing makes it a material risk signal. Any unresolved high-severity failure mode related to the proposed pilot route blocks that route until Quality and Engineering approve a residual-risk decision.

## 2.2 Mandatory quality controls

Unless formally changed through the approved quality and certification process, retain:

- Approved design, BOM, routing, and revision.
- Material and component certificates.
- Lot, batch, and serial traceability.
- Required in-process, final, and certified inspections.
- Regulatory or customer witnessing.
- Required functional and performance tests.
- Qualified personnel and approved procedures.
- Calibrated and suitable measurement equipment.
- Nonconformance identification, segregation, disposition, and closure.
- Engineering approval of deviations, substitutions, and repairs.
- Independent Quality release authority.
- Complete objective evidence before shipment.

Production owns readiness. Quality owns independent release and may stop or prevent shipment.

No executive, Sales, or Production decision may bypass a mandatory certified control or independent Quality release.

## 2.3 Quality-gate matrix

Before pilot launch, document for each gate:

- Requirement source: regulation, certification, contract, customer, or internal control.
- Required evidence.
- Performer and verifier.
- Release authority.
- Permitted parallel activity.
- Escalation time.
- Change-control requirement.
- Record-retention requirement.

For every proposed change affecting inspection, testing, sampling, sequencing, supplier controls, staffing qualification, records, materials, tooling, or routing, Quality must document:

- Applicable requirements.
- Whether customer, regulatory, or certification-body notification is required.
- Validation or requalification required.
- Updated documents and training.
- Effective date and affected serial/lot range.

No pilot launch may occur with an unresolved certification determination.

# 3. Prioritized interventions

Priorities will be ranked using a documented 1–5 matrix covering:

- Measured delay contribution.
- Frequency.
- Customer impact.
- Financial benefit.
- Quality and reliability risk.
- Implementation effort and time.
- Reversibility.
- Controllability.

The score is a prioritization aid, not a precise benefit forecast.

| Opportunity | Evidence required | Initial posture |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce production queues | Queue, WIP, throughput, and constraint data | Pilot if confirmed |
| Reserve inspection/test capacity | Request-to-start and hold data | Prioritize |
| Reduce rework | Defect Pareto, FPY, and rework data | Prioritize |
| Target critical-part inventory | Shortage frequency, delay, demand, obsolescence | Pilot selected parts |
| Pre-approved configurations | Engineering cycle time and option data | Prioritize standard family |
| Supplier capacity agreements | Usable-receipt and quality performance | Prioritize ranked suppliers |
| Smaller batches | Setup, batch, and queue data | Pilot selectively |
| Add permanent capacity | Sustained demand and constraint evidence | Data-dependent |
| Reduce mandatory inspection | Certification and risk review | Not an initial action |

All benefits remain **to be quantified after the baseline**.

## 3.1 Engineering and order release

For standard products:

- Pre-release drawings, BOMs, routings, inspection plans, and test procedures.
- Define approved option combinations.
- Create a rapid standard-order review lane.
- Set response-time targets for clarification and approval.
- Establish a requirements freeze point.
- Apply formal change control after the freeze.

Sales may not issue a standard promise for incomplete, technically invalid, or unapproved configurations.

## 3.2 Material and inventory

Prioritize parts that:

- Stop otherwise complete units.
- Have long or highly variable supplier lead times.
- Are common across products.
- Have stable demand and low obsolescence risk.
- Have high order-level customer impact.

For each proposed stocked part, document:

- Shortage frequency and delay days.
- Demand variability.
- Supplier lead-time P50/P90.
- Target service level.
- Reorder-point and safety-stock method.
- Carrying cost and cash impact.
- Obsolescence and engineering-change exposure.
- Expected effect on complete-order shipment.
- Maximum age and exit rule.

Use targeted stock, blanket orders, scheduled releases, supplier capacity reservations, and pre-kitting. Do not use unapproved substitutions or broad inventory increases.

Prebuilt modules require quantity limits, demand-coverage limits, traceability, storage controls, aging reviews, and disposition rules.

## 3.3 Production flow and batching

Once the constraint is validated:

- Use finite-capacity scheduling.
- Set WIP limits before constrained resources.
- Release work only when materials, documents, and downstream capacity are ready.
- Reduce batches where setup economics justify it.
- Sequence compatible products to control changeovers.
- Pre-stage tools and fixtures.
- Use standardized setup and first-piece verification.
- Protect the constraint from incomplete or defective work.

WIP limits must reflect throughput, product mix, setup needs, downstream inspection/test capacity, and maximum acceptable aging. They are reviewed weekly during the pilot.

## 3.4 Scheduling discipline

Establish:

1. Monthly sales and operations planning.
2. Weekly master production scheduling.
3. Daily finite-capacity scheduling.
4. Visible dispatch rules.
5. A five-working-day frozen execution window.
6. A documented priority and expedite process.

Each priority change records:

- Requestor and reason.
- Displaced orders.
- Capacity and quality implications.
- Recovery plan.
- Approver.

## 3.5 Rework and information defects

Use:

- First-piece verification after setup, tooling, program, or material changes.
- Revision-controlled point-of-use instructions.
- Controlled recording of critical parameters.
- Error-proofing for part selection and orientation.
- Immediate defect containment.
- Defined nonconformance response times.
- Daily defect Pareto reviews.
- Verified rework and retest.

Reducing rework is generally safer than reducing inspection.

## 3.6 Supplier performance

Measure supplier performance from company need date to **usable receipt**, including incoming inspection holds.

For critical suppliers:

- Share rolling forecasts.
- Confirm realistic capacity and commit dates.
- Use blanket orders or scheduled releases.
- Establish recovery plans for late or defective material.
- Qualify alternate sources through Engineering, Quality, Reliability, and Procurement.
- Consider reduced incoming inspection only after demonstrated capability and formal quality-system approval.

Supplier qualification requires technical equivalence, validation builds or first-article approval where applicable, process assessment, traceability, certificate requirements, ongoing verification, and reversion triggers.

## 3.7 Quality flow around mandatory gates

Subject to formal quality-system review:

- Reserve inspectors and test equipment.
- Require complete kits, records, and approved documents before requesting inspection.
- Pre-stage calibrated gauges, fixtures, adapters, and procedures.
- Digitize results and route dispositions immediately.
- Remove avoidable calendar batching.
- Conduct administrative review in parallel with physical preparation where independence is preserved.
- Set disposition response-time targets.
- Use risk-based sampling only where formally permitted, validated, documented, and approved.

The initial pilot must not shorten, skip, combine, or reduce mandatory certified inspection or test content.

# 4. Quality protections and pilot controls

## 4.1 Pilot scope

Select one stable standard product family with:

- Sufficient order volume.
- Repeatable configurations.
- Known route and quality requirements.
- Comparable historical orders.
- Manageable supplier and certification complexity.

## 4.2 Permitted pilot changes

The pilot may include:

- Approved configuration rules.
- Targeted critical-part inventory.
- Material and document pre-kitting.
- Finite-capacity scheduling.
- WIP limits.
- Smaller batches.
- Reserved inspection/test slots.
- Complete-kit readiness checks.
- Standardized work and first-piece checks.
- Digital Quality disposition.
- Enhanced layered audits.

It must not include:

- Unapproved substitutions.
- Skipped or shortened certified gates.
- Undocumented deviations.
- Reduced test coverage.
- Uncontrolled overtime.
- Shipment of incomplete or unreleased product.

Each pilot order must record which interventions were applied.

## 4.3 Pilot measurement protocol

Before launch, approve:

- Minimum matched baseline and pilot order count, calculated from baseline variance and the minimum meaningful improvement.
- Minimum calendar duration covering normal supplier and inspection cycles.
- Matching rules for configuration and complexity.
- Primary outcome: P80 or P90 order-to-ship lead time, only if the sample supports that percentile.
- Secondary outcomes: queue time, shortage days, FPY, rework, test failures, nonconformance aging, cost, original-promise adherence, supplier performance, and early-life events.
- Statistical method or control-chart approach.
- 30/60/90/180-day early-life monitoring.
- Independent review by Quality and Finance.

If the sample cannot support a P90 conclusion, report flow-time distributions and control-chart evidence without claiming that P90 performance has been proven.

## 4.4 Pilot acceptance criteria

Expansion requires:

- Demonstrated reduction in the selected lead-time measure.
- No missed certified gate.
- Complete traceability and objective evidence.
- Stable or improved FPY, rework, test performance, and capability.
- Stable supplier and material performance.
- No benefit caused by partial shipment, uncontrolled overtime, or incomplete inventory.
- Acceptable incremental cost and inventory exposure.
- Approved work instructions, control plan, FMEA, training, and ownership.

## 4.5 Stop and rollback criteria

Before launch, issue a signed threshold table showing:

- Baseline.
- Warning limit.
- Stop limit.
- Measurement method.
- Review frequency.
- Immediate action.
- Decision authority.

Immediate stop or containment is required for:

- Missed or bypassed certified inspection, test, or release.
- Traceability or objective-evidence gap.
- Unapproved supplier, material, substitution, deviation, or process change.
- Critical characteristic out of specification without approved disposition.
- Critical test failure exceeding the pre-approved threshold.
- Material deterioration in FPY, rework, repeat defects, or retest.
- Unstable or inadequate capability for critical characteristics.
- Calibration or measurement-system failure.
- Nonconformance aging beyond the approved limit.
- Plausible safety, field, or early-life failure linked to the pilot.
- Safety, qualification, fatigue, or excessive-overtime concern.
- Lead-time improvement achieved through incomplete or held inventory.

A missed mandatory gate or traceability gap is an automatic stop regardless of statistical significance.

Rollback requires:

1. Serial/lot containment.
2. Customer-impact assessment.
3. Restoration of the validated process.
4. Root-cause analysis.
5. Corrective-action plan.
6. Quality authorization before restart.

# 5. Product segmentation and quoting policy

## Standard configurations

- Pre-released technical data.
- Approved option combinations.
- Common parts available or supplier capacity reserved.
- Standard route and quality plan.
- Reserved production, inspection, and test capacity.
- System-generated promise date.

## Configured products

- Validated configuration.
- Defined engineering review service level.
- Common modules prebuilt or stocked where justified.
- Order-specific material triggered after configuration confirmation.
- Promise issued after technical and capacity checks.

## Engineered-to-order products

- Separate quote category.
- Explicit engineering, supplier, certification, test, and capacity assumptions.
- No automatic access to the standard promise.

Six weeks remains a strategic benchmark, not an approved target.

## Quote-system controls

- **Planning Manager:** owns the promise-date algorithm.
- **Sales Operations Director:** owns configuration and product-family rules.
- **IT/ERP Product Owner:** owns integration and data freshness.
- Promise calculations are timestamped.
- Missing or stale data triggers a conservative fallback.
- Manual overrides require reason code, approver, expiry, and audit.
- Original-promise accuracy is reported.
- Partial shipments are reported separately and cannot improve the primary complete-unit KPI.
- An unapproved accelerated route cannot be quoted.

Lead-time commitments should be reset only after pilot evidence demonstrates stable delivery and quality performance. A product-family quote may be shortened only when representative P80/P90 performance is demonstrated at the selected service level with stable quality, reliability, supply, capacity, and economics.

| Quality gates, stop/restart, and release | Quality Director |
| Customer promise communication | Sales Operations Director |
| Early-life field surveillance | Service/Reliability Manager |
| Financial validation | Finance Business Partner |

These roles must be formally assigned to named individuals, with due dates, backup owners, required inputs, and escalation routes.

## 6.2 Decision rights

- Quality has final authority over certification, mandatory controls, shipment release, stop, and restart.
- Engineering has design authority and approves technical substitutions or deviations.
- Planning owns promise-date calculation.
- Procurement owns supplier commitments and recovery.
- The executive sponsor decides investment, customer allocation, and commercial trade-offs only within Quality and certification constraints.
- No function may override another function’s assigned safety, compliance, or release authority.

## 6.3 Meeting cadence

| Meeting | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Constraint control | Daily, 15 minutes | Remove blockers for orders due within two weeks |
| Order-risk review | Twice weekly | Align Sales, Planning, Engineering, Procurement, and Quality |
| Quality/reliability review | Weekly | Review FPY, defects, tests, nonconformances, and field signals |
| Supplier review | Weekly during pilot | Manage critical supplier commitments |
| S&OP | Monthly | Align demand, capacity, inventory, suppliers, and investment |
| Steering committee | Biweekly | Review milestones, risks, benefits, and decisions |
| Pilot gate review | Weeks 4, 8, and 12 | Continue, correct, expand, or roll back |

Every action must have one owner and due date.

## 6.4 Shared scorecard

Measure jointly:

- On-time shipment against original promise.
- Quote accuracy.
- Median and P80/P90 lead time.
- Waiting versus touch time.
- FPY.
- Rework and cost of poor quality.
- Warranty and early-life reliability.
- Expedite frequency.
- Promise-date revisions.
- Critical shortage days.
- Supplier usable-receipt performance.

The metric dictionary must define formulas, data sources, timing, inclusions, exclusions, partial shipments, customer holds, targets, warning limits, and anti-gaming controls.

# 7. Phased roadmap

## Days 0–30: Mobilize and establish facts

### Actions

- Appoint sponsor, Program Director, and named workstream owners.
- Approve lead-time and metric definitions.
- Freeze undocumented fast-track practices.
- Preserve all certified gates.
- Begin warranty-event review.
- Select pilot family.
- Extract and reconcile 12–18 months of data.
- Begin direct observation.
- Establish the shared order-status board.
- Publish initial scorecard.

### Exit criteria

- Approved data dictionary.
- Product-family segmentation.
- Data-quality gap list.
- Warranty review plan.
- Pilot charter and governance.
- Quality-gate matrix initiated.

No numerical lead-time reduction target is committed.

## Days 31–60: Diagnose and prepare

### Actions

- Complete lead-time waterfalls.
- Quantify queue, batching, shortage, approval, scheduling, supplier, rework, inspection, and hold losses.
- Confirm the bottleneck by product family.
- Rank opportunities using the approved matrix.
- Define WIP and release rules.
- Prepare component inventory business cases.
- Define supplier recovery and qualification actions.
- Complete FMEA, control-plan, certification, calibration, and training reviews.
- Define pilot sample size, duration, statistical method, thresholds, and rollback plan.
- Reserve inspection and test capacity.

### Gate

The Steering Committee and Quality Director approve pilot launch only if measured delay and the control plan justify it.

## Days 61–90: Execute and evaluate the pilot

### Actions

- Launch the standard-family pilot.
- Apply finite-capacity scheduling, WIP limits, pre-kitting, readiness checks, and reserved Quality capacity.
- Use approved standard work and enhanced layered audits.
- Review pilot orders twice weekly.
- Monitor flow and quality indicators daily.
- Compare with matched baseline orders.
- Conduct early-life field surveillance.
- Contain adverse signals immediately.

### Gate

Continue, correct, expand, or roll back against the signed criteria. Do not change the quote band based only on an improved average.

## Months 4–6: Stabilize and expand

Only if the pilot passes:

- Expand proven practices to additional standard families.
- Integrate promise logic into ERP/CRM.
- Formalize critical supplier agreements.
- Qualify alternate sources where justified.
- Cross-train constrained resources.
- Add temporary capacity where evidence supports it.
- Introduce modular prebuild or postponement where justified.
- Retire unofficial priority systems and spreadsheets.
- Set product-family quote bands from demonstrated performance.

No specific 8-, 9-, or 10-week promise is assumed in advance.

## Months 6–9: Structural changes where justified

- Add capacity at validated constraints.
- Establish product-family cells where volume supports them.
- Add inspection/test equipment or shifts if justified.
- Complete second-source qualification.
- Negotiate supplier capacity reservations.
- Improve setup and changeover performance.
- Formalize inventory and postponement policies.

## Months 9–12 and beyond: Institutionalize

- Recalculate lead-time bands quarterly.
- Use control charts for critical processes.
- Review inventory and obsolescence.
- Audit WIP, promise, quality, and change-control adherence.
- Continue 30/60/90/180-day early-life monitoring and six-/twelve-month reliability reviews.
- Link incentives to shared outcomes.
- Revisit inspection sequencing or sampling only through formal Quality and certification review.
- Reassess the bottleneck as improvements shift the system.

A six-week offer may eventually be credible for selected standard configurations, but only if demonstrated at the required service level without quality, certification, or reliability deterioration.

# 9. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Faster flow increases defects | Pilot, first-piece checks, layered audits, FPY and reliability monitoring | Quality/Production |
| Mandatory gate is bypassed | Quality-gate matrix, independent release, automatic stop authority | Quality |
| Inventory becomes obsolete | Targeted stock, aging review, exit rules, postponement | Supply Chain/Finance |

| Supplier acceleration reduces quality | Qualification, audits, incoming-risk review, corrective action | Procurement/Supplier Quality |
| Bottleneck shifts | Re-run waterfall and capacity review | Program Director |
| Expediting becomes normal | Frozen schedule, displacement cost, root-cause review | Planning |
| Complex orders enter standard flow | Eligibility rules and engineering review | Sales/Engineering |
| Overtime creates fatigue or errors | Limits, qualification checks, staffing and safety controls | Production/HR |
| Certification breach | Formal change control and external consultation where required | Quality |
| Data is inaccurate | Timestamp reconciliation and physical observation | IT/Industrial Engineering |
| Warranty signal appears late | Serial tracking, matched cohorts, early-life surveillance | Service/Quality |
| Pilot benefit is only inventory movement | Complete-order shipment KPI and inventory aging controls | Planning/Finance |

Each risk receives an initial rating, mitigation, residual rating, trigger, contingency, owner, and review date.

# 11. Success definition

The program is successful when the company can demonstrate that:

- Actual lead time and its causes are measured by product family.
