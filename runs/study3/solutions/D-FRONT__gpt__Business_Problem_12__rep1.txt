# Sustainable Tourism Development Plan

## Executive Summary

This plan sets out a limits-led approach to attracting international visitors while protecting ecosystems, heritage, culture, housing, public services, and community well-being.

The region will pursue **higher-value tourism rather than unrestricted visitor growth**. Priority will be given to longer stays, greater local spending, off-season travel, low-impact mobility, local ownership, and community benefit. Visitor numbers may be capped, redirected, reduced, or suspended whenever ecological, cultural, safety, housing, infrastructure, or resident-well-being thresholds are exceeded.

Implementation will proceed in stages:

1. **Safeguards and feasibility:** establish legal authority, baselines, site limits, budgets, enforcement, housing protections, and community agreements.
2. **Two controlled pilots:** test products, visitor-management systems, local benefit models, and monitoring.
3. **Conditional expansion:** expand only products and destinations that meet measurable environmental, social, cultural, financial, safety, and accessibility requirements.
4. **Long-term consolidation:** adapt limits, restore damaged sites, strengthen resilience, and diversify local livelihoods.

Broad international promotion and major tourism investment will remain restricted until management systems are funded, operational, and independently verified.

---

## 1. Purpose, assumptions, and priorities

### Strategic objective

> Build an internationally attractive, higher-value tourism economy while keeping ecological impacts, heritage pressures, cultural change, housing effects, infrastructure demand, and resident disruption within agreed and enforceable limits.

### Planning assumptions

Because the region and its legal, ecological, economic, and infrastructure conditions are not specified:

- Visitor limits will be site-specific, not based on a universal regional quota.
- Numeric targets will be finalized after baseline studies.
- Indicative financing ranges are planning guidance, not approved budgets.
- Some ecological, housing, climate, and social trends will require more than one annual cycle to assess.
- Existing laws may not provide all required powers; legal reforms will therefore be identified during the first year.

### Priority hierarchy

When objectives conflict, decisions will follow this order:

1. Legal, Indigenous, customary, community, and cultural rights; resident safety; and protection of sacred or private places.
2. Ecological integrity, climate resilience, and heritage conservation.
3. Adequate water, sanitation, waste, transport, housing, health, and emergency capacity.
4. Fair local ownership, employment, and benefit distribution.
5. Visitor quality, length of stay, and local expenditure.
6. Growth in visitor numbers, only where all higher priorities are satisfied.

No arrival, revenue, investment, or marketing target may override a binding ecological, heritage, cultural-rights, housing, safety, infrastructure, or resident-well-being threshold.

---

## 2. Guiding principles

1. **Limits before promotion:** no major promotion or development at sites without enforceable management systems.
2. **Value over volume:** prioritize longer stays, local spending, lower-impact products, and off-season travel.
3. **Community rights and ownership:** affected communities must have meaningful decision-making power and a fair share of benefits.
4. **Precaution:** where evidence is incomplete, begin with conservative limits and an uncertainty margin.
5. **Cultural integrity:** some places, knowledge, ceremonies, and practices will remain private or non-touristic.
6. **Resident well-being:** monitor housing, public access, congestion, employment quality, and cost-of-living effects.
7. **Climate responsibility:** reduce emissions and resource consumption directly; offsets cannot substitute for reductions.
8. **Accessibility and inclusion:** include people with disabilities and underrepresented groups in tourism, employment, consultation, and governance.
9. **Transparency:** publish fees, contracts, ownership, impacts, decisions, closures, and corrective actions.
10. **Financial viability:** projects must have funded operating and maintenance arrangements, not only capital finance.
11. **Adaptive management:** management will change when monitoring shows that limits are being approached or exceeded.
12. **Equal enforcement:** rules will apply equally to local, national, international, small, large, public, and politically connected operators.

---

## 3. Outcomes and target-setting

During the first five-year program, the government will seek to achieve:

- Stable or improving condition of priority ecosystems and heritage assets.
- No priority site operating above approved ecological, heritage, social, safety, or infrastructure limits.
- Increased visitor expenditure and length of stay without uncontrolled arrival growth.
- Increased local employment, procurement, ownership, and income retention.
- Stable or improved resident satisfaction.
- No unacceptable tourism-related displacement or loss of public access.
- Reduced water use, waste, energy use, and emissions per visitor-night.
- Improved accessibility, labor conditions, safety, and cultural-protocol compliance.
- Funded systems for visitor management, monitoring, enforcement, restoration, and community benefit sharing.

### Baseline assessment: months 0–12

The tourism agency, independent researchers, authorities, and communities will establish baselines for:

- Visitor flows, spending, length of stay, seasonality, transport, and complaints.
- Biodiversity, habitat condition, erosion, water availability, waste, emissions, and wildlife disturbance.
- Heritage condition, visitor damage, archaeological risk, and maintenance needs.
- Resident attitudes, rents, land prices, evictions, short-term rentals, and public access.
- Jobs, wages, procurement, ownership, taxes, and revenue leakage.
- Accessibility, health, safety, emergency, utility, and infrastructure capacity.
- Climate, public-health, security, cyber, biosecurity, and market risks.

For every core indicator, the approved management plan will specify:

- Baseline and uncertainty range.
- Five-year target and annual milestones.
- Warning and critical thresholds.
- Collection method and frequency.
- Data owner and budget.
- Independent verification method.
- Mandatory corrective action.

Site limits will be reviewed after at least one full annual cycle, following major climate or infrastructure changes, and at least every three years.

## 4. Governance, accountability, and legal authority

### 4.1 Lead agency

The regional tourism agency will:

- Coordinate the strategy and annual work program.
- Manage market research, branding, and publicly funded promotion.
- Maintain a public tourism and sustainability dashboard.
- Link grants, concessions, licensing support, and marketing eligibility to compliance.
- Coordinate investment screening.
- Publish annual sustainability, financial, market, and performance reports.
- Ensure that agency performance is not measured by arrivals alone.

A program director with cross-government coordination authority will be appointed within 30 days.

### 4.2 Destination Sustainability Council

A compact council will be established within 60 days and supported by technical working groups. It will include representatives of:

- Tourism, environment, heritage, planning, housing, labor, transport, finance, public health, and emergency authorities.
- Protected-area and heritage-site managers.
- Local governments.
- Indigenous, customary, and community-rights institutions.
- Residents, tenants, workers, informal operators, farmers, fishers, artisans, and local businesses.
- Accessibility organizations, universities, civil society, utilities, waste providers, insurers, transport providers, and digital platforms where relevant.

Representation rules, selection procedures, terms, quorum, voting, conflicts of interest, minutes, and dissenting views will be public. Reasonable participation costs will be reimbursed for community and civil-society representatives.

The council will approve site-management plans, review monitoring, oversee benefit sharing, coordinate agencies, and publish decisions. It may not override statutory environmental, heritage, planning, housing, safety, or legally protected community rights.

### 4.3 Local destination committees

Each pilot destination will establish a local committee responsible for:

- Participatory planning and product approval.
- Cultural protocols and interpretation review.
- Community benefit agreements.
- Local tourism funds.
- Resident-impact monitoring.
- Grievance escalation.

Membership must include affected residents, tenants, women, youth, minority groups, persons with disabilities, customary authorities, informal and seasonal workers, and businesses—not only landowners or investors.

### 4.4 Authority matrix

Within six months, the government will publish a legally reviewed matrix identifying:

| Function | Responsible authority |
|---|---|
| Site-entry, group, vehicle, boat, and accommodation limits | Relevant protected-area, heritage, planning, or local authority |
| Site closure and reopening | Statutory site manager or designated emergency authority |
| Land-use and building approvals | Planning and local government authorities |
| Short-term-rental regulation | Housing or local government authority |
| Operator licensing | Tourism or sector regulator |
| Wildlife and heritage enforcement | Environmental, heritage, police, customs, and prosecution authorities |
| Fee collection and allocation | Finance authority and designated funds |
| Concession approval and termination | Public asset owner, subject to safeguards |
| Cultural access and representation approval | Authorized community or cultural institution |
| Marketing suspension | Tourism agency, following site-management triggers |
| Appeals | Designated administrative or judicial body |

The matrix will specify required approvals, consent requirements, decision deadlines, emergency powers, appeals, the consequence of inaction, and funding responsibilities. Legal amendments will be prepared by month 12 where necessary.

### 4.5 Conflict resolution

Environmental, heritage, safety, housing, and legally protected rights are minimum conditions and cannot be overridden by tourism objectives.

For ordinary disputes:

1. A technical options paper is prepared within 30 days.
2. Affected communities and statutory authorities review the options.
3. The council issues a recommendation with reasons.
4. The legally competent authority decides within 60 days.
5. The decision, evidence, dissenting views, and appeal route are published.

For urgent risks, the responsible authority may impose an immediate protective order or closure, followed by technical review within 30 days.

### 4.6 Oversight and grievances

An independently appointed oversight body will review environmental, cultural, financial, labor, housing, accessibility, and community outcomes annually. It will have protected access to records and may publish dissenting findings.

A free, confidential, multilingual, accessible, and offline grievance system will:

- Acknowledge complaints within 10 working days.
- Resolve or explain unresolved cases within 30–60 days.
- Protect complainants and whistleblowers.
- Provide appeal and remedy options.
- Publish anonymized statistics.

## 5. Spatial planning and visitor management

### 5.1 Zoning

Destinations will use five zones:

1. **Strict conservation or cultural-protection zones:** no general tourism or only controlled access.
2. **Controlled visitor zones:** permits, reservations, guides, small groups, seasonal restrictions, and designated routes.
3. **Community tourism zones:** locally managed accommodation, guiding, food, crafts, and approved cultural activities.
4. **Service and development zones:** accommodation, utilities, transport, and visitor services away from fragile areas.
5. **Restoration zones:** closed or restricted until ecological, heritage, or social recovery occurs.

Zoning will be incorporated into statutory land-use plans where possible. Cumulative effects will be assessed across habitats, watersheds, heritage landscapes, roads, utilities, and communities.

### 5.2 Site-management sheets

No priority site will be promoted internationally until it has an approved and funded management sheet specifying:

- Baseline condition, evidence quality, and uncertainty.
- Maximum daily and annual entries.
- Maximum group, vehicle, boat, bed, accommodation, and activity levels where relevant.
- Ecological, heritage, social, water, waste, noise, safety, and operational thresholds.
- Monitoring methods and frequency.
- Enforcement authority, staffing, equipment, and budget.
- Emergency and evacuation arrangements.
- Closure, restoration, and reopening criteria.
- Resident access provisions.
- Fees and benefit-sharing arrangements.
- Review date and public basis for the limits.

Capacity will consider physical, ecological, heritage, social, safety, operational, water, waste, energy, and emergency conditions.

High-risk destinations will also control total tourist beds, short-term rentals, cruise or excursion arrivals, coaches, vehicles, and water consumption where necessary.

### 5.3 Trigger-action system

| Status | Required action |
|---|---|
| Normal | Routine operation and monitoring |
| Early warning | Increase inspections, visitor information, and data collection |
| Operating threshold | Redirect demand, reduce group sizes, adjust fees, restrict bookings, or suspend activities |
| Critical threshold | Immediately reduce or close access, suspend promotion, investigate, and begin restoration |
| Repeated breach | Fine, suspend, or cancel permits; review concessions or project approvals |

Critical breaches involving safety, rights, ecological damage, heritage damage, or infrastructure failure trigger immediate temporary action. Reopening requires documented recovery and approval by the legally competent authority.

Resident access will be protected through resident rates, allocation days, community booking quotas, or other locally appropriate measures.

## 6. Environmental protection and climate resilience

### 6.1 Infrastructure approval test

No new accommodation, attraction, road, utility extension, or visitor facility may proceed without demonstrating:

- Reliable water supply under normal and drought conditions.
- Adequate wastewater treatment and disposal.
- Solid-waste capacity.
- Energy and grid capacity.
- Health, rescue, communications, and emergency capacity.
- Climate and disaster resilience.
- No unacceptable habitat fragmentation or heritage impact.
- Lifecycle maintenance funding.
- Acceptable housing and public-access effects.
- Required community consent.

Projects will be assessed individually and cumulatively.

### 6.2 Environmental standards

Licensed or publicly supported operators will progressively be required to:

- Meet wastewater and waste-disposal standards.
- Maintain measurable water and energy plans.
- Reduce unnecessary single-use materials.
- Separate and safely dispose of waste.
- Control noise, lighting, runoff, and wildlife disturbance.
- Use climate-appropriate and wildlife-safe design.
- Maintain relevant fire, flood, heat, drought, storm, landslide, and evacuation plans.
- Report significant environmental incidents.

Technical standards will define construction footprints, water use, wastewater quality, energy performance, lighting, noise, accessibility, restoration obligations, and climate resilience.

Additional leading indicators will address groundwater stress, habitat fragmentation, invasive species, wildlife disease, breeding and migration disruption, night lighting, noise, soil compaction, and delayed heritage damage.

### 6.3 Water management

Water allocation will prioritize:

1. Essential household needs.
2. Ecosystem flows and legally protected uses.
3. Agriculture and other essential local livelihoods.
4. Tourism and other discretionary uses.

Large operators must disclose consumption and maintain drought plans. Water-stress triggers will require consumption reductions, restrictions on new bookings or beds, or temporary tourism closures.

### 6.4 Climate scenarios and resilience finance

Infrastructure and major tourism decisions will be tested against moderate, severe, and extreme climate scenarios over the asset’s expected life.

A protected climate-resilience reserve will support:

- Emergency closures and reopening.
- Disaster restoration.
- Redesign, relocation, or decommissioning of vulnerable assets.
- Early-warning systems.
- Temporary support for affected community enterprises.
- Insurance gaps and business continuity.

The reserve will have defined capitalization, release authority, eligible uses, emergency-disbursement deadlines, audit requirements, and replenishment rules. Criteria will be established for permanent closure, managed retreat, or relocation where rebuilding is unsafe or unsustainable.

### 6.5 Emissions

The destination will publish a tourism carbon inventory covering, with stated assumptions:

- International and domestic transport to the region.
- Ground transport.
- Accommodation and visitor facilities.
- Food, waste, and major supply chains.

Reporting will include emissions per visitor and visitor-day. Market selection will consider flight distance, connectivity, expected stay duration, and emissions per visitor-day.

The agency will promote longer stays, fewer internal transfers, public and shared transport, rail, ferry, walking, and cycling where feasible. The destination will not claim to be carbon-neutral or climate-neutral. Offsets may address residual emissions only after direct reductions and must be independently verified.

## 7. Heritage and cultural integrity

### 7.1 Heritage management

By month 18, all priority heritage assets will have:

- Condition and risk assessments.
- Visitor and conservation limits.
- Maintenance and conservation budgets.
- Emergency plans.
- Accurate, multilingual interpretation.
- Visitor-flow and accessibility plans.
- Named managers and enforcement arrangements.

Unauthorized excavation, artifact removal, damage, and commercial exploitation will be addressed through inspections, police and customs cooperation, penalties, prosecution, provenance requirements, and controls on online sales and exports.

### 7.2 Cultural classification

Cultural places, knowledge, ceremonies, designs, names, and practices will be classified as:

1. Open for general interpretation.
2. Accessible only with permission or at specified times.
3. Restricted to particular people or purposes.
4. Permanently non-touristic.

Tourism will not require disclosure, photography, filming, performance, or commercialization of restricted practices.

### 7.3 Consent and intellectual property

For tourism involving community-controlled or sensitive cultural resources, agreements will specify:

- All affected rights-holders and authorized decision-makers.
- Representation of women, youth, minorities, tenants, workers, and customary authorities.
- How dissent is recorded and addressed.
- Scope, timing, scale, and conditions of access.
- Attribution, payment, licensing, and ownership.
- Image, data, and intellectual-property rights.
- Visitor conduct.
- Withdrawal, breach, and remedy provisions.

Consent applies to marketing, filming, branding, digital publication, and commercial use where relevant, not only physical access.

Communities may use collective marks, licensing systems, monitoring, takedown procedures, and legal assistance for unauthorized domestic or international use. Sensitive cultural information will not be digitized solely for tourism.

A proportional cultural-impact assessment is required for products involving sacred or sensitive areas, private homes or villages, cultural performances, traditional knowledge, personal stories, filming, photography, digital distribution, or repeated visitor contact.

## 8. Community rights, ownership, housing, and livelihoods

### 8.1 Participation requirements

Each initiative will be assigned a participation level:

- **Information:** routine, low-impact activity.
- **Consultation:** effects on local services or public spaces.
- **Co-design:** tourism products, infrastructure, or marketing involving communities.
- **Consent:** use of community-controlled, customary, Indigenous, sacred, or culturally restricted resources.
- **Shared decision-making or ownership:** major concessions, public land, or long-term projects.

Failure to complete the required level prevents approval, public funding, concession award, or promotion.

Distributional assessments will identify who gains and who bears costs by gender, age, disability, ethnicity or Indigenous identity where lawful and appropriate, land ownership, tenancy, employment, location, and business size.

### 8.2 Investment safeguards

Projects receiving public land, finance, promotion, permits, or concessions must disclose beneficial ownership and include:

- Environmental, heritage, housing, labor, and community-impact assessment.
- Community consent where required.
- Local employment and procurement obligations.
- Fair land and concession terms.
- Public-access guarantees.
- Community equity or revenue sharing where public or community assets are involved.
- Tax compliance and anti-corruption safeguards.
- Performance bonds.
- Review, termination, and post-concession restoration provisions.

Communities will receive independent legal, financial, and technical advice before signing agreements.

All PPPs and concessions require competitive procurement, value-for-money assessment, public disclosure before signature, independent review of amendments, limits on exclusivity, and termination rights for rights violations, environmental harm, insolvency, or repeated non-performance.

### 8.3 Community enterprises

Priority will go to:

- Community-owned accommodation and campsites.
- Cooperatives for guiding, transport, food, crafts, and conservation.
- Local heritage centers and producer markets.
- Community conservation concessions.
- Women-, youth-, minority-, and disability-owned enterprises.

A community-enterprise facility will provide grants, concessional loans, credit guarantees, shared equipment, accounting, insurance, digital booking, and market access.

Financing will include demand assessments, cash-flow forecasts, governance arrangements, debt-sustainability tests, business-continuity plans, financial-literacy support, and grant-first or blended finance for higher-risk start-ups.

### 8.4 Revenue sharing

Before any fee is collected, the government will publish:

- Fee and levy types.
- Collection authority.
- Distribution percentages.
- Administrative-cost ceiling.
- Payment schedule.
- Eligible beneficiaries.
- Community-fund governance.
- Audit procedures.
- Rules for unspent balances.
- Resident discounts or exemptions.

Allocation categories will include site management and enforcement, heritage conservation, local government services, community funds or enterprises, monitoring, restoration, and administration.

Funds will use dual-signature controls, conflict-of-interest rules, public expenditure registers, participatory budgeting where appropriate, audited accounts, and independent social audits. Tourism revenue must supplement, not replace, ordinary conservation, heritage, and resident-service budgets.

### 8.5 Local procurement and leakage

The value-chain program will measure and reduce:

- Imports.
- Foreign ownership.
- Online-platform commissions.
- External tour-operator fees.
- Profit repatriation.
- Tax exemptions.
- External transport and professional services.

Targets will be set by business size and sector for:

- Local food and agricultural purchasing.
- Guiding and cultural services.
- Local transport.
- Maintenance and construction.
- Professional and digital services.
- Local employment and wages.
- Community-owned accommodation.

Verification will use payroll, invoices, supplier surveys, ownership records, tax data, and independent audits. Large operators receiving public benefits will have mandatory local employment and procurement targets; small operators will receive proportional requirements and technical assistance.

### 8.6 Housing and displacement

High-risk destinations must adopt minimum protections before major promotion or investment incentives:

- Registration and enforcement of short-term rentals.
- Protection of long-term rental housing.
- Monitoring of rents, land prices, evictions, land transfers, beneficial ownership, and public access.
- Worker-housing requirements for major projects.
- Community land trusts or cooperative ownership where feasible.
- Anti-displacement clauses in concessions.
- Limits on gated tourism enclaves.
- Guarantees of public access to beaches, trails, waterways, and cultural landscapes.

If approved housing, displacement, or public-access thresholds are exceeded, new tourism approvals and promotion will pause until corrective measures are operating.

### 8.7 Labor standards

Minimum labor standards will be conditions of licensing, marketing eligibility, grants, concessions, and public contracts. They include:

- Formal contracts where legally applicable.
- Lawful wages and working hours.
- Occupational health and safety.
- Protection against harassment, discrimination, trafficking, and exploitation.
- Protections for migrant, seasonal, informal, domestic, and subcontracted workers.
- Anonymous complaints and labor inspections.
- Training and advancement opportunities.

## 9. International markets and products

### 9.1 Priority visitor segments

Market research will focus on visitors likely to stay longer, spend locally, travel outside peak periods, and follow destination rules. Potential segments include:

- Nature and wildlife travelers.
- Heritage and cultural travelers.
- Soft-adventure visitors.
- Responsible premium travelers.
- Educational and special-interest groups.
- Diaspora and culturally connected visitors.

Mass visitation to fragile sites, uncontrolled cruise excursions, and high-volume, low-spend travel will not be the primary strategy.

### 9.2 Market readiness

The tourism agency, foreign-affairs authorities, transport providers, businesses, and emergency services will address:

- Visa and entry information.
- Multilingual service.
- International payments.
- Reliable online and offline booking.
- Safety, health, insurance, and rescue information.
- Crisis communication.
- Responsible trade partnerships.
- International service and accessibility expectations.

Controlled demand testing will assess market size, willingness to pay, booking conversion, length of stay, seasonality, transport costs, fee acceptance, site distribution, emissions, visitor satisfaction, and resident impacts before major investment.

### 9.3 Tourism products

Potential products include:

- Guided nature, wildlife, walking, cycling, and paddling experiences.
- Heritage routes, museums, architecture, archaeology, and historic towns.
- Approved community food, craft, agriculture, and cultural experiences.
- Slow travel using public or shared transport.
- Educational, conservation, and research visits under ethical safeguards.
- Wellness products where culturally and environmentally appropriate.

Every promoted product must have:

- Licensed operators.
- Safety and emergency procedures.
- Environmental rules.
- Cultural approval where relevant.
- Transparent prices and cancellation conditions.
- A local-benefit statement.
- Verified accessibility information.
- Insurance and emergency contacts.
- Complaint and feedback procedures.

### 9.4 Accessibility

Within 18 months, the agency will audit gateways, accommodation, trails, transport, toilets, attractions, websites, booking systems, consultations, workplaces, and grievance mechanisms.

New public infrastructure will meet minimum accessibility and universal-design standards unless an independently justified, time-limited exception is approved. Existing priority facilities will have funded remediation schedules.

Information will accurately describe:

- Step-free access.
- Surfaces, gradients, distances, and seating.
- Accessible toilets and transport boarding.
- Audio, tactile, captioned, sign-language, and plain-language options.
- Dietary, sensory, and medical provisions.
- Limitations and alternative experiences.

Disability organizations will participate in design, inspection, and verification. Misleading accessibility claims will result in correction, removal, loss of marketing eligibility, or penalties where available.

### 9.5 Responsible marketing and claims

Marketing will:

- Promote only products with required safeguards.
- Include visitor codes, cultural expectations, booking rules, and environmental responsibilities.
- Favor longer stays, shoulder seasons, and less-pressured destinations.
- Suspend or redirect campaigns when thresholds are exceeded.
- Avoid precise geotagging of vulnerable sites.
- Prohibit stereotypes such as “untouched,” “primitive,” “exotic,” or “timeless” unless specifically approved in context.
- Publish current limits, closures, fees, accessibility information, and sustainability performance.

A formal claims standard will define permitted environmental, cultural, accessibility, and community-benefit claims; required evidence; validity periods; approval; independent verification; disclosure of limitations; takedown procedures; and penalties. It will apply to government agencies, operators, influencers, online travel agencies, and trade partners.

## 10. Mobility and infrastructure sequence

Investment will proceed in this order:

1. Resident-serving water, sanitation, waste, energy, health, and emergency services.
2. Safe trails, toilets, viewpoints, visitor orientation, and waste facilities.
3. Public and shared transport between gateways, towns, heritage sites, and natural areas.
4. Integrated ticketing and journey information.
5. Park-and-ride facilities and private-vehicle restrictions at sensitive sites.
6. Rescue, medical, communications, and early-warning systems.
7. Accessible public spaces and facilities.
8. Affordable worker accommodation where needed.
9. Low-emission vehicles and charging infrastructure where demand and grid capacity justify them.
10. New visitor attractions only after the preceding systems are funded and operating.

Each project will be scored for environmental risk, resident benefit, accessibility, climate resilience, local ownership, lifecycle cost, maintenance, cumulative effects, and fiscal risk.

## 11. Financing options and budget controls

A costed five- and ten-year investment plan will be completed by month 12. It will include:

- Capital costs by project.
- Staffing and training.
- Monitoring, enforcement, and data systems.
- Conservation and heritage maintenance.
- Water, sanitation, waste, mobility, accessibility, and emergency infrastructure.
- Community-enterprise finance and legal assistance.
- Annual operating and maintenance costs.
- Expected revenue by source.
- Confirmed funding and gaps.
- Low, central, high-demand, and climate-disruption scenarios.
- Debt, guarantee, affordability, and stranded-asset risks.
- Recurrent financing after external grants end.

Indicative planning ranges for available tourism-development funds are:

| Program area | Indicative share |
|---|---:|
| Conservation, heritage maintenance, and restoration | 25–30% |
| Water, sanitation, waste, resilience, and resident-serving infrastructure | 20–25% |
| Enforcement, monitoring, data, and emergency capacity | 10–15% |
| Community enterprises, procurement, and workforce development | 15–20% |
| Accessibility and sustainable mobility | 10–15% |
| Market research and conditional promotion | 5–10% |

These ranges do not authorize expenditure and must be replaced by a needs-based budget.

Potential funding sources include:

- Government capital and operating budgets.
- Site-entry fees and visitor levies.
- Heritage and conservation funds.
- Climate and biodiversity finance.
- Development-bank grants and loans.
- Community-enterprise finance.
- Impact investment.
- Competitive PPPs and concessions.

Tourism fees must be legally ring-fenced where possible. If this is not legally possible, the government must publish equivalent additional budget commitments and independent evidence that tourism revenue benefits conservation and communities.

A protected adaptive-management and climate-resilience budget will finance closures, additional monitoring, restoration, visitor redirection, and temporary livelihood support without waiting for a new annual appropriation.

## 12. Phased action plan

### Phase 1: Immediate safeguards and feasibility — months 0–6

**Accountable stakeholders:** Tourism agency, environment and heritage authorities, local governments, housing, labor, finance, communities, and independent researchers.

Actions:

- Appoint the program director and establish governance arrangements.
- Identify the three to five most vulnerable natural and heritage sites.
- Apply provisional limits, closures, or protections where necessary.
- Begin baseline, climate, infrastructure, housing, accessibility, value-chain, and market studies.
- Audit permits, concessions, public land, infrastructure, and existing impacts.
- Establish grievance, data-protection, and anti-greenwashing systems.
- Begin legal, budget, staffing, and enforcement design.
- Suspend major promotion of fragile sites.

### Phase 2: Site plans and pilot preparation — months 7–18

Actions:

- Complete initial site-management sheets.
- Set provisional numerical thresholds and monitoring methods.
- Confirm legal authorities, appeals, and emergency powers.
- Adopt benefit-sharing, consent, investment, labor, accessibility, housing, and operator standards.
- Recruit and train enforcement and monitoring staff.
- Complete infrastructure and climate-feasibility assessments.
- Establish housing and land safeguards.
- Select two pilot destinations using the published scorecard.
- Collect additional seasonal data where needed.

**Readiness gate:** No pilot may begin without confirmed legal authority, funded enforcement, operating monitoring, verified infrastructure capacity, tested emergency procedures, adopted housing safeguards, signed benefit-sharing agreements, licensed and trained operators, and independently checked accessibility information.

### Phase 3: Pilot implementation — months 19–36

Actions:

- Launch no more than two pilots.
- Install essential, resident-serving infrastructure.
- Establish reservations, permits, fees, enforcement, and revenue sharing.
- Implement community agreements and cultural protocols.
- Train and license guides and operators.
- Test accessible products, public transport, and multi-day itineraries.
- Conduct limited market testing in no more than three international markets.
- Report environmental, social, cultural, economic, safety, and visitor data quarterly.

#### Pilot selection scorecard

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Community consent and readiness | 20% |
| Environmental and heritage risk/manageability | 20% |
| Infrastructure and emergency feasibility | 15% |
| Local ownership and benefit potential | 15% |
| Market viability and access | 10% |
| Climate resilience | 10% |
| Accessibility and inclusion | 5% |
| Monitoring feasibility | 5% |

Scores, evidence, conflicts of interest, and independent review will be published.

#### Pilot expansion gate

Expansion requires:

- No unresolved critical ecological, heritage, safety, rights, housing, or infrastructure breach.
- At least 90% compliance among regulated operators, or a stricter approved target.
- Resident satisfaction within the approved baseline tolerance.
- Local employment, procurement, ownership, and enterprise milestones achieved.
- Community agreements implemented and independently audited.
- Fee allocations paid on schedule and publicly accounted for.
- No unacceptable housing, displacement, or public-access trend.
- Functioning reservation, emergency, accessibility, and grievance systems.
- Financial viability or an explicitly approved public-service subsidy.

### Phase 4: Controlled expansion — years 4–5

Only pilots meeting the expansion gate may scale. Actions include:

- Targeted international promotion.
- Shoulder-season and secondary-destination campaigns.
- Expanded multi-day itineraries.
- Improved shared and public transport.
- Community-enterprise finance.
- Destination-wide operator standards.
- Annual housing, water, labor, ecological, heritage, resident, and climate reviews.
- Immediate suspension or redirection of campaigns when thresholds are breached.

### Phase 5: Consolidation and resilience — years 5–10

- Recalculate site limits using monitoring and climate projections.
- Restore, redesign, or permanently retire deteriorating sites.
- Review concessions, land arrangements, and benefit-sharing.
- Expand complementary livelihoods in agriculture, fisheries, crafts, conservation, and other sectors.
- Update transport, housing, utility, disaster, and climate plans.
- Conduct an independent five-year evaluation.
- Revise laws, budgets, fees, standards, and targets.

## 13. Monitoring framework

The public dashboard will initially contain no more than 15–20 core indicators. Reporting will be proportional to business size, with shared systems and technical support for small operators.

| Area | Core indicators |
|---|---|
| Environment | Habitat condition; erosion; wildlife disturbance; water use per visitor-night; waste per visitor-night; wastewater compliance; emissions per visitor and visitor-night; sites exceeding limits |
| Heritage and culture | Heritage condition; damage or artifact incidents; sites with current management plans; cultural-protocol compliance; practitioner payment; community satisfaction with representation |
| Community | Resident satisfaction; local jobs, wages, and contracts; local procurement; community-enterprise revenue; grievances; rents; land prices; evictions; short-term rentals; public access |
| Economy and markets | Arrivals by market and season; length of stay; local expenditure; secondary-destination share; visitor satisfaction; business survival; external ownership; leakage |
| Governance | Operator compliance; fee transparency; decision and grievance response times; participation; data coverage; independent verification |
| Accessibility and safety | Accessible products and facilities; verified accessibility complaints; serious incidents; emergency-response performance |

### Responsibilities

- **Site managers:** daily or weekly visitor, safety, waste, and incident data.
- **Environmental and heritage authorities:** seasonal or monthly condition data.
- **Local governments:** quarterly housing, infrastructure, and resident-service data.
- **Labor authorities:** annual and risk-based inspections.
- **Tourism agency:** quarterly market and economic reporting.
- **Local committees:** quarterly community-impact and grievance review.
- **Independent reviewers:** annual verification and five-year evaluation.

Data will be disaggregated by location, season, gender, age, disability, ethnicity or Indigenous identity where lawful and appropriate, ownership, and employment type. Sensitive cultural information and personal data will not be published.

### Adaptive-management deadlines

- Critical breach: immediate temporary action.
- Technical investigation: within 30 days.
- Formal decision: within 60 days.
- Public explanation: within 15 days of the decision.
- Reopening: only after documented recovery.
- Annual review: targets, thresholds, budgets, and enforcement capacity.

Each indicator will have a baseline, target, warning threshold, critical threshold, methodology, data owner, budget, verification method, and automatic corrective action.

## 15. Final government decision rules

The government will:

1. Measure success through conservation, resident well-being, local income retention, cultural integrity, safety, accessibility, labor quality, and financial viability—not arrivals alone.
2. Suspend, reduce, redirect, or reverse tourism growth when binding thresholds are exceeded.
3. Prohibit projects that exceed ecological, heritage, housing, infrastructure, water, or emergency capacity.
4. Require consent where legally or culturally applicable; consultation cannot replace consent.
5. Require transparent ownership, performance conditions, community benefits, and public-interest safeguards for public land, funding, and concessions.
6. Protect resident access, affordability, and public use of shared resources.
7. Publish fees, contracts, impacts, closures, decisions, and corrective actions.
8. Prohibit unsupported environmental, cultural, accessibility, and community-benefit claims.
9. Expand pilots only after numeric and documentary pass conditions are met.
10. Maintain funding for monitoring, enforcement, restoration, maintenance, and community resilience.
11. Ensure tourism fees supplement ordinary public funding.
12. Prevent political or commercial pressure from weakening approved safeguards without an equivalent or stronger public review.

## Conclusion

The region should develop a selective, higher-value tourism economy based on protected landscapes, conserved heritage, living culture, skilled local workers, accessible experiences, and community-owned or fairly shared enterprises.

The first year is primarily a safeguards, legal, baseline, budgeting, and feasibility program. The following 18 months should test no more than two carefully selected pilots. Broader international promotion and major investment should occur only where site limits are enforceable, infrastructure and emergency services are adequate, cultural rights are protected, housing pressure is controlled, benefits reach local people, and monitoring confirms that tourism remains within agreed limits.
