# Regional Sustainable Tourism Strategy

## 1. Vision and strategic objectives

### Vision

**A distinctive, high-value tourism economy in which international visitors experience the region’s natural and historical assets responsibly, while ecosystems remain healthy, cultural authority is respected, and tourism income improves local livelihoods and quality of life.**

The region should pursue **managed value growth rather than unrestricted visitor growth**. No marketing or development target should override ecological limits, heritage protection, community consent, or resident welfare.

### Objectives

1. Attract international visitors who stay longer, spend locally, travel beyond peak periods, and comply with destination rules.
2. Protect biodiversity, landscapes, water resources, and climate resilience.
3. Preserve historical sites and ensure communities control the use and interpretation of their culture.
4. Increase local ownership, employment, procurement, and tourism revenue retention.
5. Improve infrastructure and services for residents as well as visitors.
6. Diversify markets, products, locations, and seasons without exporting overtourism to unprepared communities.
7. Establish transparent governance, enforceable limits, reliable data, and adaptive management.

---

## 2. Strategic operating rules

Government should adopt five mandatory gates:

1. **Evidence:** No major promotion or development before baseline conditions and site capacity are assessed.
2. **Rights and consent:** No tourism activity affecting Indigenous, customary, sacred, or community-controlled assets without documented authorization.
3. **Capacity:** No additional demand where ecological, heritage, water, waste, housing, or emergency-service thresholds are exceeded.
4. **Local benefit:** Public incentives and major concessions must include enforceable local employment, procurement, ownership or revenue-sharing, labor, and anti-displacement conditions.
5. **Accountability:** Sustainability claims must be evidence-based, independently verified where material, publicly reported, and subject to remedy.

---

## 3. Assumptions and initial baseline assessment

No regional facts have been supplied on geography, visitor volumes, infrastructure, community attitudes, ecology, heritage condition, or market access. Targets below are therefore **illustrative and conditional**. They must be replaced or calibrated after the baseline.

### Working assumptions to test

- Natural and historical assets are sufficiently distinctive to support international demand.
- Some sites can accommodate additional visitors if access and management improve.
- International visitors can reach the region safely and affordably.
- Local businesses and workers can participate if finance, skills, and market access are provided.
- Government can coordinate tourism, environment, heritage, infrastructure, finance, and local-government functions.
- Visitor demand can be shifted toward suitable sites and seasons.
- Legal mechanisms exist, or can be created, to regulate development, access, fees, operators, and cultural use.

### First 12–18 months: baseline program

Create a publicly available **State of Tourism and Destination Capacity Report** covering:

| Assessment area | Required information |
|---|---|
| Visitor demand | International and domestic arrivals, visitor nights, source markets, purpose, spending, length of stay, seasonality, transport mode, site use |
| Local economy | Local ownership, wages, job quality, procurement, tourism tax contribution, external leakage, business readiness |
| Communities | Resident attitudes, perceived benefits and costs, housing affordability, public-space access, congestion, cultural concerns |
| Natural assets | Sensitive habitats, biodiversity, water availability, trail and landscape condition, pollution, climate hazards |
| Heritage | Site condition, legal status, ownership, visitor impacts, conservation needs, cultural access protocols |
| Infrastructure | Accommodation, roads, public transport, water, sanitation, waste, energy, digital connectivity, emergency services |
| Governance | Legal mandates, staffing, budgets, enforcement powers, land tenure, Indigenous/customary rights, data ownership |
| Markets | Connectivity, visas, competition, demand by season, market acquisition costs, product and distribution gaps |

### Institutional-capacity assessment

Before creating new bodies, assess existing agencies’:

- statutory powers;
- staffing and technical capabilities;
- inspection and enforcement resources;
- procurement and financial-management systems;
- data capacity;
- coordination arrangements;
- conflict-of-interest and corruption risks.

The result should be a **minimum viable governance model**, with one clearly accountable lead authority and formal site-level community decision structures.

## 4. Priority visitor segments and value proposition

### Priority segments

The agency should prioritize segments based on **value and impact**, not volume alone.

1. **Nature-based and conservation-oriented travelers**
   - Wildlife, landscapes, hiking, birding, and environmental learning.
   - Suitable for regulated, guided experiences and conservation fees.

2. **Cultural and heritage travelers**
   - Historical sites, architecture, archaeology, food, crafts, and approved cultural interpretation.
   - Support museums, guides, artisans, community enterprises, and heritage conservation.

3. **Special-interest travelers**
   - Birdwatching, photography, cycling, hiking, wellness, culinary travel, research, and education.
   - Often compatible with longer stays and shoulder-season visitation.

4. **Slow and longer-stay travelers**
   - Prefer deeper itineraries, shared transport, local accommodation, and multiple destinations.

5. **Diaspora and heritage-connected visitors**
   - May generate repeat visitation and strong local spending, subject to respectful cultural protocols.

6. **Responsible premium travelers**
   - Willing to pay for quality, conservation, interpretation, and community benefit.
   - Must not lead to exclusionary development or resident displacement.

### Segments requiring caution

High-volume short-stay groups, cruise or mass-tour products, and activities that create high ecological pressure should be admitted only where site capacity, infrastructure, community consent, and enforcement are adequate.

### Proposed value proposition

> **An authentic nature-and-heritage journey, experienced at an appropriate pace, guided by local knowledge, and contributing directly to conservation and community livelihoods.**

This proposition should be supported by verifiable evidence, not vague claims such as “green,” “authentic,” or “sustainable.”

### Market-screening criteria

Potential source markets should be scored on:

- visitor nights rather than arrivals;
- local expenditure and retained value;
- seasonality;
- transport emissions;
- direct connectivity;
- cultural compatibility;
- willingness to use guided or regulated products;
- likelihood of visiting multiple prepared sites;
- marketing acquisition cost;
- exposure to geopolitical, climate, or economic shocks.

## 5. Coordinated initiative portfolio

## A. Environmental protection and climate resilience

### 1. Site-level ecological management

For each priority natural site:

- identify sensitive habitats and no-go zones;
- establish visitor and vehicle limits;
- monitor wildlife disturbance, erosion, litter, water, and biodiversity;
- regulate lighting, noise, drones, wildlife contact, and off-route travel;
- fund restoration and ranger capacity;
- close or rotate routes when conditions deteriorate.

Apply the mitigation hierarchy:

1. avoid damage;
2. minimize unavoidable impacts;
3. restore affected resources;
4. compensate only for residual impacts that cannot reasonably be avoided.

### 2. Water, waste, and wastewater

- Establish water budgets for high-pressure destinations.
- Require wastewater treatment and prohibit untreated discharge.
- Introduce refill stations, source separation, composting, recycling, and deposit-return systems.
- Require tourism businesses and events to submit waste-management plans.
- Use drought-stage rules requiring visitor or operator reductions when safe water reserves are threatened.
- Prohibit new accommodation where projected dry-season demand exceeds sustainable supply.

### 3. Climate and hazard resilience

- Prepare a tourism emissions inventory, including transport where feasible.
- Screen all major projects for flood, wildfire, heat, storm, drought, and other climate risks.
- Prioritize renovation, passive design, water efficiency, renewable energy, and resilient utilities.
- Create emergency closure, evacuation, and business-continuity procedures.
- Report both **emissions per visitor night and absolute emissions**, since efficiency improvements can be offset by visitor growth.

---

## B. Cultural and historical heritage

### 1. Heritage conservation

- Complete condition assessments and digital inventories.
- Stabilize vulnerable structures before increasing access.
- Use timed entry, group-size limits, route rotation, barriers, and professional conservation.
- Regulate photography, drones, filming, souvenirs, vibration, and commercial use.
- Train local conservators, guides, and interpreters.
- Fund preventive maintenance rather than relying only on major restoration.

### 2. Cultural authority and consent

Use a four-level cultural access framework:

| Level | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Restricted | No visitor access, photography, interpretation, or marketing |
| Community-controlled | Access only with authorization and approved local hosts |
| Regulated public interpretation | Community-approved content, imagery, language, and commercial terms |
| Open cultural product | Voluntarily approved, non-sensitive experience with agreed benefits |

Where Indigenous or customary rights are affected, apply **free, prior, and informed consent**. Communities should control:

- what is shared;
- who may host or interpret it;
- whether photography or filming is allowed;
- pricing and commercial licensing;
- acceptable visitor conduct;
- the right to withdraw an experience.

The right not to perform, disclose, or commercialize cultural practices must be protected.

---

## C. Community-economic development

### 1. Local enterprise and workforce program

Provide:

- language, guiding, hospitality, digital-sales, accounting, food-safety, first-aid, accessibility, and conservation training;
- grants and affordable loans for local businesses;
- shared booking, payment, transport, and marketing services;
- apprenticeships and career pathways;
- support for women, youth, rural residents, Indigenous communities, and low-income entrepreneurs;
- local supplier directories and hotel/operator procurement agreements.

### 2. Define and measure local benefit

The agency should distinguish:

- **local ownership:** resident or community equity and voting control;
- **local procurement:** payments to locally owned suppliers, excluding pass-through purchases;
- **local retention:** spending remaining after imported goods, external debt service, franchise fees, and repatriated profits;
- **job quality:** wages, contract security, benefits, safety, training, and worker turnover.

Illustrative targets, subject to baseline findings, could include:

- a 10–15% increase in average visitor expenditure retained locally within five years;
- a 20–30% increase in local supplier contracts;
- improved real tourism wages and reduced seasonal turnover;
- a rising share of locally owned or community-controlled tourism products.

### 3. Anti-displacement safeguards

Before approving major development:

- complete housing and land-price impact assessments;
- register tourism land acquisitions;
- regulate short-term rentals where they reduce housing availability;
- protect resident access to beaches, trails, public spaces, and culturally important areas;
- consider community land trusts or anti-speculation measures where legally feasible;
- require developers to provide worker and community infrastructure where appropriate.

---

## D. Infrastructure and access

Infrastructure should serve residents first and visitors second.

Priorities:

- safe water and sanitation;
- wastewater and waste collection;
- emergency communications and rescue capacity;
- accessible trails and facilities;
- public or shared transport;
- low-emission shuttles and park-and-ride systems;
- reliable digital connectivity;
- multilingual visitor information;
- climate-resilient roads and utilities.

New roads, airports, ports, resorts, or large accommodation projects should require environmental, heritage, housing, and cumulative-impact assessments. Access improvements must not open fragile sites before management capacity is ready.

---

## E. Tourism products and regional dispersal

Develop integrated nature–heritage itineraries that encourage longer stays and spread spending across prepared communities.

Possible products include:

- guided nature and wildlife observation;
- heritage-town and archaeological routes;
- local food and farm experiences;
- approved craft and cultural workshops;
- hiking, cycling, and wellness;
- research, education, and conservation travel;
- community-hosted experiences where consent exists;
- shoulder-season festivals and learning programs.

Dispersal must be conditional. A site should not be promoted until it has:

- community authorization;
- water, waste, sanitation, and emergency capacity;
- trained providers;
- a management plan;
- a clear visitor limit;
- a mechanism to capture local benefits.

---

## F. International marketing and demand management

### Market approach

Marketing should promote:

- longer stays;
- shoulder and low seasons;
- multiple approved destinations;
- local guides, accommodation, food, and transport;
- advance reservation for fragile sites;
- shared or lower-emission transport;
- conservation and heritage contributions;
- visitor codes of conduct.

Campaigns should use multilingual, booking-ready content and partnerships with qualified tour operators, airlines, transport providers, universities, diaspora organizations, and specialist media.

### Keeping demand within ecological and social limits

International demand generation must be **capacity-led rather than campaign-led**:

1. Each site is classified as:
   - **Green:** additional demand allowed within limits;
   - **Amber:** limited demand with reservations and enhanced monitoring;
   - **Red:** no promotional growth; access restricted or closed;
   - **Community-protected:** access determined by the relevant community authority.

2. Marketing inventory is linked to actual permit and accommodation capacity.

3. Campaigns direct visitors toward:
   - open sites;
   - off-peak periods;
   - longer itineraries;
   - certified or legally compliant local suppliers.

4. Marketing is automatically reduced or redirected when:
   - visitor thresholds are reached;
   - ecological or heritage indicators deteriorate;
   - water or wastewater capacity is constrained;
   - resident support falls below the agreed minimum;
   - housing or congestion pressures worsen.

5. No campaign promotes restricted, closed, or capacity-constrained sites.

6. Marketing contracts include compliance clauses. Operators that breach environmental, cultural, labor, or consumer-protection standards lose public marketing support and may lose licenses.

Campaign success should be measured by **incremental suitable visitor nights, local spending, seasonality, dispersal, compliance, and net contribution**, not impressions or arrivals alone.

---

## 6. Visitor-management and carrying-capacity rules

Carrying capacity should be treated as a **site-specific management system**, not a single regional number. Establish limits of acceptable change for:

- daily and seasonal visitors;
- vehicles and parking;
- group size and visitor-to-guide ratios;
- trail erosion and wildlife disturbance;
- water availability and wastewater;
- noise and nighttime activity;
- heritage footfall, vibration, humidity, and access;
- resident congestion and public-space access;
- emergency-service capacity.

### Management tools

- advance reservations and timed entry;
- quotas and group-size limits;
- peak-period pricing;
- resident and domestic access provisions;
- shuttle and private-vehicle restrictions;
- seasonal closures and route rotation;
- licensed guides;
- visitor codes communicated before arrival;
- multilingual interpretation;
- real-time alerts and closure information;
- penalties for operators and visitors who breach rules.

### Illustrative automatic triggers

| Trigger | Required action |
|---|---|
| Ecological limit exceeded for two monitoring periods | Freeze site marketing, reduce permits, investigate and restore |
| Heritage condition falls beyond approved tolerance | Restrict access, reduce groups, fund conservation |
| Water reserve falls below safe dry-season level | Stop new beds, impose operator reductions, activate drought rules |
| Untreated wastewater or repeated serious noncompliance | Immediate corrective order; suspension for repeat breaches |
| Resident support falls below an agreed floor, such as 60% | Pause expansion and conduct an independent social-impact review |
| Housing affordability materially worsens | Cap short-term rentals and require mitigation |
| Serious cultural-protocol breach | Suspend operator and marketing; community-led review |
| Safety incidents exceed the site threshold | Close or redesign the activity pending independent review |

Thresholds must be set after the baseline and reviewed annually.

## 7. Governance and stakeholder roles

### Destination Sustainable Tourism Council

Establish a legally recognized council to coordinate strategy, approve cross-sector priorities, oversee limits and fee allocation, and publish an annual sustainability report.

Membership should include:

- tourism, environment, heritage, transport, infrastructure, finance, and planning authorities;
- municipalities and protected-area managers;
- Indigenous and traditional-community representatives with defined decision rights;
- resident, women’s, youth, worker, and rural organizations;
- local businesses and accommodation providers;
- conservation organizations, universities, and civil society;
- transport operators and relevant investors.

### Destination Management Organization

A professional DMO should execute:

- product development;
- international marketing;
- trade relations;
- visitor information;
- data and campaign reporting;
- operator support.

The DMO should not replace statutory environmental, heritage, planning, or enforcement authorities.

### Responsibility matrix

| Function | Accountable authority | Implementers |
|---|---|---|
| Site limits and closures | Statutory site manager, with community authority where applicable | Rangers, site staff, municipalities |
| Major development approval | Planning, environmental, and heritage authorities | Developers and contractors |
| Cultural access | Authorized Indigenous/community body | Approved operators and interpreters |
| Marketing | DMO under public oversight | Agencies and distribution partners |
| Operator licensing | Tourism regulator | Inspectors and local authorities |
| Fee allocation | Public finance authority with community oversight | Fund managers and site agencies |
| Monitoring | Independent observatory or university partner | Agencies, operators, researchers |
| Complaints and appeals | Independent grievance or ombuds mechanism | Relevant authority and site managers |

Add conflict-of-interest declarations, public violation registers, independent audits, whistleblower protections, and enforceable permit suspension powers.

---

## 8. Community consent and benefit sharing

Create site-level community tourism committees with representation determined locally and with participation from affected Indigenous or customary authorities.

Mechanisms should include:

- documented consultation and consent processes;
- participatory mapping;
- written benefit-sharing agreements;
- community-controlled development funds;
- local procurement and concession preferences;
- direct stewardship payments;
- cultural intellectual-property protections;
- confidential worker and resident complaints;
- independent dispute resolution;
- annual public reporting by site and beneficiary group.

A provisional revenue allocation might be considered only after a cost assessment. For illustration, public tourism revenues could be allocated among conservation, heritage, community benefit, infrastructure, enforcement, and a disaster reserve. The exact percentages should be based on verified management costs, with a guaranteed minimum community share and independent audit.

---

## 9. Financing and policy instruments

### Financing sequence

1. **Public funding first:** baseline data, governance, legal systems, conservation, heritage protection, and enforcement.
2. **Grant and development finance:** utilities, climate resilience, community enterprises, and workforce development.
3. **Visitor charges:** entry, permits, accommodation or conservation levies once credible management systems are operating.
4. **Private investment:** only after zoning, cumulative-impact assessment, community benefit, and operating-cost requirements are in place.

### Instruments

- park and heritage entry fees;
- conservation or accommodation levies;
- vehicle, filming, trekking, and concession permits;
- differential peak/off-peak pricing;
- tourism taxes where legally feasible;
- green bonds or sustainability-linked finance;
- revolving loan funds for local enterprises;
- public-private partnerships with enforceable safeguards;
- tax or marketing incentives tied to local employment, procurement, resource efficiency, and compliance.

Ring-fence revenues into separate accounts for:

- conservation;
- heritage;
- community benefits;
- basic infrastructure;
- monitoring and enforcement;
- emergency response.

Publish quarterly statements and conduct annual independent audits. “Polluter pays” provisions should recover costs from operators causing measurable damage.

## 10. Phased implementation roadmap

| Phase | Timing | Priorities and early wins |
|---|---:|---|
| **1. Establish and protect** | Years 1–2 | Create governance and legal responsibility matrix; complete baseline; secure community agreements; publish site codes; conduct heritage and ecological condition surveys; pilot timed entry, shuttles, refill stations, waste separation, and local enterprise grants at two or three sites |
| **2. Test and build** | Years 3–5 | Launch approved nature–heritage itineraries; expand training and digital booking; improve shared transport; implement water, wastewater, accommodation, and waste standards; begin targeted international campaigns for green and shoulder-season sites |
| **3. Scale selectively** | Years 6–8 | Expand only sites meeting readiness criteria; strengthen quotas, dynamic pricing, route rotation, and operator licensing; develop climate-resilient infrastructure; deepen international distribution partnerships |
| **4. Consolidate and adapt** | Years 9–10 | Conduct independent strategy review; reset limits and investment priorities; scale successful community products; reduce or close underperforming or damaged sites; institutionalize long-term conservation and heritage finance |

### Early wins within 12 months

- public destination baseline dashboard;
- signed community and Indigenous participation protocols;
- visitor codes at all priority sites;
- temporary protection of vulnerable zones;
- pilot booking and timed-entry system;
- local-guide accreditation;
- local supplier directory;
- multilingual responsible-visitor information;
- transparent destination fund design;
- public commitment not to market sites without management plans.

## 11. Monitoring scorecard

A Destination Sustainability Observatory should collect and publish data, ideally through a university or independent public institution overseen by the council.

| Outcome | KPI | Illustrative target or threshold | Owner | Review |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Visitor value | Average length of stay | Increase 10–15% from baseline, if capacity permits | DMO/statistics office | Quarterly |
| Local value | Spending retained locally | Increase from baseline; no decline during growth | Tourism agency/finance authority | Annual |
| Local participation | Locally owned tourism businesses and products | Baseline first; conditional increase thereafter | Enterprise agency/DMO | Semiannual |
| Jobs | Local share, real wages, safety, turnover | Improve wages and job quality; no growth based solely on low-paid work | Labor authority | Annual |
| Seasonality | Shoulder-season visitor nights | Conditional 15–25% increase without threshold breaches | DMO | Quarterly |
| Concentration | Peak-to-off-peak ratio and site concentration | Reduce at priority sites | DMO/site managers | Monthly in peak season |
| Ecology | Visitors versus site limit; biodiversity, erosion, disturbance | No sustained threshold breach; stable or improving condition | Environment authority | Monthly/seasonal |
| Water | Water per guest night and dry-season reserve | Reduce intensity; no new beds beyond sustainable supply | Utilities/municipalities | Monthly |
| Waste | Waste per visitor and diversion rate | Annual reduction; conditional 50% diversion by Year 5 | Municipalities/operators | Quarterly |
| Wastewater | Compliance rate | 100% at managed tourism sites | Environment/health authorities | Monthly |
| Climate | Absolute emissions and emissions per visitor night | Declining trend; target set after inventory | Transport/energy authority | Annual |
| Heritage | Condition rating and incidents | Stable or improving; closure trigger for material decline | Heritage authority | Quarterly |
| Community | Resident support and perceived benefits | Maintain agreed floor, e.g. 60–70% after baseline | Municipalities/independent surveyor | Annual |
| Housing | Rents, availability, short-term rental share | No tourism-driven deterioration beyond agreed threshold | Planning/housing authority | Quarterly |
| Culture | Protocol breaches and approved experiences | Zero serious breaches; all products authorized | Community authorities/heritage body | Immediate and quarterly |
| Visitor quality | Satisfaction, learning, safety incidents, compliance | Improve quality without crowding growth | DMO/site managers | Quarterly |
| Governance | Public reporting, inspections, resolved complaints | 100% annual reporting; complaints acknowledged within 15 working days | DSTC/ombuds | Quarterly |
| Finance | Fee collection and traceable expenditure | Audited annually; funds linked to published outputs | Finance authority | Quarterly/annual |

### Adaptive-management protocol

- **Green:** indicators within limits; maintain or cautiously expand approved promotion.
- **Amber:** indicator approaches a threshold; freeze additional promotion, increase monitoring, adjust prices or permits, and implement mitigation.
- **Red:** threshold exceeded or serious rights breach; reduce or close access, stop marketing, investigate, restore, and reassess the management plan.

Economic targets cannot be counted as successful if environmental, heritage, cultural, or resident indicators are in the red zone.

---

## 12. Final decision recommendation

Government should approve this as a **conditional sustainable-tourism program**, not as an immediate visitor-growth mandate.

The immediate decision should authorize:

1. the baseline and capacity-assessment phase;
2. legal and institutional clarification;
3. community and Indigenous consent processes;
4. pilot site-management and visitor-control measures;
5. local enterprise and workforce preparation;
6. a protected, audited financing mechanism;
7. market screening and capacity-led international marketing;
8. public monitoring and automatic corrective actions.

The central policy commitment should be:

> **Protect the assets first, establish rights and limits, build local capability, test managed products, and promote only the tourism that the region can safely, fairly, and credibly accommodate.**
