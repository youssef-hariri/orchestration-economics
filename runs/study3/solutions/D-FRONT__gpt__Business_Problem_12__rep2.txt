# Sustainable Tourism Stewardship and International Visitor Strategy

## Executive Summary

This strategy provides a framework for attracting appropriate international visitors while protecting the region’s natural environment, historical sites, living cultures, and local communities.

Tourism growth will be **conditional, phased, and reversible**. International arrivals will not be treated as the primary measure of success. No increase in visitor access, accommodation, transport capacity, marketing, or connectivity will proceed unless ecological, cultural, social, infrastructure, emissions, labor, and enforcement conditions are satisfied.

The strategy prioritizes:

- Longer stays and multi-site itineraries rather than high-volume short visits.
- Nature, heritage, rural, educational, wellness, and low-impact active tourism.
- Local ownership, employment, procurement, and revenue retention.
- Community participation and legally applicable consent.
- Protection of places, practices, and knowledge that should remain private.
- Site-specific visitor limits, reservations, quotas, seasonal closures, and adaptive management.
- Climate resilience and reductions in both emissions intensity and total tourism emissions.
- Transparent financing, monitoring, enforcement, and public reporting.
- Economic diversification so tourism supplements rather than replaces other livelihoods.

The region’s specific laws, ecosystems, visitor markets, infrastructure, and fiscal conditions have not been provided. Demand, financing, emissions, and local-supply projections are therefore **planning assumptions** until validated through baseline studies.

---

# 1. Vision and Strategic Objectives

## Vision for 2035

By 2035, the region will be recognized as a responsible, welcoming, and climate-resilient destination where international visitors experience exceptional nature and heritage within clearly managed limits, while local communities retain meaningful decision-making power and receive a growing share of tourism benefits.

## Strategic objectives

1. **Protect natural and cultural assets** through enforceable limits, conservation investment, restoration, and effective management.
2. **Attract appropriate international visitors** who stay longer, spend locally, travel in approved seasons and locations, and respect local rules.
3. **Increase local economic participation** through ownership, procurement, employment, training, finance, and transparent benefit sharing.
4. **Improve visitor experience and accessibility** without overdeveloping sensitive places.
5. **Strengthen resilience** to climate change, disasters, market shocks, overtourism, and tourism dependence.
6. **Establish accountable governance** based on community participation, independent oversight, monitoring, and remedy.

# 2. Operating Principles and Non-Negotiable Safeguards

## 2.1 Operating principles

- **Limits before growth:** ecological, cultural, social, and infrastructure limits govern tourism expansion.
- **Regenerative stewardship:** tourism should maintain or improve the assets on which it depends.
- **Community rights and participation:** affected communities and rights-holders must have meaningful influence, with consent where legally applicable.
- **Local value retention:** priority will be given to local ownership, employment, procurement, and management.
- **Authenticity and dignity:** culture will not be misrepresented, exposed, or commercialized without authorization.
- **Climate responsibility:** emission reductions and adaptation are required; offsetting is not a substitute for direct reductions.
- **Precaution:** uncertainty will result in conservative access limits, not higher visitor numbers.
- **Adaptive management:** access, marketing, quotas, and products will change when monitoring shows unacceptable impacts.
- **Transparency:** major decisions, contracts, revenues, allocations, and results will be publicly reported where legally possible.

## 2.2 Safeguards that precede expansion

Before major promotion, new accommodation, transport expansion, increased site access, or long-term concessions:

### Environmental and infrastructure

- No development in legally protected no-build areas, critical habitats, sacred places, or locations where impacts cannot be effectively mitigated.
- Adequate water, wastewater, waste, emergency, safety, and maintenance capacity.
- Environmental, climate, heritage, water, social, and cumulative-impact assessments for major projects.
- Lawful water sources and wastewater treatment.
- Restoration bonds, decommissioning plans, and continuing developer liability for damage.
- Enforceable controls on extraction, discharge, noise, lighting, wildlife disturbance, invasive species, disease transmission, and land conversion.

### Cultural and rights

- Rights screening for projects affecting land, livelihoods, access, cultural practices, or recognized rights.
- Completion of the legally applicable consultation, agreement, or consent process.
- Authorization before using ceremonies, stories, names, images, sacred symbols, or traditional knowledge.
- Community approval of official cultural narratives and imagery.
- Protection for practices, places, objects, and knowledge designated as private.
- No unjustified loss of public or customary access.

### Social and economic

- Housing, land, labor, livelihood, public-access, and displacement assessments.
- No avoidable involuntary displacement from publicly supported projects.
- Enforceable local employment, procurement, training, ownership, or revenue-sharing requirements in concessions.
- Compliance with mandatory labor, safety, accessibility, cultural, environmental, consumer-protection, and reporting standards.
- Ring-fenced, audited, and publicly reported visitor fees and tourism levies.
- Tourism revenues supplement, rather than replace, ordinary public funding for conservation, heritage, sanitation, housing, and emergency services.

# 3. Priority International Visitor Strategy

## 3.1 Target visitor segments

The region should prioritize visitors whose interests and behavior are compatible with conservation and community benefit:

- **Nature and wildlife travelers:** guided, small-group experiences with strict wildlife-distance and no-feeding rules.
- **Cultural and heritage travelers:** professionally interpreted sites and community-approved living-culture experiences.
- **Active and soft-adventure travelers:** walking, cycling, kayaking, and similar activities subject to safety, seasonal, and environmental controls.
- **Slow, wellness, rural, and food travelers:** longer stays with spending distributed among rural businesses and communities.
- **Educational, research, and special-interest visitors:** universities, conservation groups, historians, photographers, and other approved specialists.
- **Diaspora and heritage travelers:** where relevant, visitors with cultural or family connections to the region.

## 3.2 Market selection

By the end of Year 2, the tourism agency should select two to four initial international markets using evidence on:

- Product and cultural fit.
- Expected length of stay and local spending.
- Season compatibility.
- Connectivity, visa, and border feasibility.
- Willingness to comply with reservations and visitor rules.
- Accessibility needs.
- Carbon implications.
- Enforcement and infrastructure requirements.

Demand forecasts must include low, central, and high scenarios. Debt, permanent staffing, accommodation capacity, and major transport investment must not be based on optimistic forecasts.

## 3.3 Destination positioning

A proposed positioning is:

> **A living landscape where remarkable nature, enduring heritage, and local communities welcome responsible explorers.**

Marketing should emphasize authenticity, meaningful experiences, local hosts, conservation, safety, accessibility, and longer stays. It must not:

- Portray residents as attractions.
- Promise unrestricted access to fragile sites.
- Market places as “untouched,” “secret,” or “undiscovered.”
- Use cultural imagery or narratives without approval.
- Promote sites whose capacity or infrastructure is already exceeded.

## 3.4 Market-readiness requirements

Before major campaigns, the tourism agency must verify:

- Accurate visa, entry, health, safety, insurance, and closure information.
- Reliable multilingual digital services.
- Transparent pricing and payment systems.
- Secure booking systems and offline alternatives.
- Verified accommodation, guide, and transport inventories.
- Emergency, medical, evacuation, and disruption information.
- International complaints access.
- Independently audited accessibility information.
- Accredited operators and guides.
- Transport and last-mile arrangements.

Performance standards should cover booking availability, inquiry response time, inventory accuracy, complaint resolution, accessibility-information accuracy, and visitor satisfaction by market.

# 4. Spatial Planning and Visitor Management

## 4.1 Destination zones

The planning authority, with community and council participation, should designate:

- **Strict protection zones:** no routine tourism access.
- **Controlled-access zones:** permits, quotas, guides, reservations, designated routes, or seasonal closures.
- **Community tourism zones:** locally governed activities within approved limits.
- **Service and development zones:** towns and transport hubs where accommodation and visitor infrastructure are concentrated.
- **Restoration zones:** temporarily closed or restricted locations.

Dispersal will not be assumed to be sustainable. A new destination must demonstrate ecological, water, wastewater, waste, housing, transport, safety, cultural, and community capacity before promotion.

## 4.2 Site Management Plans

Every priority natural and heritage site should have a funded Site Management Plan by the end of Year 2. Plans should define:

- Baseline and desired conditions.
- Ecological, physical, social, cultural, and visitor-experience indicators.
- Seasonal and climate-adjusted limits.
- Maximum visitor-nights, group sizes, vehicle movements, guide licenses, accommodation beds, water use, and waste loads where relevant.
- Routes, operating hours, reservations, and closure procedures.
- Monitoring methods and quality controls.
- Thresholds and mandatory responses.
- Staffing, equipment, maintenance, and funding.
- Community participation and review arrangements.

Ecological, cultural, and rights-based limits take precedence over economic targets.

## 4.3 Adaptive trigger rules

| Indicator | Trigger | Required response |
|---|---|---|
| Trail erosion exceeds the approved standard | Two consecutive monitoring periods | Repair, route rotation, smaller groups, or closure |
| Wildlife disturbance increases | Repeated violations or documented behavioral change | Smaller groups, guide requirement, or seasonal closure |
| Water abstraction exceeds allocation | Any legal exceedance | Reduce extraction, operations, or accommodation |
| Heritage condition deteriorates | Approved threshold exceeded | Barriers, timed entry, route change, or closure |
| Resident support falls below threshold | Two annual surveys | Community review and product redesign |
| Waste exceeds treatment capacity | Sustained exceedance | Reduce visitation and require corrective action |
| Serious safety incident occurs | Any major incident | Suspend activity and conduct independent review |
| Tourism emissions exceed the approved budget | Verified annual exceedance | Suspend expansion and revise marketing or transport measures |

Quota increases require published evidence, independent review, and formal approval by the legally authorized authority. Residents should receive free or discounted access, with a defined share of reservations protected for local use.

# 5. Environmental and Climate Stewardship

## 5.1 Biodiversity

The environment and protected-area authorities should:

- Map sensitive habitats, breeding areas, corridors, and seasonal restrictions.
- Regulate drones, off-road vehicles, boating, fishing, camping, lighting, noise, and wildlife photography.
- Prohibit wildlife feeding, handling, purchase, and trafficking.
- Apply invasive-species and wildlife-disease controls at airports, ports, farms, trails, and accommodation.
- Assess marine, freshwater, and fisheries impacts where relevant.
- Require restoration plans with baselines, success criteria, maintenance funding, and independent verification.
- Make operators and developers liable for damage and failed restoration.
- Monitor habitat condition and wildlife behavior, not only population numbers.

Restoration bonds should reflect the full cost of restoration, monitoring, maintenance, and decommissioning.

## 5.2 Water, wastewater, waste, and pollution

By Year 3 in pilot destinations:

- All licensed tourism facilities should have lawful wastewater treatment or an approved connection.
- Medium and large facilities should complete water-use audits.
- New facilities should meet water-efficiency and drought-contingency standards.
- Remote operators should have approved waste-removal arrangements.
- Refill and reusable systems should replace avoidable single-use plastics.
- Noise and light controls should protect residents and wildlife.
- Facilities exceeding water, wastewater, or waste capacity should reduce operations until compliance is restored.

## 5.3 Climate and emissions

The tourism agency and climate authority should adopt a greenhouse-gas accounting protocol covering, where relevant:

- International and domestic aviation.
- Travel to and within the region.
- Accommodation and food.
- Construction and land-use change.
- Waste.
- Staff travel.
- Cruise or transit visitors.
- Multi-destination journeys.

The region should establish:

- An emissions-intensity pathway per visitor-night.
- An absolute tourism-emissions ceiling or declining pathway.
- Independent verification.
- A carbon-budget test for flights, roads, accommodation, and visitor capacity.
- Corrective action when the budget is exceeded.

The preferred hierarchy is to:

1. Avoid unnecessary short stays and low-value travel.
2. Promote longer, multi-site itineraries.
3. Consider market distance and lower-emission connectivity.
4. Shift internal travel to public, shared, electric, walking, and cycling modes.
5. Require energy standards for new and substantially renovated facilities.
6. Report total and per-visitor emissions.
7. Use verified climate contributions only for residual emissions.

Higher spending or longer stays do not automatically make international visitors low-carbon.

## 5.4 Climate resilience

By Year 3, major projects should complete climate-risk screening addressing flooding, wildfire, heat, drought, storms, landslides, coastal hazards where relevant, evacuation, early warning, backup power, communications, water security, and continuity of essential services.

# 6. Heritage and Cultural Integrity

## 6.1 Tangible heritage

By Year 2, priority heritage sites should have funded conservation and visitor-management plans covering:

- Condition and structural assessments.
- Preventive maintenance.
- Visitor routes, barriers, timed entry, and no-contact rules.
- Heritage impact assessments for surrounding projects.
- Controls on filming, photography, excavation, object removal, and commercial use.
- Qualified conservators and locally trained craftspeople.
- Emergency procedures for fire, flooding, climate events, and other hazards.

Irreplaceable heritage must not be traded for tourism revenue or compensated through unrelated offsets.

## 6.2 Living and intangible heritage

A community-led cultural interpretation and intellectual-property process should:

- Approve official stories, images, names, and tour scripts.
- Identify restricted knowledge, places, symbols, and practices.
- Require authorization and fair payment for performances, demonstrations, filming, and digital reproduction.
- Provide royalties or recurring licensing payments for commercial reuse.
- Permit correction or removal of inaccurate or offensive representations.
- Prohibit staged sacred practices without authorization.
- Protect children and vulnerable people from exploitative photography or participation.
- Support language, craft, music, food, and knowledge transmission for community purposes, whether or not marketed to visitors.

Communities will not be required to make all traditions visible or accessible.

## 6.3 Visitor conduct

A multilingual visitor code should cover:

- Dress and behavior at sacred and residential sites.
- Photography and filming.
- Drones and wildlife interaction.
- Purchasing and bargaining.
- Antiquities and cultural objects.
- Waste and environmental conduct.
- Interaction with residents and children.

Operators should provide the code at booking and explain it in person where risks are high.

# 7. Community Rights, Housing, and Local Economic Benefit

## 7.1 Rights screening and consent

Before project approval, authorities should identify:

- Legally recognized rights-holders.
- Indigenous and customary custodians.
- Landowners, tenants, seasonal users, workers, and resource users.
- People affected by traffic, water use, waste, or access restrictions.
- Cultural practitioners and relevant community institutions.
- Groups at risk of exclusion, including women, youth, minorities, disabled people, and low-income households.

The assessment must determine whether consultation, negotiated agreement, or legally applicable free, prior, and informed consent is required. It should identify the legitimate decision-making institution, provide accessible local-language information, fund independent advice where needed, protect dissenting groups, and define remedies for coercion, misinformation, or incomplete disclosure.

A signed mitigation and benefit-sharing agreement must precede relevant permits. A generic community committee must not replace a legally recognized rights institution.

## 7.2 Housing, land, and public access

High-risk destinations should require:

- Registration and limits on short-term rentals.
- Housing-impact assessments for major projects.
- Accommodation-bed caps in residential areas where necessary.
- Public-access conditions in concessions.
- No-net-displacement requirements for publicly supported projects.
- Independent review of land acquisition.
- Fair compensation and remedy.
- Monitoring of rents, land prices, housing availability, evictions, agricultural land conversion, and access restrictions.

Where existing law is inadequate, the authority matrix should identify required reforms and interim safeguards.

## 7.3 Local Value Retention Standard

For publicly supported projects and concessions, subject to national law, the government should require:

- Beneficial-ownership disclosure and verification.
- Minimum local or community equity or revenue sharing.
- Anti-fronting and anti-concentration controls.
- Local management and advancement targets.
- Community right of first refusal or transfer controls where appropriate.
- Accessible finance for local enterprises.
- Internal governance and benefit-distribution audits.
- Reporting of wages, procurement, taxes, platform commissions, and profit repatriation.

A value-chain study should establish leakage and local-retention baselines and be repeated at least every three years. Procurement targets should be sector-specific and supported by supplier training, aggregation, quality assurance, finance, and fair payment terms.

## 7.4 Decent work

A Tourism Decent Work Standard should apply to direct and subcontracted workers, including cleaners, security workers, guides, drivers, seasonal workers, migrant workers, and event workers.

Licensed operators and contractors should provide:

- Written contracts.
- Timely payment.
- Legal wages and comparison with a living-wage benchmark.
- Regulated working hours and overtime.
- Safe working conditions, including climate and emergency risks.
- Anti-harassment and nondiscrimination protections.
- Anonymous complaints and anti-retaliation protection.
- Social protection where applicable.
- Training, promotion, and management pathways.
- Suitable accommodation where employer-provided housing is necessary.

Repeated breaches may result in fines, license suspension, or exclusion from public contracts.

# 8. Infrastructure, Transport, and Accessibility

## 8.1 Infrastructure priorities

Essential systems must precede large-scale promotion:

- Drinking water.
- Wastewater treatment.
- Waste collection and disposal.
- Emergency medical, fire, and rescue services.
- Reliable energy and communications.
- Toilets and visitor information.
- Universal signage and interpretation.
- Fire, flood, heat, and evacuation systems.

All projects should include full life-cycle capital, operating, maintenance, replacement, climate-resilience, and decommissioning costs. Modular and staged construction should be preferred.

## 8.2 Transport

The transport authority and local governments should prioritize:

- Public transport from airports, ports, borders, and cities.
- Shared or electric shuttles in sensitive areas.
- Integrated ticketing.
- Walking and cycling connections.
- Private-vehicle restrictions where necessary.
- Luggage transfer and last-mile services.
- Timetables useful to residents as well as visitors.

New airports, roads, routes, accommodation capacity, or major parking facilities require demand, carbon, ecological, water, financial, safety, and community assessments.

## 8.3 Accessibility

Accessibility audits should cover sites, transport, accommodation, digital systems, and information. Improvements may include accessible routes where feasible, adapted toilets, captions, audio description, tactile interpretation, easy-read materials, sign-language options, and trained staff.

Where physical alteration would damage a fragile site, equivalent access should be provided through virtual, audio, tactile, off-site, or alternative-site experiences. Marketing may claim accessibility only where independently verified.

# 9. Governance and Accountability

## 9.1 Regional Sustainable Tourism Council

A legally designated coordinating authority should establish the council within six months. Membership should include:

- Relevant public authorities and local governments.
- Protected-area and heritage managers.
- Indigenous, customary, and other legally recognized rights-holder institutions where relevant.
- Affected community organizations.
- Women, youth, disability, minority, worker, and small-business representatives.
- Tourism businesses.
- Researchers, conservation organizations, and civil society.

The council should:

- Approve annual destination-management and marketing plans.
- Review ecological, heritage, social, housing, economic, emissions, and visitor data.
- Coordinate investment and budgets.
- Review benefit-sharing agreements.
- Commission independent reviews.
- Recommend or initiate quotas, closures, redesign, or expansion through legally authorized bodies.
- Publish annual performance and financial reports.

Its legal status must be settled before implementation. If it lacks regulatory power, a binding interagency protocol must establish who acts, by when, and how noncompliance is escalated.

## 9.2 Local destination committees

Each pilot destination should establish a representative committee to help decide:

- Local visitor rules and cultural protocols.
- Community-benefit fund allocations.
- Local tourism products.
- Recommendations on quotas, closures, and operating hours.
- Monitoring of resident sentiment, housing, access, and local benefits.
- Complaints requiring escalation.

Participation costs should be reimbursed so that low-income residents, workers, caregivers, and small businesses can participate.

## 9.3 Legal and project approval system

Within Year 1, the tourism agency and legal authority should publish a matrix specifying, for each major control:

- Lead authority.
- Legal instrument.
- Enforcement body.
- Funding source.
- Appeal route.
- Implementation deadline.

Every major tourism project should pass these gates:

1. Strategic and zoning compatibility.
2. Rights-holder and custodianship screening.
3. Legally applicable consultation, agreement, or consent.
4. Environmental and cumulative-impact assessment.
5. Water, wastewater, waste, climate, biodiversity, and emergency feasibility.
6. Housing, land, labor, livelihood, and displacement assessment.
7. Local ownership, beneficial ownership, procurement, and benefit plan.
8. Financing, maintenance, decommissioning, and debt assessment.
9. Public disclosure and appeal.
10. Construction approval.
11. Operational inspection and license.

Failure at any gate requires refusal or redesign.

# 10. Financing and Financial Controls

## 10.1 Five-year financial plan

Within Year 1, the finance ministry and tourism agency should prepare a costed five-year plan distinguishing capital and recurrent costs for:

- Baseline studies and monitoring.
- Rangers, inspectors, conservators, and site managers.
- Wastewater and solid waste.
- Heritage conservation.
- Transport and accessibility.
- Community participation and enterprise finance.
- Workforce training.
- Reservations, data systems, and cybersecurity.
- Climate resilience.
- Ombudsperson services and independent audits.
- Targeted marketing.

The plan must include:

- Costs by pilot destination.
- Staffing, equipment, and training.
- Maintenance, replacement, and decommissioning costs.
- Low-, central-, and high-demand revenue scenarios.
- Funding gaps.
- Debt-affordability limits.
- Public-private partnership safeguards.
- Minimum-service funding if visitor revenues underperform.
- Disaster and closure reserves.
- The legal authority responsible for collecting and disbursing each revenue stream.

No potential funding source will be treated as committed until legally confirmed and contractually secured.

## 10.2 Tourism Conservation and Community Fund

By Year 2, government should establish a legally protected fund, subject to national law. It should receive a defined share of visitor fees, levies, fines, and concessions and:

- Allocate funding using published formulas based on visitor pressure, conservation need, and community impact.
- Maintain closure, disaster, and urgent-repair reserves.
- Publish quarterly receipts and annual audited expenditure.
- Include community representatives in allocation decisions.
- Supplement, not replace, ordinary public funding.

Annual reports should compare tourism-related revenues with the pre-tourism public funding baseline for conservation, sanitation, housing, heritage, and emergency services.

# 11. Implementation Roadmap

## First 100 days

- Establish the interagency implementation team and interim council.
- Publish authority, procurement, conflict-of-interest, disclosure, and data rules.
- Map natural, cultural, community, infrastructure, housing, and hazard assets.
- Identify no-go and high-risk areas.
- Select no more than two or three pilot destinations.
- Review development proposals in sensitive areas.
- Begin baseline studies.
- Establish interim wildlife, heritage, filming, visitor, and vehicle rules.
- Identify recurring funds for enforcement, waste, wastewater, maintenance, monitoring, and complaints.
- Address urgent safety, sanitation, and accessibility deficiencies.
- Establish the public dashboard and complaints channel.

No major international marketing expansion will occur during this period.

## Years 1–2: Foundation

- Complete baseline, rights, housing, leakage, labor, climate, emissions, and capacity studies.
- Publish the authority and implementation-dependency matrix.
- Approve zoning and Site Management Plans.
- Establish the consent protocol, cultural process, ombudsperson, and ring-fenced fund.
- Adopt required legislation, regulations, licensing conditions, and interagency agreements.
- Staff and train enforcement teams.
- Repair critical water, wastewater, waste, safety, and access systems.
- Establish enterprise finance, procurement support, and workforce programs.
- Launch the reservation and information platform.
- Set final targets and trigger values through public approval.

International marketing remains limited to compliant products and sites.

## Years 3–5: Pilots

- Launch two to four capacity-managed circuits.
- Introduce permits, timed entry, group limits, seasonal closures, or shuttles where required.
- Begin targeted international marketing in approved markets.
- Implement community funds, local-value standards, labor inspections, conservation fees, and cultural protocols.
- Improve public and low-emission transport.
- Complete priority ecological restoration and heritage conservation.
- Conduct an independent evaluation at the end of Year 4 or 5.

Each pilot receives a formal **stop, redesign, or scale** decision.

## Years 6–10: Conditional expansion

Expansion is permitted only for sites and products meeting all thresholds. Actions may include:

- Scaling successful community enterprises.
- Extending accessible and low-emission infrastructure.
- Improving connectivity within carbon and capacity limits.
- Restoring degraded ecosystems and heritage.
- Strengthening climate adaptation and emergency systems.
- Reassessing zoning and capacity at least every three years.
- Maintaining domestic, regional, and international market diversity.
- Supporting non-tourism sectors where tourism concentration becomes excessive.

Beyond Year 10, the region will operate an adaptive destination-management system rather than pursue permanent expansion.

# 12. Risk Management

The council secretariat should maintain a quarterly risk register recording probability, impact, early-warning indicators, owner, preventive controls, contingency action, funding, escalation authority, and review date.

| Risk | Principal safeguards |
|---|---|
| Overtourism and threshold exceedance | Quotas, reservations, closures, demand controls, automatic review |
| Water, wastewater, or waste failure | Pre-expansion infrastructure tests, operating restrictions, recurrent funding |
| Community conflict or consent failure | Rights screening, independent advice, legitimate consent processes, remedy |
| Housing pressure and displacement | Short-term-rental controls, housing assessments, bed caps, public-access protections |
| Regulatory capture or corruption | Beneficial-ownership disclosure, public tenders, audits, conflict rules |
| Weak enforcement | Funded staffing, license-to-inspection standards, meaningful sanctions |
| Climate disasters | Climate screening, early warning, evacuation, reserves, diversified products |
| Financing shortfall or debt stress | Low-demand scenarios, debt limits, staged investment, minimum-service budgets |
| Labor exploitation | Decent Work Standard, inspections, worker complaints, license sanctions |
| Cybersecurity or data failure | Privacy-by-design, access controls, audits, offline alternatives |
| International demand collapse | Domestic and regional markets, contingency reserves, business continuity |
| Tourism dependence | Diversification thresholds and continued support for other sectors |
| Greenwashing | Mandatory standards, independent certification, penalties for false claims |

# 13. Core Performance Indicators

Baselines will be established during Years 1–2. Every indicator must have a data owner, measurement method, collection cost, quality-control process, reporting frequency, target or trigger, and management response.

## Environmental

- Priority sites with funded management and capacity plans: **100% by Year 2**.
- Tourism-related protected-habitat loss: **zero net loss**.
- Habitat and indicator-species condition: **stable or improving from baseline**.
- Licensed tourism wastewater safely treated: **100% by the date set in regulation; date published by the end of Year 1**.
- Water use per visitor-night: **declining from baseline and within site allocations**.
- Tourism waste formally collected and treated: **at least 90% in pilot destinations by Year 5, subject to baseline confirmation**.
- Tourism emissions per visitor-night: **declining from baseline**.
- Total tourism emissions: **within an approved ceiling and declining pathway**.
- Unaddressed ecological threshold exceedances: **zero**.
- Restoration projects meeting approved success criteria: **target established by Year 2 and independently verified**.

## Heritage and culture

- Priority heritage sites with funded conservation plans: **100% by Year 2**.
- Heritage condition: **stable or improving**.
- Sites operating within physical and cultural limits: **within site-specific thresholds**.
- Commercial cultural products using approved protocols: **100%**.
- Contracted cultural payments and royalties paid on time: **100%**.
- Official campaigns using community-approved narratives and imagery: **100%**.
- Unauthorized filming, object removal, or cultural misuse: **all cases investigated within the published standard**.

## Community and social

- Major projects completing rights and displacement assessments: **100%**.
- Avoidable involuntary displacement from publicly supported tourism: **zero**.
- Resident support for tourism: **above the threshold established through the Year-1 survey and public approval**.
- Housing, rents, land prices, short-term rentals, evictions, and access: **reported annually, with corrective action at defined triggers**.
- Licensed workers with written contracts: **100% by Year 5**.
- Gender pay and advancement gaps: **declining from baseline**.
- Complaints resolved within the published standard: **at least 90%**.
- Participants in community decision-making receiving participation support: **100% of eligible participants**.

## Economic

- Visitor spending retained locally: **increasing from baseline**.
- Local procurement: **sector-specific targets established after the Year-1 supply-chain study**.
- Local and community-owned enterprises: **increasing, with revenue and survival tracked**.
- Community-fund receipts and payments: **published and independently audited**.
- Tourism wages versus living-wage benchmark: **reported annually, with gaps declining**.
- Average length of stay: **increasing from baseline**.
- Off-peak visitor nights: **increasing without exceeding seasonal limits**.
- Tourism share of employment, land use, household income, and public revenue: **within diversification thresholds established by Year 2**.

## Visitor experience and governance

- Visitor satisfaction and likelihood to recommend: **maintained or improved**.
- Congestion and wait times: **within site-specific limits**.
- Compliance with visitor codes: **increasing from baseline**.
- Serious safety incidents: **all independently reviewed**.
- Accessibility satisfaction: **improving from baseline**.
- Accurate capacity and accessibility information: **100% of promoted controlled sites**.
- Licensed operators submitting required data: **at least 90% by Year 3; 100% for high-risk operators**.
- Council and committee meetings with published minutes: **100%**.
- Tourism revenues, allocations, and contracts publicly reported: **annually**.
- Fund audits: **annually**.
- Strategy reviews: **every three years**.
- Core public funding for conservation, sanitation, housing, heritage, and emergency services: **not below the pre-tourism baseline unless publicly justified and approved**.

# 14. Binding Rules for Tourism Growth

International visitor growth may proceed in a destination only when:

1. Site limits are approved, monitored, and legally enforceable.
2. Water, wastewater, waste, safety, emergency, and transport systems meet requirements.
3. Enforcement staffing, equipment, and recurring budgets are secured.
4. Rights and community procedures are complete.
5. Benefit-sharing agreements are active.
6. Ecological, heritage, housing, resident, labor, and visitor indicators remain within thresholds.
7. Tourism emissions remain within the approved carbon budget.
8. Licensed operators meet environmental, labor, cultural, safety, accessibility, and reporting requirements.
9. The destination passes the market-readiness standard.
10. The council publishes the evidence supporting expansion.
11. The legally authorized authority formally approves the increase.
12. Expansion is compatible with the low-demand financial scenario and does not create unaffordable public liabilities.

If a threshold is exceeded, the default response is **pause, reduce, redesign, or close**. Visitor demand, investor pressure, optimistic forecasts, or future mitigation promises do not justify exceeding ecological, cultural, social, rights-based, infrastructure, labor, or emissions limits.

International arrivals are a supporting indicator. The region’s success will be measured by healthy ecosystems, protected heritage, resident well-being, fair work, local economic retention, reliable visitor experiences, diversified livelihoods, and accountable governance.
