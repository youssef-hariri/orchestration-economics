# Japan International Market-Entry Strategy

## Executive summary

Japan is a promising expansion market for an environmentally responsible European apparel company, but entry should begin as a **controlled, stage-gated commercial pilot**, not a nationwide rollout.

Japan offers affluent urban consumers, sophisticated e-commerce and logistics, strong expectations for product quality, and specialist textile, repair and manufacturing capabilities. However, competition is intense, customer-service expectations are high, and sustainability alone will not justify premium pricing.

### Recommended market-entry approach

- Start in the **Tokyo metropolitan area**, including Yokohama.
- Launch a Japanese-language **direct-to-consumer website**.
- Offer a focused assortment of **20–30 styles**.
- Prioritize one clearly defined gender and category scope.
- Lead communications with design, fit, comfort, construction and service.
- Support the proposition with precise, product-level sustainability evidence.
- Use existing proven suppliers during the pilot.
- Test cross-border fulfilment before moving core inventory to a Japanese 3PL.
- Use one Tokyo pop-up, a limited creator programme and two or three paid acquisition channels.
- Defer permanent retail, national wholesale, local manufacturing and a Japanese subsidiary until commercial evidence is established.

### Funding recommendation

Approve funding in tranches:

| Funding stage | Maximum amount | Purpose |
|---|---:|---|
| Tranche 1 | €150,000 | Discovery, market sizing, compliance and capability assessment |
| Tranche 2 | Additional €150,000 | Product, fit, price and paid-demand validation |
| Tranche 3 | Up to €600,000 total Phase 0 exposure | Controlled pilot after paid-demand and compliance gates |
| Optional 18-month programme | Approximately €1.2 million | Local fulfilment, selected wholesale, repair and scale-up |

The €1.2 million figure is an indicative ceiling, not an upfront commitment.

### Core success criterion

Scale only when Japan demonstrates:

> **Repeat, substantially full-price purchases across multiple products at acceptable acquisition, return, fulfilment and contribution costs, without dependence on one product, creator or channel.**

# 1. Target-market rationale

## Why Japan

Japan is a credible next market because it combines:

- A mature apparel sector with affluent urban customer groups.
- Strong expectations for quality, fit, presentation, reliability and service.
- Developed e-commerce, payment and domestic-delivery infrastructure.
- Potential receptiveness to differentiated international design.
- Established textile, repair, alteration and small-batch manufacturing capabilities.
- A consumer culture in which care, longevity and product provenance can support premium purchasing.

These characteristics indicate opportunity but do not guarantee demand for this company’s products. The company must validate market size, price acceptance, customer acquisition costs and repeat purchase before committing to scale.

## Geographic sequence

### Phase 1: Tokyo metropolitan area

Tokyo and nearby Yokohama should be the initial operating region because they provide:

- The greatest concentration of likely target customers.
- Access to design, retail, media and creator networks.
- Efficient pop-up and appointment-based testing.
- Proximity to logistics providers, advisers and potential partners.

### Phase 2: Osaka

Test Osaka only after Tokyo demonstrates:

- Acceptable conversion and contribution.
- Reliable service and returns.
- Demand across at least two product categories.
- Early repeat purchasing.
- Sufficient customer density outside Tokyo.

### Phase 3: Kyoto

Initially use Kyoto for:

- Textile and craft partnerships.
- Repair and care collaborations.
- Design-led events.
- Carefully selected concept retail.

Do not assume Kyoto will be a high-volume market without sales evidence.

## Regional expansion

Japan should not automatically be treated as a gateway to South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore or other Asian markets. Each market requires separate research covering language, climate, sizing, regulation, platforms, pricing and logistics.

# 2. Customer segments

## Primary segment: urban conscious professionals

**Profile**

- Approximately 25–45 years old.
- Middle- to upper-income.
- Primarily located in Tokyo and other large cities.
- Digitally active.
- Interested in quality, versatility, design and responsible consumption.
- Uses clothing for work, travel and leisure.

**Needs**

- Reliable fit.
- Comfortable fabrics in humid and variable conditions.
- Versatile garments.
- Good construction and care information.
- Precise environmental information.
- Convenient delivery and returns.
- Premium products that do not rely on overtly activist branding.

## Secondary segment: premium minimalist consumers

**Profile**

- Approximately 30–55 years old.
- Motivated mainly by fabric, construction, design and wardrobe value.
- Potentially more willing to pay for premium products.
- Reached through design media, curated e-commerce and concept stores.

**Positioning emphasis**

- Refined European design.
- Fabric and construction quality.
- Versatility.
- Limited availability.
- Long-term wardrobe value, where supported by evidence.

## Selective younger sustainability consumers

Reach through:

- Entry-price products.
- Accessories.
- Archive or second-life products.
- Repair and care events.
- Credible creator content.

This should remain a secondary segment until willingness to pay and repeat purchasing are demonstrated.

## Deferred B2B opportunities

Concept stores, boutique hotels, uniform programmes and institutional customers should be treated as separate future business cases. They involve different sales cycles, specifications, margins, payment terms and production requirements.

Before the second funding tranche, management must decide whether the initial launch covers:

- Womenswear.
- Menswear.
- Unisex products.
- One or two specific categories.

A narrow initial scope is preferable to a broad gender and category launch.

# 3. Product positioning

## Recommended positioning

> **European-designed everyday clothing for versatile use, combining understated design, practical comfort and specific, documented product attributes.**

The brand should occupy the space between:

- Mass-market basics.
- Premium Japanese and international brands.
- Luxury fashion.
- Technical outdoor apparel.
- Generic ethical basics.

The company should be:

- More design-led than generic ethical basics.
- More transparent than conventional premium apparel.
- More accessible than luxury.
- More refined and versatile than technical apparel.
- More internationally distinctive than undifferentiated basics.

European origin and environmental attributes should be tested as purchase drivers rather than assumed to be differentiators.

## Message hierarchy

Marketing should lead with:

1. Design and wearability.
2. Fit and comfort.
3. Construction and care.
4. Material and production information.
5. Repair and second-life options.
6. European design heritage.

Suggested wording:

> “Thoughtfully designed European clothing for everyday use, made with carefully selected materials and documented production practices.”

“Documented production practices” should be explained using specific evidence, such as production location, certification scope or defined facility assessment. It should not imply overall ethical or environmental superiority.

# 4. Product and assortment strategy

## Initial assortment

Launch with **20–30 styles**, concentrating approximately **60–70% of inventory in hero products**.

Potential categories include:

- Elevated T-shirts and basics.
- Shirts and overshirts.
- Lightweight knitwear.
- Relaxed trousers.
- Selected dresses, skirts or light outerwear.
- Accessories.

The final mix must be based on the company’s existing product data rather than generic category assumptions.

Prioritize products with:

- Strong European sell-through and reviews.
- Low historical return rates.
- Broad fit tolerance.
- Low seasonal risk.
- Replenishment potential.
- Climate suitability.
- Complete material and production documentation.
- Positive expected contribution after Japan costs.

Avoid substantial initial purchases of highly seasonal, heavily fitted or low-volume products.

## SKU approval scorecard

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Existing demand and customer reviews | 20% |
| Expected contribution margin | 20% |
| Fit and return performance | 15% |
| Supplier capacity and replenishment | 15% |
| Climate suitability and seasonality | 10% |
| Product differentiation | 10% |
| Claims and traceability evidence | 10% |

### Automatic exclusions

- Negative landed contribution.
- Incomplete Japanese legal or labelling information.
- Unsupported environmental, durability or repairability claims.
- Unacceptable supplier quality or capacity risk.
- Excessive seasonal or markdown exposure.

## Fit and product localization

Do not assume that all Japanese customers require shorter or narrower European patterns. Test changes by product and customer type.

Required actions:

- Conduct wear tests with 30–50 target customers.
- Publish measurements in centimetres.
- Provide Japanese and international size conversions.
- Use local models across varied heights and body types.
- Test shoulders, sleeves, rises and inseams.
- Analyse returns by product, size, body measurement and reason.
- Test breathable, lightweight and layering-friendly fabrics.
- Provide Japanese washing, drying and care instructions.
- Include spare parts only where they provide genuine repair value.

Terms such as “durable,” “long-lasting” and “made for repeated wear” should be used only when supported by relevant testing, documented wear studies, historical defect data or comparable evidence.

The company should also establish a Japan-specific after-sales policy covering defects, replacements, refunds, repairs, customer charges and escalation.

# 5. Sustainability and claims governance

## Claims principles

Use narrow, verifiable claims such as:

- “Made with 80% certified organic cotton.”
- “Contains 50% certified recycled fibre.”
- “Manufactured by a documented supplier in [country].”
- “Repair service available for specified products.”
- “Packaging contains [specified percentage] recycled fibre.”

Avoid unqualified claims such as:

- “Eco-friendly.”
- “Green.”
- “Planet-friendly.”
- “Sustainable.”
- “Zero impact.”
- “Carbon neutral.”
- “Climate positive.”
- “Lower impact,” without a defined comparison and evidence.

A certificate supports only the certified attribute. It does not establish that the entire garment is environmentally superior.

## Claims register

Every claim used on a website, label, package, advertisement, creator brief, press release or retail display must be recorded.

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Exact wording | Japanese and English versions |
| Scope | Product, material, facility or service |
| Evidence | Certificate, test, audit, LCA or supplier record |
| Comparator | Required for comparative claims |
| Validity | Issue and review dates |
| Owner | Named internal owner |
| Approval | Legal and sustainability sign-off |
| Qualification | Consumer-facing explanation |
| Monitoring | Publication monitoring and takedown process |

No claim should be published without approval. All creator, retailer and media content should be monitored, with correction and takedown procedures.

## Product traceability

For each launch SKU, maintain a controlled record linking:

- SKU and production batch.
- Fibre composition.
- Material certificates.
- Factory and country.
- Relevant process evidence.
- Label and care information.
- Approved claims.
- Japanese translation.
- Review date.
- Responsible approvers.

Separate the following attributes rather than combining them into a generalized sustainability claim:

- Traceability.
- Material certification.
- Labour assessment.
- Chemical and wastewater controls.
- Energy and water data.
- Product durability.
- End-of-life outcomes.

## Repair and circular services

Begin with a limited repair pilot. Define:

- Eligible products and repairs.
- Provider capacity.
- Customer charge or subsidy.
- Transport process.
- Turnaround time.
- Quality standard.
- Cost per repair.
- Customer satisfaction.

Do not launch take-back or resale until collection, sorting, hygiene, grading, reuse, recycling and disposal routes are defined and costed.

Report separately:

- Collected units.
- Repaired units.
- Reused units.
- Resold units.
- Recycled units.
- Disposed units.

Collection volume alone is not evidence of environmental benefit.

# 6. Marketing and sales channels

## Initial pilot scope

The minimum viable pilot should use:

- One Japanese-language DTC website.
- Two or three tested paid acquisition channels.
- One creator programme.
- One Tokyo pop-up or showroom.
- One narrowly defined repair experiment.

Marketplaces, wholesale, take-back, resale and additional physical locations require separate approval.

## Direct-to-consumer requirements

The Japanese website should provide:

- Japanese navigation and product pages.
- Yen pricing.
- Clear tax and duty treatment.
- Locally appropriate payment methods.
- Centimetre measurements.
- Japanese size conversion.
- Delivery estimates.
- Order tracking.
- Japanese returns information.
- Product reviews.
- Japanese privacy and commercial disclosures.
- Japanese-language customer support.

Product pages should lead with fit, fabric, construction and use before environmental claims.

## Paid acquisition

Initially test:

- Search and shopping advertising.
- Social advertising.
- Creator-led video.
- Relevant Japanese fashion, design and lifestyle platforms identified through research.

Each channel requires:

- Test budget.
- CAC ceiling.
- Conversion target.
- Contribution target.
- Minimum sample size.
- Stop-loss rule.

## Creator partnerships

Prioritize credible micro- and mid-sized creators in:

- Minimalist fashion.
- Capsule wardrobes.
- Sustainable living.
- Design and architecture.
- Travel and outdoor lifestyle.
- Garment care and repair.

Evaluate creators on audience relevance, attributable contribution, repeat purchase and content quality—not reach alone.

Contracts must require advertising disclosure, prohibit unsupported environmental claims and provide monitoring and takedown rights.

## Physical activation

The first Tokyo event should include:

- Try-on and fit consultation.
- Fabric and construction information.
- Care guidance.
- Repair information where applicable.

Measure:

- Footfall or appointments.
- Try-on rate.
- Try-on-to-purchase conversion.
- Average order value.
- Product and colour preferences.
- Price and fit objections.
- Follow-on online sales.
- Cost per acquired customer.
- Learning generated relative to event cost.

## Marketplaces and wholesale

If a marketplace is tested, define:

- Assortment.
- Commission ceiling.
- Price-parity rules.
- Inventory allocation.
- Customer-data rights.
- Return responsibility.
- Promotional restrictions.
- Brand-presentation standards.

After DTC evidence is established, test five to fifteen carefully selected concept stores. Add accounts only where full-price sell-through, reordering potential and brand presentation are satisfactory.

# 7. Pricing and unit economics

Indicative test prices are:

| Category | Japan test range |
|---|---:|
| T-shirt | ¥8,000–¥12,000 |
| Shirt or blouse | ¥16,000–¥28,000 |
| Trousers or skirt | ¥22,000–¥36,000 |
| Knitwear | ¥24,000–¥45,000 |
| Overshirt or light jacket | ¥30,000–¥55,000 |
| Outerwear | ¥45,000–¥85,000 |
| Accessories | ¥5,000–¥18,000 |

These are hypotheses, not forecasts. Validate them through local competitor research and actual purchases.

Prices should be set in yen and reviewed periodically for currency movement. Avoid permanent discounting. Define whether launch offers, bundles, creator codes and loyalty benefits count as discounted sales.

## SKU-level cost model

Calculate for every product:

- Product cost.
- Freight and insurance.
- Customs duty.
- Brokerage.
- Import and consumption tax.
- 3PL receiving, storage and pick-pack.
- Domestic delivery.
- Payment fees.
- Returns and exchanges.
- Customer service.
- Marketing allocation.
- Wholesale margin.
- Currency exposure.
- Inventory financing.
- Markdown and obsolescence risk.
- Chargebacks and refund losses.

## Margin definitions

1. **Gross margin:** Sales less product cost.
2. **Variable contribution before marketing:** Gross margin less freight, duties, fulfilment, payment fees and returns.
3. **Contribution after marketing:** Above less attributable acquisition costs.
4. **Market operating contribution:** Contribution less local staff, advisers, technology and fixed costs.

## Provisional scale thresholds

Refine after testing, but use the following for planning:

- Contribution after variable marketing: at least 30%.
- Return rate: below 20% or clearly improving.
- Fit-related returns: declining during the pilot.
- Full-price sales: at least 50%.
- Second purchase within 180 days: at least 15% in sufficiently mature early cohorts.
- Twelve-month repeat purchase: target at least 25% once cohorts mature.
- No single product, creator or channel above 50% of sales.
- No structural dependence on discounts.

CAC ceilings should initially be based on first-order contribution. Lifetime-value assumptions should not justify scaling until mature cohort evidence exists.

# 8. Manufacturing and supply-chain approach

## Pilot supply model

For the first 6–12 months:

- Retain proven existing suppliers.
- Ship consolidated inventory.
- Make small initial buys.
- Replenish only proven products.
- Avoid large seasonal commitments.
- Apply Japanese checks to labels, packaging and care information.

Obtain written supplier confirmations covering:

- Capacity.
- Minimum order quantities.
- Lead times.
- Replenishment capability.
- Material availability.
- Quality performance.
- Backup options.
- Japanese labelling and packaging capability.

Maintain a SKU capacity plan covering:

- Initial quantity.
- Reorder point.
- Safety stock.
- Maximum weeks of supply.
- Maximum inventory cash exposure.
- Maximum unsold units at pilot end.
- Delay and quality-failure responses.

## Japanese manufacturing opportunities

Explore Japanese production for:

- Premium knitwear.
- Denim and cotton.
- Small-batch prototypes.
- Dyeing and finishing.
- Repairs and alterations.
- Craft-led limited editions.

Do not assume “made in Japan” means lower environmental impact, higher quality or better economics. Evaluate:

- Unit cost.
- Minimum order quantity.
- Lead time.
- Capacity.
- Quality.
- Material origin.
- Energy and process information.
- Transport.
- Customer willingness to pay.

Initially restrict local production to products where it creates demonstrable customer value.

# 9. Logistics and fulfilment

## Cross-border pilot

Before commercial launch, select and document:

- Importer of record.
- Delivered-duty-paid or alternative model.
- HS classification by SKU.
- Rules of origin.
- Duties and taxes.
- Consumer-facing price treatment.
- Customs broker.
- Failed-delivery responsibility.
- Return-export process.
- Refund treatment.

Conduct real sample shipments and returns before launch. The company should not proceed if it cannot provide clear landed pricing and a workable return process.

## Japanese 3PL transition

Obtain proposals from at least three providers. Calculate the order volume at which Japanese stock becomes more economical than cross-border fulfilment, including:

- Storage.
- Receiving.
- Pick-pack.
- Domestic delivery.
- Returns.
- Inventory financing.
- Tax and cash timing.

Move core products to a Japanese 3PL only when the improvement in service and contribution justifies the fixed commitment.

## Initial service targets

Subject to provider validation:

- Dispatch stocked products within two business days.
- Deliver within the published period.
- Order accuracy above 99%.
- Customer-service response within one Japanese business day.
- Return authorization within two business days.
- Refund within the legally required and published period.
- Customs failure rate below 1%.
- Return processing within five business days.

These targets should be costed and included in provider agreements with reporting, escalation and remediation provisions.

Before launch, Japanese advisers should confirm:

- Fibre-content and care labelling.
- Country-of-origin presentation.
- Product safety and product liability.
- Consumer protection and returns.
- E-commerce commercial disclosures.
- Environmental advertising claims.
- Customs and import documentation.
- Consumption tax.
- Packaging and waste obligations.
- Personal-data handling and cross-border transfers.
- Trademark registration.
- Employment and contractor arrangements.
- Product recall procedures.
- Insurance requirements.

## Compliance responsibility matrix

| Obligation | Applicable law or authority | Product/process | Owner | Evidence | Deadline | Launch-blocking? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Labelling | To be confirmed locally | Each SKU | Legal/product | Approved label | Before shipment | Yes |
| Customs and import | To be confirmed locally | Each SKU/shipment | Importer/broker | Classification and customs file | Before shipment | Yes |

All launch-blocking requirements must be completed, documented and approved before commercial shipment. A scheduled resolution is not sufficient.

# 12. Phased implementation timeline

## Phase 0A: discovery and compliance — Months 0–1

Complete:

- Company capability assessment.
- Bottom-up Tokyo/category market model.
- Customer and competitor research design.
- Initial gender and category decision.
- Trademark work.
- Legal and customs scoping.
- Supplier and 3PL assessment.

### Gate A: release Tranche 2

Requirements:

- Quantified target segment and preliminary market model.
- No identified structural compliance barrier.
- Initial customer and category scope approved.
- At least 30 competitor records completed.
- Supplier and fulfilment options appear viable.
- Phase 0B learning objectives and budget approved.

## Phase 0B: product and demand validation — Months 1–3

Complete:

- Fit and wear testing.
- Japanese landing page.
- Price and message tests.
- Sample shipments and returns.
- SKU scorecard.
- Claims register.
- Preliminary paid-demand test.
- 24-month financial model.
- Supplier capacity plan.

### Gate B: authorize paid pilot

Requirements:

- All launch-blocking legal requirements completed or scheduled for completion before any shipment; no unresolved issue may remain at launch.
- Initial gender/category scope confirmed.
- At least 30 fit-test participants completed.
- Paid deposits, pre-orders or completed purchases provide a credible demand signal.
- At least two acquisition channels show potential.
- Selected SKUs have non-negative landed contribution before fixed costs.
- Import, delivery and return processes tested.
- All launch claims have approved evidence files.
- Pilot inventory and cash exposure remain within limits.

## Phase 1: controlled Tokyo launch — Months 4–6

- Launch Japanese DTC.
- Offer 20–30 styles through limited stock or pre-orders.
- Run one Tokyo pop-up.
- Activate selected creators.
- Test search, social and retargeting.
- Monitor conversion, fit, returns, service and cash weekly.
- Avoid permanent discounting.
- Do not establish a subsidiary or commit to permanent retail.

### Gate C: authorize operational scaling

Require, over a defined and adequate sample:

- At least 300 completed paid orders.
- A defined minimum number of unique customers.
- At least two product categories generating meaningful demand.
- At least two acquisition channels producing sales.
- Contribution after variable marketing of at least 30%.
- Return rate below 20% or demonstrably improving.
- At least 50% full-price sales.
- No product, creator or channel above 50% of sales.
- Service-level targets met.
- Second purchase emerging in a sufficiently mature cohort.
- Inventory exposure within approved limits.

The 300-order threshold supports operational learning but is not, by itself, proof of long-term retention.

## Phase 2: operational localization — Months 7–12

If Gate C is passed:

- Move core stock to a Japanese 3PL if economically justified.
- Establish local returns.
- Replenish proven products.
- Test Osaka selectively.
- Pilot repairs.
- Add a small number of concept stores.
- Improve fit, content and customer service.

## Phase 3: establishment — Months 13–18

Only if performance remains positive:

- Compare entity options.
- Consider local incorporation.
- Hold Japanese stock of proven bestsellers.
- Add five to fifteen strategic retail partners.
- Launch one carefully scoped Japanese collaboration.
- Evaluate selected Japanese manufacturing.
- Prepare separate cases for other Asian markets.

The launch date is conditional on completion of all critical-path work, including trademarks, compliance, payments, fit testing, supplier confirmation, fulfilment setup and sample returns.

- Absorb local staff and fixed costs.

# 15. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation | Contingency |
|---|---|---|
| Interest does not convert into purchases | Paid deposits, pre-orders and controlled sales tests | Stop acquisition; revise product, price or message |
| Price is unacceptable after landed costs | Local price testing and SKU economics | Introduce entry products or discontinue weak categories |
| Poor fit | Local wear tests and return analytics | Pause affected SKUs and revise patterns |
| Claims are challenged | Claims register and legal approval | Withdraw, correct or narrow claims immediately |
| Import model is unworkable | Written customs/tax advice and test shipments | Change importer or fulfilment structure |
| Cross-border fulfilment is too slow or costly | End-to-end delivery and return tests | Move core stock to Japanese 3PL |
| Duties are misclassified | SKU-level broker review | Reprice, revise origin model or discontinue |
| Inventory is overbought | Small buys, pre-orders and stock ceilings | Controlled liquidation without permanent discounting |
| FX reduces margin | Yen pricing, sensitivity analysis and hedge policy | Adjust future prices and purchase commitments |
| Competition is stronger than expected | Benchmark product, fit and service | Narrow to the strongest segment or category |
| Channel discounts dilute the brand | Price and channel rules | Reduce or suspend the channel |
| Partner failure | SLAs, reviews and replacement options | Replace provider or bring function in-house |
| Data breach or privacy failure | Japanese privacy review, security controls and incident plan | Suspend affected systems and activate response |
| Product defect or recall | Testing, insurance and recall procedure | Stop sales and execute recall |
| Supplier disruption | Capacity checks, dual sourcing and safety stock | Shift production or reduce assortment |
| Repair or take-back overstates impact | Defined downstream outcomes | Report only verified results or suspend programme |
| Trademark conflict | Search and registration before launch | Rename product or delay launch |
| Payment fraud or chargebacks | Screening and monitoring | Restrict payment methods or suspend affected orders |
| Natural disaster or logistics disruption | Multiple providers and contingency planning | Re-route fulfilment or pause affected regions |
| Management distraction | Dedicated owner and formal gates | Freeze expansion until core operations stabilize |

The formal risk register should also record probability, financial impact, owner, early-warning indicator, review frequency, response budget and residual risk.

Every KPI should have a formula, owner, data source, reporting frequency, target, minimum sample size and decision consequence.

## Commercial

- Revenue by channel, city and product.
- Gross margin.
- Variable contribution before marketing.
- Contribution after marketing.
- Average order value.
- Units per order.
- Sell-through by SKU, colour and size.
- Full-price sales percentage.
- Inventory turnover.
- Wholesale reorder rate.

## Customer

- Website conversion.

## Final recommendation

Japan merits a disciplined pilot, not an unconditional expansion commitment. The company’s most credible route to success is:

- A narrow, high-confidence assortment.
- Local fit and sizing validation.
- Japanese-language content and service.
- Clear landed pricing and returns.
- Product-led marketing.
- Specific, evidence-backed sustainability claims.
- Proven existing manufacturing during the pilot.
- Selective physical discovery.
- Staged fulfilment, wholesale and production localization.
- Strict inventory, cash and stop-loss controls.

Scale only when Japanese customers demonstrate **repeat, substantially full-price purchases across multiple products at acceptable acquisition, return, fulfilment and contribution costs**.
