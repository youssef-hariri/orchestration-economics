# International Innovation and Experimentation Strategy

## Executive Direction

Build **one global innovation system with local flexibility**. Differences in proactive innovation should not be attributed automatically to national culture; they may reflect workload, leadership behavior, decision rights, language, prior initiatives, technology, regulation, market conditions, or trust.

Standardize the company’s **purpose, ethical boundaries, risk controls, learning discipline, and transparency**. Allow local offices to adapt participation methods, priorities, funding, and implementation based on evidence.

Success is not the number of ideas submitted. It is whether more employees can contribute safely, experiments generate reliable learning, useful solutions transfer across markets, and innovation occurs without avoidable harm or loss of control.

---

## 1. Global Principles

These principles should apply in every country:

1. **Solve meaningful problems**  
   Focus innovation on customer needs, operational challenges, emerging risks, and strategic opportunities.

2. **Make challenge safe**  
   Employees should be able to question assumptions, report concerns, and disagree respectfully without retaliation.

3. **Test before scaling**  
   Use small, evidence-based experiments before committing significant capital or organizational change.

4. **Take bounded risks**  
   Encourage experimentation within clear legal, ethical, safety, privacy, cybersecurity, employment, financial, and reputational boundaries.

5. **Separate results from conduct**  
   A disciplined experiment that produces an unfavorable result is different from negligence, misconduct, or bypassing controls.

6. **Include diverse contributors**  
   Ensure fair access for frontline, operational, technical, customer-facing, junior, remote, disabled, contract, and locally based employees, subject to applicable law and employment arrangements.

7. **Adapt to local evidence**  
   Offices may modify or reject solutions when local customer, employee, market, infrastructure, or regulatory conditions require it.

8. **Close the feedback loop**  
   Employees should receive timely explanations of how their suggestions were handled.

9. **Protect capacity**  
   Innovation must be planned, paid work—not an expectation to work additional unpaid hours.

10. **Reward learning, not activity**  
    Idea volume, workshops, and participation counts are not sufficient measures of innovation.

---

## 2. Global Standards and Local Flexibility

Use a **comply, adapt, or seek exemption** model.

### Global requirements

These should be mandatory:

- Legal, ethical, safety, privacy, cybersecurity, employment, and financial controls.
- Nonretaliation and conscientious-refusal protections.
- Minimum experiment documentation and risk assessment.
- Transparent decision rights and escalation routes.
- Definitions of good-faith failure, poor execution, negligence, and misconduct.
- Central recording of material experiments.
- Learning reviews and incident reporting.
- Independent assurance and portfolio-level risk oversight.

Stricter local law or mandatory safety requirements always prevail.

### Locally adaptable practices

Offices should be able to tailor:

- Language and communication channels.
- Anonymous, written, verbal, or asynchronous participation.
- Meeting and consultation formats.
- Local innovation priorities.
- Working-time and scheduling arrangements.
- Funding thresholds within global limits.
- Customer research methods.
- Recognition practices.
- Local review-panel membership.
- Manager coaching and facilitation methods.

Each adaptation should document its purpose, evidence, risk implications, accountable owner, and review date.

---

## 3. Diagnose Barriers Before Setting Targets

During the first 60–90 days, conduct a global diagnostic with local input:

- Survey psychological safety, workload, trust, manager behavior, and access to decision-makers.
- Interview frontline, shift-based, remote, junior, contract, non-native-language, and operational employees.
- Review existing innovation programs, unused ideas, previous failures, duplicated initiatives, and approval bottlenecks.
- Assess local technology, staffing, procurement, labor, privacy, safety, accessibility, and regulatory constraints.
- Gather customer and stakeholder insight on local problems and unmet needs.
- Establish baselines for participation, approval speed, experiment quality, learning, business impact, and control incidents.

Do not label an office “uninnovative” until structural barriers have been examined.

---

## 4. Create a Low-Friction Experimentation System

### Fast path: low-risk experiments

Examples include:

- Internal workflow changes.
- Process simulations.
- Prototypes using non-sensitive data.
- Small internal tests with no customer, safety, employment, production-system, or material financial impact.

Minimum requirements:

- One-page hypothesis and test plan.
- Named owner.
- Defined budget and duration.
- Success and stop criteria.
- Confirmation that restricted data and systems are not involved.
- Results recorded within 10 working days.

The local innovation owner should acknowledge submissions within five working days.

### Controlled path: material-risk experiments

Examples include:

- Customer-facing pilots.
- Personal or sensitive data.
- Pricing, contracts, financial reporting, or critical suppliers.
- Regulated products or services.
- Safety, cybersecurity, employment, privacy, or material reputational exposure.
- Production-system changes.

These require proportionate review from relevant legal, privacy, security, finance, safety, compliance, ethics, or employee-relations specialists.

Define:

- Maximum customer, employee, system, and financial exposure.
- Data-use, consent, notice, and retention requirements.
- Monitoring and incident procedures.
- Independent stop authority.
- Required decision timeframe, normally no more than 15 working days after a complete submission unless complexity or law requires more.

### Risk governance

Maintain a central experiment register containing the owner, location, purpose, risk category, approvals, exposure, incidents, results, and final decision.

The Chief Risk Officer or delegated independent function should be able to pause an experiment where there is credible material risk, concealment, control failure, or excessive cumulative exposure. Employees and local managers should also be able to trigger a safety or compliance review.

---

## 5. Fund Experiments in Stages

Provide progressively larger resources:

1. **Discovery grant** — interviews, observation, research, or data analysis.
2. **Prototype grant** — mock-ups, simulations, minimum viable products, or internal trials.
3. **Pilot grant** — controlled tests with defined users, sites, or processes.
4. **Scale funding** — broader deployment only after evidence supports it.

Each proposal should identify:

- The problem and affected users.
- The hypothesis and smallest useful test.
- Expected value.
- Risks and controls.
- Required data and expertise.
- Success, stop, and pivot criteria.
- Decision date and accountable owner.

Funding must include access to relevant design, data, technology, customer, operations, procurement, finance, legal, security, and compliance expertise.

### Protect local priorities

To prevent headquarters from controlling the agenda:

- Reserve a defined share of discretionary funding—for example, 30%—for locally originated priorities, calibrated to business context.
- Allow local offices to sponsor low- and moderate-risk experiments within delegated limits.
- Give regional and local representatives voting rights on relevant funding decisions.
- Publish aggregate funding allocations and major deferrals.
- Provide an appeal route for offices that believe a global decision is unsuitable.

---

## 6. Make Participation Broad and Inclusive

Provide multiple contribution routes:

- Local-language submissions.
- Anonymous channels.
- Written, verbal, collective, and asynchronous proposals.
- Small-group and one-to-one discussions.
- Facilitated frontline problem collection.
- Low-bandwidth or offline options.
- Accessible formats and reasonable accommodations.
- Paid, scheduled participation for shift-based and frontline employees.
- Trusted employee representatives or ombuds routes.
- Translation of decision-critical material.

Evaluate ideas on evidence, relevance, feasibility, learning potential, and risk—not presentation fluency, confidence, seniority, or proximity to headquarters.

Participation should not be mandatory outside allocated work time. Employees who decline an experiment because of a legitimate ethical, safety, legal, or professional concern must not be penalized.

Consult unions, works councils, and employee representatives where required. Protect and appropriately govern any demographic data used to assess inclusion.

---

## 7. Strengthen Leadership and Manager Behavior

### Senior executives should

- Identify a limited set of strategic problems requiring new approaches.
- Fund experimentation and make capacity trade-offs explicit.
- Ask, “What evidence would change our view?” and “What is the smallest safe test?”
- Publicly report experiments stopped and lessons learned, not only successes.
- Apply the same failure standards to senior leaders as to employees.
- Remove unnecessary approval barriers.
- Hold managers accountable for the conditions they create.

### Local managers should

- Protect paid time for innovation.
- Invite input from less visible employees.
- Use structured turn-taking, anonymous input, and written review.
- Explain why ideas are accepted, declined, combined, or deferred.
- Respond constructively to bad news.
- Separate experiment outcomes from individual performance judgments.
- Recognize risk identification and responsible stopping.
- Escalate capacity, technology, and authority barriers.

Manager evaluations should include timely idea response, protected capacity, psychological-safety results, inclusive participation, coaching quality, and evidence of nonretaliation.

---

## 8. Establish Psychological Safety with Accountability

Required mechanisms include:

- Confidential reporting outside the local management chain.
- Anonymous reporting where feasible.
- Skip-level listening sessions.
- Regular psychological-safety surveys.
- Manager training and coaching.
- Structured and asynchronous alternatives to public disagreement.
- Formal protection for conscientious refusal of unlawful or unsafe work.

An independent Ethics, Ombuds, Internal Audit, or equivalent function should investigate retaliation complaints. Establish:

- Acknowledgement within five working days.
- Initial risk assessment within 10 working days.
- Interim protection where needed.
- Resolution or a progress update within a defined period, such as 30 working days.
- Monitoring of complainants and witnesses.
- Escalation to regional, global, executive, or board-level channels.
- Aggregate reporting on complaints, corrective actions, and repeat locations.

Psychological safety does not remove accountability or authorize unsafe behavior.

---

## 9. Reward Responsible Creativity

Recognize:

- Ideas adopted into operations.
- Experiments that disprove assumptions.
- Early identification of significant risks.
- Valuable customer or employee insight.
- Cross-office collaboration.
- Reusable tools and methods.
- Timely stopping or pivoting.
- Mentoring and support for other contributors.

Use team recognition, development opportunities, transparent career credit, and leadership visibility where appropriate. Financial awards must comply with local law and employment requirements.

To reduce bias:

- Record individual and team contributions using clear criteria.
- Include local, frontline, and functional representatives on recognition panels.
- Do not base recognition solely on executive visibility, presentation skills, or English fluency.
- Review recognition patterns for geographic, language, gender, seniority, and functional bias where lawful.
- Do not penalize employees who do not participate when innovation is not part of their allocated role.

### Distinguish failure types

- **Good-faith failure:** disciplined test, honest reporting, and controls followed.
- **Execution or learning failure:** weak design, monitoring, or response requiring coaching and improvement.
- **Negligence or misconduct:** concealment, control bypass, unlawful action, or reckless exposure to harm.

Only the latter categories should normally result in adverse consequences, following fair investigation.

---

## 10. Share Learning Across Offices

Organize collaboration around shared customer or operational problems rather than geography alone.

Use:

- A searchable, multilingual experiment repository.
- Cross-border communities of practice.
- Rotating regional showcases.
- Asynchronous video, written summaries, and discussion boards.
- Office pairings around similar problems.
- Cross-border discovery grants.
- Virtual assignments and short employee exchanges.
- Reusable experiment, risk, and learning templates.

Before transferring a solution, the receiving office should assess:

- What problem the original solution solved.
- Which assumptions were local.
- Differences in customers, regulation, labor, infrastructure, suppliers, and technology.
- What must be redesigned.
- What local evidence is required.
- Who can adapt or reject the solution.

Receiving offices should have the right to adapt or decline solutions that lack local support.

---

## 11. Governance and Ownership

| Owner | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| Board and executive team | Set risk appetite, fund the system, model learning behavior, and oversee material culture or risk concerns |
| Accountable enterprise executive | Own overall outcomes, capacity trade-offs, and systemic barrier removal |
| Global Innovation Council | Set standards, priorities, funding rules, cross-border principles, and portfolio oversight |
| Innovation Operations leader | Run the platform, service levels, experiment register, data, training, and reporting |
| Regional leaders | Adapt the framework, allocate resources, and resolve cross-border issues |
| Local managers and sponsors | Protect capacity, enable participation, and ensure local relevance |
| Experiment owners | Manage hypotheses, evidence, controls, documentation, and decisions |
| Control functions | Provide early, proportionate advice and independent review |
| HR and employee representatives | Address workload, labor requirements, inclusion, incentives, and retaliation |
| Internal Audit, Ethics, or Ombuds | Independently assess controls, retaliation cases, and program integrity |

The board or relevant committee should receive quarterly reporting on portfolio risk, material incidents, psychological safety, and retaliation trends. Conduct independent assurance at least annually.

## 12. Rollout Sequence

### First 90 days: Diagnose and design

1. Conduct the global and local diagnostic.
2. Review existing initiatives and close the loop on neglected ideas.
3. Publish the global standard and risk taxonomy.
4. Define decision rights, service levels, reporting routes, and refusal protections.
5. Identify protected capacity and pilot budgets.
6. Appoint enterprise, regional, local, and measurement owners.
7. Select diverse pilot units.
8. Train managers and control-function advisers.

### Months 3–6: Pilot

1. Launch multilingual, anonymous, and asynchronous idea intake.
2. Fund a limited portfolio of low- and moderate-risk experiments.
3. Operate the central experiment register.
4. Run proportionate learning reviews.
5. Monitor workload, inclusion, psychological safety, approval speed, and risk.
6. Begin cross-office communities of practice.

### Months 6–9: Evaluate and improve

1. Compare results with the baseline.
2. Remove unnecessary approvals and documentation.
3. Address resource, language, accessibility, technology, and manager-capability gaps.
4. Review recognition for headquarters or visibility bias.
5. Publish successful and stopped experiments.
6. Redesign or discontinue activities that produce volume without learning.

### Months 9–18: Scale and institutionalize

1. Scale practices that demonstrate value and responsible behavior.
2. Integrate innovation capability into planning, leadership development, and performance reviews.
3. Expand local funding and cross-border networks.
4. Conduct independent assurance.
5. Refresh strategic themes and retire low-value programs.

## 13. Compact Success Scorecard

### Access and inclusion

- Employee access to allocated innovation time and channels.
- Participation by role, level, location, and other lawful categories.
- Frontline and operational participation.
- Access to funding, expertise, translation, and accommodations.

### Safety and leadership

- Employees’ ability to raise concerns safely.
- Perception that leaders respond constructively to bad news.
- Retaliation concerns, cases, and resolution times.
- Manager performance on enabling experimentation.

### Process and learning

- Time from submission to acknowledgement.
- Time from complete submission to decision.
- Time from approval to first test.
- Experiments with clear hypotheses, controls, and stop criteria.
- Experiments stopped or changed based on evidence.
- Documented and reused learning.

### Business and customer value

- Customer adoption, satisfaction, retention, or problem-resolution improvements.
- Revenue, cost, quality, productivity, or cycle-time benefits.
- Time from validated experiment to adoption.
- Benefits relative to experimentation cost.
- Value of insights from stopped experiments.

### Risk and responsibility

- Control breaches and near misses by severity.
- Customer, employee, privacy, safety, cybersecurity, and reputational incidents.
- Quality and timeliness of risk reviews.
- Evidence that good-faith failure is treated differently from misconduct.

Do not rank or shame countries or offices. Compare similar business units and interpret metrics in context.

---

## Final Recommendation

Adopt a model of **global consistency in purpose, safeguards, learning standards, transparency, and employee protection—combined with local flexibility in language, participation, capacity, funding, priorities, and implementation**.

The most important leadership actions are to:

1. Diagnose structural barriers rather than blame locations.
2. Protect paid capacity for innovation.
3. Establish clear, risk-based decision rights.
4. Make speaking up and responsible failure genuinely safe.
5. Give local offices funding and authority to pursue relevant problems.
6. Reward disciplined learning, collaboration, and responsible stopping.
7. Share knowledge without forcing headquarters solutions.
8. Scale only what improves participation, learning, value, and control performance.
