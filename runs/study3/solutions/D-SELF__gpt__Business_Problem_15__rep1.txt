# Global Future-of-Work Framework

## 1. Strategic ambition

The company should manage the future of work as an operating-model transformation—not simply as a remote-work policy, office program, or technology initiative.

The framework should enable the company to:

- Deliver strong customer, operational, financial, and risk outcomes.
- Provide meaningful flexibility where work can be performed effectively.
- Preserve collaboration, learning, innovation, culture, and belonging.
- Ensure equitable access to opportunity regardless of location.
- Use offices, technology, and management practices intentionally.
- Respect local law, collective agreements, employee representation, culture, infrastructure, and employee needs.
- Remain resilient during infrastructure failures, crises, and business change.

### Core principle

> **Standardize outcomes, principles, minimum protections, decision criteria, and measurement; localize work patterns, implementation, and support.**

There should be no single global answer of “remote,” “hybrid,” or “office-based.” The appropriate model depends on the work, role, team, customer, country, infrastructure, risk profile, and legal context.

---

# 2. Global design principles

## 2.1 Design around the work

Work arrangements should reflect the work being performed—not ideology, personal preference, real-estate priorities, or assumptions about commitment.

Each role or team should be assessed against:

- Customer and operational requirements.
- Need for physical equipment, facilities, or secure environments.
- Customer, patient, public, or colleague interaction.
- Degree of interdependence with colleagues.
- Need for coaching, learning, innovation, or rapid decision-making.
- Time-zone and coverage requirements.
- Security, privacy, health, safety, and accessibility needs.
- Technology, workspace, power, and connectivity availability.
- Business-continuity requirements.
- Employment, tax, immigration, and employee-relations obligations.

The governing question is:

> **What combination of location, time, technology, and human interaction best enables this work?**

## 2.2 Fairness means equitable opportunity, not identical treatment

Different roles may require different work arrangements. A laboratory technician, field engineer, contact-center employee, software developer, and sales executive cannot necessarily have the same location flexibility.

All employees should nevertheless have equitable access to:

- Career development and promotion.
- Information and decision-making.
- Leadership exposure.
- Recognition and rewards.
- Appropriate equipment and workspace.
- Safe, accessible, and sustainable working conditions.
- Flexibility in the dimensions their role permits.

For roles that cannot be performed remotely, consider predictable schedules, shift swaps, compressed schedules, flexible start and finish times, part-time work, job sharing, advance roster notice, and occasional remote administrative work where feasible.

## 2.3 Flexibility with accountability

Employees should understand:

- The outcomes they own.
- How quality and performance are assessed.
- Expected working hours or availability.
- Which activities require physical presence.
- Team collaboration windows.
- Decision-making and escalation practices.
- How feedback, development, and recognition will occur.

Managers should assess outcomes, quality, judgment, collaboration, and capability—not physical presence, online status, webcam activity, or message volume.

## 2.4 Global coherence with local adaptation

### Global standards must establish

- Core principles and minimum employee protections.
- Objective work-model decision criteria.
- Accessibility, security, privacy, and safety requirements.
- Leadership and management expectations.
- Workforce-data and artificial-intelligence safeguards.
- Measurement standards.
- Governance, appeals, and escalation routes.

### Local entities may adapt

- Eligible work locations and attendance patterns.
- Working hours and collaboration windows.
- Benefits, allowances, and equipment support.
- Office design and investment.
- Local-language communications.
- Consultation and implementation processes.
- Health-and-safety controls.
- Cross-border work limits.

Every material deviation must document its rationale, affected employees, legal or operational basis, equity and cost impacts, mitigation, accountable owner, and review or sunset date.

## 2.5 Inclusion and accessibility by design

Every work model should be assessed for its impact on:

- Disabled and neurodivergent employees.
- Caregivers and employees with health conditions.
- Women and underrepresented groups.
- Early-career employees and new joiners.
- Frontline and non-desk employees.
- Employees working across languages and cultures.
- Employees with limited home space, connectivity, or transportation.
- Employees who prefer or require office-based work.
- Employees with temporary impairments, domestic-safety concerns, or changing family circumstances.

Accessibility must be built into offices, tools, meetings, communications, travel, and processes. Individual accommodations remain essential but should not be the only route to an accessible experience.

## 2.6 Security, privacy, and resilience by design

Remote, hybrid, and office work create different risks. Use a proportionate, risk-based approach rather than assuming one location is inherently safer.

Minimum expectations should cover:

- Secure identity and device management.
- Role-based access and encryption.
- Confidential-work requirements.
- Secure printing and document disposal.
- Incident reporting and response.
- Physical workplace and home-workplace safety.
- Business continuity during power, network, office, or geopolitical disruption.
- Appropriate privacy controls for workforce data.

# 3. Work-model architecture

Work should be designed across three dimensions: **location, time, and interaction**.

## 3.1 Location models

### Primarily site-based

Appropriate where work requires:

- Physical equipment or specialized facilities.
- Manufacturing, laboratory, retail, or operational environments.
- Direct customer, patient, or public interaction.
- High-security or safety-critical settings.
- Frequent physical coordination.

Flexibility may still include predictable scheduling, shift swaps, compressed hours, flexible start times, and occasional remote administrative work.

### Hybrid

Appropriate where work can be performed remotely but benefits from planned in-person interaction.

Teams must define:

- The purpose of office attendance.
- Activities best performed together.
- Expected collaboration patterns, if any.
- How capacity and booking will be managed.
- How remote participants will participate equitably.
- How exceptions and accommodations will work.
- How the arrangement will be reviewed.

Attendance should not be required merely to demonstrate commitment or reproduce individual work that can be done elsewhere.

### Primarily remote

Appropriate where work can be performed effectively away from a company site and local legal, security, tax, infrastructure, and health-and-safety conditions permit.

Employees should receive:

- Suitable equipment and secure access.
- Connectivity and workspace support appropriate to the role.
- Clear availability expectations.
- Ergonomic and accessibility support.
- Opportunities for connection, learning, and periodic in-person interaction.
- A controlled process for changing work country or location.

### Distributed by design

Teams working across multiple countries and time zones require deliberate practices for:

- Asynchronous work.
- Searchable documentation.
- Decision ownership.
- Handoffs.
- Reasonable overlap hours.
- Rotating meeting times.
- Language inclusion.
- Virtual onboarding and relationship building.

Highly distributed teams should also be assessed for handoff complexity, manager capacity, decision speed, customer coverage, reporting-line design, and resilience.

## 3.2 Time models

Options may include:

- Fixed schedules.
- Flexible start and finish times.
- Core collaboration hours.
- Shift-based work.
- Compressed schedules.
- Part-time work or job sharing.
- Asynchronous work.
- On-call or customer-coverage models.

No region should permanently carry inconvenient early-morning, evening, weekend, or holiday work solely because headquarters operates in another time zone. Recurring meeting times should rotate where practical, and asynchronous participation should be accepted as equivalent where the work permits.

## 3.3 Role profiles and eligibility

Every role or team should have a documented work-model profile stating:

- Default work model.
- Business rationale.
- Permitted flexibility.
- Required presence, equipment, or location.
- Security and confidentiality requirements.
- Time-zone or coverage requirements.
- Accessibility considerations.
- Business-continuity requirements.
- Review date.
- Exception, accommodation, and appeal routes.

Eligibility criteria must be objective, role-related, consistently applied, and proportionate to risk. Manager preference alone should not determine access to flexibility.

# 4. Work-model decision process

## 4.1 Decision hierarchy

Assess proposed arrangements in this order:

1. Legal, collective-agreement, safety, privacy, and security requirements.
2. Essential customer and operational continuity.
3. Role and task characteristics.
4. Team coordination, learning, and time-zone requirements.
5. Accessibility and inclusion impacts.
6. Employee preference and circumstances.
7. Cost, facilities, and environmental considerations.

Restrictions must be necessary, proportionate, documented, and periodically reviewed.

## 4.2 Required decision record

Each material decision should state:

- The problem being solved.
- Options considered.
- Recommended arrangement.
- Evidence and expected benefits.
- Affected roles, countries, and employee groups.
- Key risks and mitigations.
- Funding and resource requirements.
- Required consultation and approvals.
- Success measures.
- Accountable owner.
- Review or renewal date.

Attendance requirements must explain why physical presence is necessary, why remote participation is insufficient, expected frequency, accessibility alternatives, travel implications, and how effectiveness will be assessed.

## 4.3 Risk tiers

- **Tier 1 — Standard:** Low-risk roles and locations using approved templates and delegated approval.
- **Tier 2 — Enhanced review:** Arrangements involving significant customer interaction, confidentiality, accessibility complexity, time-zone burden, or infrastructure limitations.
- **Tier 3 — Specialist review:** High-security, safety-critical, regulated, cross-border, crisis-sensitive, or collective-bargaining-sensitive arrangements.

Tier 3 arrangements require review by relevant legal, tax, security, employee-relations, safety, accessibility, or global governance functions.

# 5. Employee rights, responsibilities, and service standards

Subject to local law and collective agreements, employees should have:

1. A clear process to request flexibility or a change in work model.
2. A written decision within a published timeframe.
3. Objective reasons for approval or denial.
4. Information about reasonable alternatives considered.
5. A review or appeal route handled by an appropriately independent reviewer.
6. Protection from retaliation for making a legitimate request.
7. Confidential handling of disability, health, and accommodation information.
8. Clear information about equipment, expenses, availability, and work-location requirements.
9. Reasonable communication and disconnection expectations.
10. A process for temporary adjustments when an approved arrangement becomes impractical.
11. Clear rules for changing work country or location.

Illustrative global service baselines, subject to stronger local requirements, are:

| Process | Baseline |
|---|---|
| Acknowledgment of flexibility request | Within 3 business days |
| Standard flexibility decision | Within 15 business days after complete information |
| Appeal decision | Within 15 business days |
| Accommodation request acknowledgment | Within 2 business days |
| Initial accommodation decision | Within 10 business days where information is available |
| Standard equipment provisioning | By the employee’s start date or within 5 business days |
| Cross-border work decision | Within 10 business days after complete information |
| Critical accessibility barrier | Immediate escalation with an agreed resolution plan |

Employees are expected to:

- Deliver agreed outcomes and maintain quality.
- Follow security, privacy, safety, and confidentiality requirements.
- Participate reliably during agreed collaboration windows.
- Document decisions and maintain shared records.
- Attend purposeful in-person activities when required.
- Raise technology, workload, wellbeing, or accessibility concerns early.
- Obtain approval before changing work country or location.

# 6. Team operating agreements

Each team should document and periodically review an operating agreement covering:

- Where work is normally performed.
- Availability expectations and core hours.
- Synchronous versus asynchronous communication.
- Response-time expectations.
- Meeting principles and decision rights.
- Documentation and file ownership.
- Escalation methods.
- In-person events and their purpose.
- Accessibility and language practices.
- Review dates and triggers for change.

Agreements should be co-created with employees, published accessibly, and reviewed at least semiannually or when team composition or business requirements materially change.

Global collaboration norms should include:

- Agendas and pre-reads for important meetings.
- Written decisions, actions, and owners.
- Fewer and shorter recurring meetings.
- Meeting-free focus periods.
- Captions and accessible materials.
- Plain-language communication.
- Translation or repeated sessions for critical information where needed.
- Rotation of inconvenient meeting times.

Employees should have a channel to challenge agreements that are inconsistent, inaccessible, discriminatory, or unsupported by business evidence.

# 7. Offices and physical workplaces

## 7.1 Define the purpose of each site

Offices should support activities that benefit from physical presence, such as:

- Team collaboration and problem-solving.
- Onboarding, learning, and mentoring.
- Innovation and experimentation.
- Customer and partner engagement.
- Secure or specialized work.
- Culture, community, and belonging.
- Access to resources unavailable at home.
- Business continuity.

Required attendance should have a stated purpose, accountable owner, evidence of expected benefit, accessibility alternatives, funding clarity, and a review date.

## 7.2 Portfolio decisions

Assess each site using:

- Business and customer requirements.
- Utilization and purpose of use.
- Workforce and team distribution.
- Accessibility and transportation.
- Local labor-market conditions.
- Lease and financial commitments.
- Security and resilience.
- Environmental performance.
- Climate and infrastructure risk.
- Future growth or contraction scenarios.

Badge swipes alone are insufficient. Assess whether a site improves collaboration, learning, customer outcomes, security, resilience, accessibility, or employee experience.

Before closing or consolidating a site, assess effects on accessibility, commuting, retention, local employment, security, specialist capability, and business continuity.

Real-estate cost may influence portfolio design but should not, by itself, justify mandatory employee attendance.

## 7.3 Inclusive workplace design

Offices should provide:

- Quiet and focus areas.
- Team and project rooms.
- High-quality hybrid meeting rooms.
- Private spaces for health, prayer, caregiving, or reflection.
- Low-stimulation areas.
- Accessible circulation and workstations.
- Confidential work areas.
- Appropriate acoustics, lighting, air quality, and temperature.
- Social and community spaces.

---

# 8. Digital workplace and responsible technology

## 8.1 Minimum digital workplace standard

For approved work models, define standards for:

- Devices and peripherals.
- Secure identity and access.
- Connectivity and backup options.
- IT support and replacement timelines.
- Accessibility and assistive technology.
- Local-language support.
- Low-bandwidth or offline alternatives.
- Secure use of hubs and coworking spaces.
- Business continuity during power, network, or office disruption.

The company should fund minimum role-appropriate equipment and necessary accessibility support. Policies must distinguish employer-provided, reimbursable, and employee-borne costs.

## 8.2 Integrated toolset and lifecycle

Provide a coherent ecosystem for:

- Video and real-time communication.
- Persistent team collaboration.
- Documents and project management.
- Enterprise search and knowledge management.
- Learning and development.
- Digital approvals and signatures.
- Virtual whiteboards and ideation.
- Accessible employee feedback.

A lifecycle process should govern procurement, security, privacy, accessibility, localization, integration, training, retention, outage response, migration, and retirement. Core platforms should be limited where practical, with documented alternatives where local restrictions apply.

## 8.3 Privacy, monitoring, and AI

The company must not use keystroke monitoring, webcam checks, online presence, office badges, message volume, or similar activity data as standalone measures of productivity or commitment.

Maintain an inventory of workforce-data and AI systems. Each system should have:

- A clearly defined purpose and accountable owner.
- An appropriate legal basis.
- Data minimization and classification.
- Transparent employee or candidate notice.
- Human oversight.
- Bias, accessibility, and fairness testing.
- Defined retention and access controls.
- Local privacy and consultation review.
- Vendor due diligence and audit rights.
- A process to correct errors or challenge material decisions.

Prohibit emotion inference, biometric productivity surveillance, inferred health or protected-characteristic profiling, union-activity prediction, and fully automated high-impact employment decisions unless expressly justified, legally permitted, and independently reviewed.

Activity data collected for facilities, security, or service delivery must not be repurposed for performance management without additional governance review and transparency.

# 9. Inclusion, wellbeing, and career equity

## 9.1 Accessibility

Establish service levels for:

- Acknowledging accommodation requests.
- Making accommodation decisions.
- Providing assistive technology.
- Supplying accessible furniture and workspace.
- Captioning and accessible meeting support.
- Planning accessible travel and events.
- Resolving reported barriers.

Accessibility testing should involve employees with relevant lived experience before major tools, offices, or processes are deployed. Unresolved barriers require an accountable owner and escalation route. Third-party coworking and serviced-office providers should meet defined accessibility standards.

## 9.2 Career equity

Monitor whether work location affects:

- Promotion and pay.
- Performance ratings.
- Bonus outcomes.
- Strategic assignments.
- Leadership exposure.
- Mentoring and sponsorship.
- Recognition and international opportunities.

Material unexplained differences should trigger root-cause analysis, a mitigation plan, an executive owner, and follow-up measurement.

Global corporate language should not become an unacknowledged career filter. Important communications should use plain language and translation or repeated sessions where needed.

## 9.3 Sustainable work

Address wellbeing through work design, including:

- Workload and staffing.
- After-hours communication.
- Meeting volume.
- Time-zone burden.
- Psychological safety.
- Ergonomic risks.
- Isolation and social connection.
- Locally appropriate mental-health support.
- Emergency and crisis procedures.

Managers are responsible for addressing recurring excessive workload or “always-on” expectations.

---

# 10. Funding, cross-border work, and resilience

## 10.1 Cost ownership

Global policy should define responsibility for:

- Devices and peripherals.
- Internet and backup connectivity.
- Ergonomic equipment.
- Assistive technology.
- Coworking or hub access.
- Mandatory travel.
- Accessibility support.
- Emergency relocation and continuity support.

Policies should specify purchasing methods, approval levels, reimbursement timelines, tax treatment, equipment ownership, and options for employees who cannot pay costs upfront. Necessary business costs should not systematically shift to employees.

## 10.2 Cross-border work

Use a formal approval process covering, as relevant:

- Immigration and work authorization.
- Tax and payroll.
- Social insurance.
- Employment law.
- Permanent-establishment risk.
- Data transfer and security.
- Insurance.
- Health and safety.
- Emergency support.
- Maximum duration and approved locations.

Provide a simple decision tree distinguishing business travel, short-term remote work, temporary relocation, and permanent relocation. Publish approved locations and service timelines where possible, with an urgent process for humanitarian or safety circumstances.

## 10.3 Workforce continuity

A workforce-continuity standard should define:

- Critical roles and minimum staffing.
- Maximum tolerable downtime.
- Primary and alternative work locations.
- Backup equipment and connectivity.
- Emergency communication channels.
- Safety-check procedures.
- Evacuation and relocation support.
- Temporary policy exceptions.
- Crisis decision rights.
- Recovery exercises and post-incident reviews.

Plans should address natural disasters, civil unrest, public-health emergencies, internet or power disruption, geopolitical restrictions, and loss of office access.

# 11. Governance and accountability

## 11.1 Global governance

Establish a Global Future of Work Council including HR, Legal and Employee Relations, Tax, Finance, Technology, Information Security, Real Estate, Accessibility and Inclusion, Health and Safety, Risk, regional and business representatives, and employee representatives where required or appropriate.

The council should:

- Approve global principles and minimum standards.
- Maintain work-model criteria and the local-variation register.
- Own workforce-data, AI, privacy, security, and continuity safeguards.
- Review cross-border and high-risk arrangements.
- Own the global risk register.
- Review implementation reporting quarterly.
- Require remediation of material noncompliance.
- Report significant risks and outcomes to executive leadership or the board.
- Review the framework at least annually.

The company should name:

- An executive sponsor.
- A central program and policy owner.
- A budget owner.
- A local-variation register owner.
- An appeals owner.
- An emergency-exception authority.
- An independent assurance or internal-audit function.

## 11.2 Local governance

Country or regional bodies should determine, within the global framework:

- Permitted work locations.
- Local attendance practices.
- Working-time and collaboration norms.
- Benefits, allowances, and equipment arrangements.
- Office portfolio and design.
- Health-and-safety controls.
- Employee communications.
- Consultation and implementation sequencing.

Where global policy conflicts with local law or a collective agreement, the stronger legal or collectively agreed requirement applies. The conflict and resolution should be documented.

## 11.3 Country implementation gate

Before activating a material policy change, complete:

1. Local legal, tax, and immigration review.
2. Works-council, union, or employee consultation where applicable.
3. Health-and-safety assessment.
4. Payroll, benefits, and expense review.
5. Privacy, monitoring, and data-transfer review.
6. Accessibility review.
7. Local communication approval.
8. Defined support, appeal, and escalation processes.

## 11.4 Manager accountability

Managers should be evaluated on:

- Clarity of objectives.
- Team effectiveness.
- Employee experience.
- Inclusion and psychological safety.
- Development and retention.
- Consistent application of work-model rules.
- Workload and time-zone management.

Conduct periodic audits of attendance decisions, exceptions, promotion outcomes, and employee feedback. Repeated misuse of attendance requirements, proximity bias, exclusion, or failure to support accommodations should require remediation and may affect leadership evaluations.

Occupancy or visibility targets must not conflict with outcome-based management.

# 12. Implementation roadmap

## Phase 1: Diagnose

Assess:

- Role and task characteristics.
- Employee needs and constraints.
- Country requirements and employee representation.
- Office utilization and purpose.
- Technology and infrastructure readiness.
- Accessibility gaps.
- Collaboration and productivity barriers.
- Time-zone distribution.
- Financial, environmental, security, and resilience implications.

Include frontline employees, employee resource groups, accessibility advisers, and employees with limited digital access.

## Phase 2: Design

Develop:

- Global principles and minimum standards.
- Work-design matrix and role profiles.
- Local-adaptation playbook.
- Office-purpose and design standards.
- Digital, security, privacy, and AI standards.
- Team-agreement templates.
- Flexibility and appeal processes.
- Manager capability and accountability program.
- Cross-border process.
- Accessibility and wellbeing standards.
- Funding and expense rules.
- Metrics and governance processes.

## Phase 3: Pilot

Test contrasting environments, including:

- A site-based operational team.
- A hybrid professional team.
- A distributed technology team.
- A laboratory or manufacturing site.
- A low-connectivity location.
- A multi-time-zone team.
- A safety-critical or consultation-sensitive environment.

Test normal operations and failure scenarios, including outages, office closures, accessibility needs, security incidents, crisis relocation, and inconsistent manager application.

## Phase 4: Implement

Implementation should include:

- Country-by-country validation.
- Required consultation and approvals.
- Clear communication of global and local elements.
- Manager training and practical scenarios.
- Equipment and accessibility deployment.
- Office and technology changes.
- HR-system and policy updates.
- Support and escalation channels.
- A defined transition period.
- Digital workflows for role assessments, flexibility requests, accommodations, and local deviations.

Existing arrangements should be addressed through a transition protocol covering notice, grandfathering or review, contract changes, relocation impacts, and employee support.

## Phase 5: Learn and adapt

Review quarterly during rollout and at least semiannually thereafter. Adjust policies based on:

- Business outcomes.
- Employee experience.
- Inclusion and career equity.
- Workload and wellbeing.
- Technology reliability.
- Office effectiveness.
- Security and privacy.
- Local feedback and representative input.
- Unintended consequences.

Major policies should have review or renewal dates rather than becoming permanent by default.

# 13. Measurement and outcomes

Use a balanced scorecard rather than a single measure such as office occupancy or digital activity.

| Dimension | Example measures | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Business | Customer satisfaction, quality, delivery, cycle time, innovation, handoff delays | Business leaders |
| Employee experience | Clarity, effectiveness, belonging, manager quality, tool satisfaction, intent to stay | HR and business |
| Equity | Promotion, pay, ratings, development, leadership exposure by work model | HR and Inclusion |
| Accessibility | Accommodation response time, accessibility satisfaction, unresolved barriers | Inclusion and HR |
| Technology | Equipment provisioning, reliability, support response, hybrid-meeting quality | Technology |
| Workplace | Purposeful use, space effectiveness, travel burden, cost, energy, emissions | Real Estate and Finance |
| Wellbeing | Workload, burnout indicators, after-hours burden, psychological safety, time-zone burden | HR and business |
| Risk | Security, privacy, safety, continuity readiness, cross-border exceptions | Risk, Legal, Security |

For every core metric, define:

- Formula and data source.
- Baseline period.
- Target or acceptable range.
- Data owner.
- Reporting frequency.
- Privacy protections.
- Corrective-action threshold.
- Escalation destination.

Use aggregation, suppression, or independent review where small populations could identify individuals. Material unexplained equity gaps, recurring accessibility delays, excessive time-zone burden, or failure to meet technology service levels should trigger corrective action.

Benefits-realization reviews should assess productivity, recruitment and retention, office and technology cost, travel and emissions, risk reduction, implementation cost, learning, innovation, and culture.

When outcomes conflict, apply this priority order:

1. Legal, safety, privacy, and employee-protection requirements.
2. Customer and operational continuity.
3. Fairness and accessibility.
4. Sustainable employee experience.
5. Financial and environmental optimization.

# 14. Principal risks and controls

| Risk | Required control |
|---|---|
| Manager inconsistency or informal attendance mandates | Documented criteria, audits, appeals, manager accountability |
| Proximity bias | Structured reviews, calibration, development-access monitoring |
| Local fragmentation | Variation register, approval rules, annual comparability review |
| Employee cost shifting | Global funding and reimbursement standards |
| Privacy or surveillance creep | Data inventory, purpose limitation, retention, access controls |
| AI discrimination | Risk classification, testing, human oversight, appeal rights |
| Digital overload | Tool governance, meeting standards, workload monitoring |
| Accessibility delays | Service levels, procurement testing, accountable escalation |
| Time-zone inequity | Rotating schedules, burden metrics, asynchronous alternatives |
| Loss of learning and culture | Mentoring, onboarding, communities of practice, purposeful gatherings |
| Crisis failure | Tested continuity plans, alternate locations, emergency authority |
| Consultation failure | Country implementation gate and employee-representative engagement |
| Office under- or overinvestment | Purpose-based portfolio reviews and benefits realization |
| Policy obsolescence | Annual review, renewal dates, legal and technology scanning |

Each material risk should have a named owner, monitoring indicator, escalation threshold, remediation deadline, and residual-risk acceptance authority.

---

# 15. Decision test for any new work practice

Before introducing or changing a work practice, leaders should ask:

1. What business or employee problem does this solve?
2. Does the work genuinely require a particular location or time?
3. What legal, tax, privacy, security, safety, accessibility, and consultation requirements apply?
4. How will different countries, roles, cultures, and employee groups be affected?
5. Could it create proximity, language, gender, caregiving, socioeconomic, or time-zone bias?
6. What alternatives were considered?
7. What technology, workspace, funding, and manager capability are required?
8. What employee or representative input is required?
9. What is the risk tier and required approval level?
10. How will success and unintended effects be measured?
11. Who is accountable for the decision?
12. When will the decision be reviewed or renewed?

## Conclusion

A successful global future-of-work strategy will not maximize either remote work or office attendance. It will create a deliberate work system in which:

- Work models reflect actual work requirements.
- Offices have a clear and measurable purpose.
- Remote work is sustainable, secure, and supported.
- Hybrid work is intentional and equitable.
- Frontline roles receive meaningful flexibility in appropriate forms.
- Managers lead through outcomes and trust.
- Technology is accessible, reliable, and responsibly governed.
- Employee voice and local requirements shape implementation.
- Career opportunity does not depend on physical visibility.
- Decisions are reviewed through evidence rather than ideology.

The framework becomes practical when its principles are converted into enforceable mechanisms: documented role decisions, employee rights, service levels, funding rules, local-variation controls, manager accountability, workforce-continuity plans, privacy safeguards, and measurable outcomes.
