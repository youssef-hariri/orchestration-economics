# Executive Roadmap: Five-Warehouse Automation and Workforce Transition

## Executive decision

Authorize a **controlled diagnostic, process-improvement, and pilot-design program** across the five-warehouse network. Defer:

- Broad equipment procurement
- Irreversible workforce reductions
- Full conversion of the largest warehouse
- A committed three-year payback
- Network-wide or “lights-out” automation

Adopt a **selective, modular, hybrid operating model**:

1. Pilot targeted automation at the highest-scoring, most recoverable site—not automatically the largest or most labor-intensive warehouse.
2. Begin with repeatable bottlenecks and no-regret process improvements.
3. Scale only after independently verified service, safety, workforce, and financial performance.
4. Transform the largest employer site through zones and multiple investment gates.
5. Use flexible, reversible automation at the seasonal site.
6. Retain a predominantly manual-plus model at the smallest or most specialized site unless evidence supports more.
7. Select the **lowest automation intensity that solves the demonstrated bottleneck**.

Success means lower verified cost per order, fewer errors, stable peak service, safer and more resilient operations, accountable suppliers, and a fair workforce transition—not simply fewer picker positions.

---

## 1. Assumptions and decisions pending

### Current assumptions

- The five warehouses have different volumes, SKU profiles, layouts, labor markets, and customer requirements.
- Picking and travel may be significant labor and error drivers, but the full receiving-to-returns flow will be assessed.
- Picker turnover is approximately 35% annually.
- Errors affect important accounts and may create rework, returns, credits, claims, or lost business.
- Capital is available but constrained relative to competing investments.
- The largest warehouse is the principal employer in its town.
- Labor-relations, consultation, transfer, severance, and collective-bargaining obligations remain to be confirmed.
- Existing WMS/WES/ERP, controls, data quality, cybersecurity, and maintenance capabilities may constrain implementation speed.
- Vendor claims of a three-year payback and 20–35% labor-hour reduction are hypotheses, not commitments.

No final site, technology, headcount, payback, or conversion date should be approved before validation.

### Required facts before capital approval

Establish by site:

- Orders, lines, units, cases, pallets, and throughput by hour
- Average, peak, and seasonal demand
- SKU dimensions, weight, velocity, fragility, and handling restrictions
- Order profiles and customer-specific requirements
- Labor hours and fully loaded cost by process
- Overtime, agency labor, vacancies, absenteeism, training time, and turnover
- Pick, pack, ship, inventory, and returns accuracy
- Damage, rework, returns, credits, claims, and complaints
- WMS/WES/ERP architecture, interfaces, and master-data quality
- Building, lease, height, floor-loading, power, fire, network, and expansion constraints
- Maintenance, engineering, and local technical-labor availability
- Workforce skills, tenure, representation, retirement outlook, and transferability
- Customer commitments, penalties, and key-account requirements
- Alternate-site, carrier, overflow, and 3PL capacity
- Capital envelope, discount rate, return hurdle, and competing projects

Every material assumption must include a value or range, source, owner, confidence rating, and sensitivity.

**Confidence standard**

- **High:** reconciled internal data or independently verified comparable-site evidence
- **Medium:** internally estimated and partially validated
- **Low:** vendor estimate, simulation, analogy, or unverified assumption

Low-confidence assumptions may support design work and sensitivity analysis, but not irreversible capital or workforce decisions.

## 2. Network design principles

The warehouses must be evaluated as one network, not as five isolated projects.

Before final sequencing, model:

- Inventory and SKU reassignment between sites
- Inter-site transfer volumes, transportation costs, and lead-time effects
- Alternate-site capacity during conversion and outages
- Cost of retaining duplicate manual capacity
- 3PL and overflow availability, cost, and lead time
- Customer-service and key-account consequences
- Whether the largest site should host technical training, repair, or shared support
- The maximum number of concurrent transformations the organization can execute safely

Assess the complete operating flow:

\[
\text{Receiving} \rightarrow \text{Put-away} \rightarrow \text{Replenishment} \rightarrow \text{Picking} \rightarrow \text{Packing} \rightarrow \text{Shipping} \rightarrow \text{Returns}
\]

Automation should address the largest verified economic, quality, safety, or service constraint—not simply the largest labor pool.

The network model must compare:

- Current-state operations
- Manual-plus improvements
- Pilot conversion
- Site-by-site scale-up
- Peak demand
- Lower-than-planned volume
- Major outage scenarios
- Retained manual resilience capacity

## 3. Provisional site portfolio

The following positions are illustrative pending completion of the site scorecard.

| Site archetype | Recommended posture | Potential scope |
|---|---|---|
| **Stable, high-volume, repetitive** | Preferred pilot candidate | Bounded goods-to-person, AMRs, scan verification, selective conveyance or sortation |
| **Largest warehouse and main employer** | Strategic, phased transformation | No-regret improvements followed by zone-based picking, replenishment, packing, or sortation |
| **Mixed-profile regional site** | Targeted automation | Pick-to-cart, pick-to-light, AMRs, verification, selected packing or sortation |
| **Seasonal or volatile site** | Flexible and reversible automation | Mobile pick-assist, AMRs, digital labor planning, slotting, modular equipment |
| **Small, constrained, or specialized site** | Manual-plus / low automation | Scanning, digital work allocation, ergonomics, inventory controls, selective pick-assist |

The actual pilot will be the highest-scoring site after evidence review. The second deployment will be the highest-scoring comparable site after pilot validation, not a predetermined warehouse.

### Site-selection scorecard

Score each site from 1 to 5, supported by evidence, confidence ratings, and similarity assessments for any comparable reference sites.

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Repeatability and standardization | 15% |
| Volume and expected utilization | 12% |
| SKU and order-profile compatibility | 10% |
| Labor opportunity in automatable processes | 10% |
| Service criticality and recoverability | 10% |
| Building and infrastructure fit | 10% |
| IT, data, and integration readiness | 8% |
| Peak-period suitability | 8% |
| Maintenance and technical capability | 6% |
| Workforce-transition feasibility | 5% |
| Capital intensity and affordability | 4% |
| Community and reputational risk | 2% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

A site is disqualified until it has credible solutions for:

- Structural, fire, lease, and infrastructure constraints
- WMS and controls integration
- Peak capacity
- Maintenance and technical support
- Manual fallback
- Benefits measurement
- Labor and legal compliance

## 4. Automation-intensity framework

For every candidate process, compare at least three alternatives:

1. **Manual-plus:** slotting, scanning, labor planning, ergonomics, digital task allocation, quality controls, and process redesign
2. **Assistive automation:** pick-to-light, pick-to-cart, AMRs, conveyance, verification, and semi-automated packing
3. **Targeted goods-to-person:** automated storage or goods-to-person for compatible SKU and order families
4. **Integrated semi-automation:** goods-to-person, sortation, packing, and WES orchestration
5. **High automation:** only where utilization, reliability, maintainability, flexibility, and returns justify it

### Required tests

#### Compatibility

\[
\text{Compatible volume share}
=
\frac{\text{Volume meeting technology criteria}}
{\text{Total volume}}
\]

Assess cube, weight, velocity, fragility, variability, packaging, and exception requirements.

#### Utilization

\[
\text{Utilization}
=
\frac{\text{Required productive capacity}}
{\text{Installed productive capacity}}
\]

Model average, peak, seasonal, growth, and downside demand.

#### Exceptions

Quantify manual intervention caused by:

- Irregular, damaged, or missing inventory
- Urgent and customer-specific orders
- Quality holds
- Replenishment failures
- Master-data defects
- System exceptions
- Products outside equipment specifications
- Returns and disposition requirements

#### Flexibility

Stress-test at least:

- 20–30% lower volume
- Higher-than-planned growth
- SKU proliferation
- Major customer gain or loss
- Seasonal peaks
- Changed order profiles

These are stress cases, not forecasts.

#### Reliability and recovery

Model two-hour, eight-hour, and 24-hour outages, including:

- Mean time to repair
- Recovery throughput
- Manual bypass capacity
- Lawful staffing and break limits
- Fatigue, absenteeism, and supervision
- Shipping-cutoff impact
- Key-account exposure
- Alternate-site capacity
- Transportation and lead-time effects

#### Workforce economics

Separate:

- Labor hours reduced
- Positions eliminated
- Vacancies not filled
- Overtime avoided
- Agency labor avoided
- Employees redeployed
- New technical roles added
- Actual recurring cash cost removed

Select the lowest-intensity solution that meets mandatory service, safety, quality, capacity, and financial requirements. If alternatives are otherwise comparable, prefer lower implementation risk, greater reversibility, and lower workforce disruption.

## 5. Roadmap and stage gates

Calendar ranges are planning windows, not commitments. Actual timing depends on data readiness, consultation, procurement, construction, integration, vendor capacity, and peak-season restrictions.

### Phase 0: Baseline, network design, and workforce preparation

Actions:

- Map the full process at all sites.
- Reconcile WMS, payroll, agency, quality, maintenance, and customer-service data.
- Establish data ownership, validation rules, and a common data dictionary.
- Define the baseline observation period, data-completeness standard, and reconciliation tolerance.
- Segment SKUs and orders by compatibility and opportunity.
- Identify labor, quality, service, safety, and ergonomic bottlenecks.
- Complete the site scorecard and network-capacity model.
- Create workforce skills inventories and future-role maps.
- Assess labor-relations, legal, community, safety, cyber, and infrastructure requirements.
- Appoint an owner’s engineer or independent technical assurance provider.
- Define the support operating model for maintenance, controls, WMS/WES, cybersecurity, spares, and incident response.
- Assess implementation capacity and set a limit on concurrent deployments.
- Define the preliminary capital envelope and governance model.

#### Gate 1: Diagnostic approval

Required evidence:

- Accepted baseline with defined observation period and reconciliation tolerance
- Site scores with confidence ratings
- Full-flow opportunity analysis
- Network capacity, inventory, transportation, and fallback model
- Workforce and labor-relations assessment
- Initial safety, cyber, building, and integration feasibility
- No-regret improvement plan
- Preliminary capital envelope and governance

---

### Phase 1: No-regret improvements

Implement where justified:

- Slotting and replenishment redesign
- Scan and location compliance
- Inventory accuracy controls
- Pick-path optimization
- Engineered labor standards
- Digital task allocation and labor planning
- Ergonomic lift and transport aids
- Packaging and order-verification controls
- Standard work and visual management
- Forecasting and peak-staffing improvements
- Preventive-maintenance and downtime reporting
- Returns and reverse-logistics process improvements

Maintain separate baselines for:

1. Pre-program performance
2. Post-no-regret performance
3. Post-automation performance

This prevents process-improvement benefits from being incorrectly attributed to automation.

---

### Phase 2: Bounded pilot

Pilot one or two defined processes at the selected site, such as:

- Goods-to-person for compatible high-volume SKUs
- AMRs for transport or replenishment
- Automated sortation for a stable outbound flow
- Semi-automated packing and verification

Retain:

- Manual exception processing
- Manual peak capacity
- Bypass capability for urgent and key-account orders
- Problem-resolution space
- Trained operators, maintainers, and supervisors
- A cross-functional command center
- Tested rollback capability

#### Commissioning sequence

1. Factory acceptance testing
2. Site installation verification
3. Integrated system testing
4. Operational simulation
5. Controlled production pilot
6. Peak-readiness test
7. Final acceptance
8. Hypercare and business-as-usual handover

Each step must have entry criteria, exit criteria, defect limits, and named approvers.

**Defect categories**

- **Critical:** safety, cyber, data-integrity, or service-blocking issue
- **Major:** material effect on throughput, accuracy, recovery, staffing, or customer service
- **Minor:** workaround available without material risk

No critical defect may remain open at production go-live. Maximum open major and minor defects must be specified in the Gate 2 approval package.

#### Gate 2: Pilot-design approval

Require:

- At least three automation alternatives
- Process-level TCO and downside cases
- Peak and outage-capacity model
- Workforce-transition plan
- Safety case
- Cybersecurity architecture
- Vendor due diligence and contract protections
- Customer-impact and fallback plan
- Defined benefits baseline and causal measurement method
- Approved numerical service, quality, uptime, backlog, recovery, safety, and financial thresholds
- Completed critical-path schedule
- Defined hypercare and support model
- Completed legal and labor-relations review
- Capital tranche limits and escalation thresholds

#### Gate 3: Go-live approval

Require:

- Passed factory and site acceptance tests
- Certified operators and technical support
- Manual fallback available at lawful and sustainable staffing levels
- Rollback and disaster-recovery tests completed
- Inventory, labor, carrier, and alternate-site buffers
- No unresolved critical safety, cyber, data, integration, or service defects
- Frontline review of standard work, alarms, ergonomics, exceptions, staffing, breaks, and fallback
- Red/amber/green readiness review with no unresolved red items
- Defined hypercare staffing, response times, escalation, and exit criteria
- Customer-priority and notification procedures approved

---

### Phase 3: Scale to the highest-scoring comparable site

Scale only after the pilot meets steady-state criteria. Select the next site based on:

- Process and SKU similarity
- Comparable order profiles
- Similar WMS and building conditions
- Available implementation capacity
- Peak-service recoverability
- Workforce feasibility
- Risk-adjusted return

Do not replicate the pilot design without revalidating local economics and constraints.

---

### Phase 4: Modular transformation of the largest warehouse

Treat the largest warehouse as a multi-year program:

1. Complete no-regret improvements.
2. Automate one defined picking, replenishment, packing, or sortation zone.
3. Stabilize and measure that zone.
4. Expand only after a separate investment gate.
5. Retire manual capacity only after sustained performance through representative peak demand.

Use the site as a potential center for:

- Automation-operator training
- Maintenance and mechatronics apprenticeships
- Repair and technical support
- Inventory-control expertise
- Network process improvement

No whole-site conversion should occur as a single event.

---

### Phase 5: Seasonal and low-fit sites

For the seasonal or volatile site:

- Use mobile, modular, relocatable, or flexible technology.
- Avoid large fixed systems unless the downside utilization case works.
- Preserve scalable manual and temporary labor options.

For the small or specialized site:

- Retain a predominantly manual-plus model unless a discrete bottleneck produces a compelling risk-adjusted return.
- Avoid forcing network standardization where the operating profile does not support it.

## 6. Workforce-transition plan

### Workforce hierarchy

1. Improve and redesign work before reducing staffing.
2. Fill new technical and support roles internally where feasible.
3. Redeploy affected employees at the same site.
4. Offer transfers to nearby sites.
5. Provide paid training linked to real or credible future roles.
6. Use retirement, voluntary separation, reduced hours, or job sharing where appropriate.
7. Reduce agency labor and unnecessary external hiring only where service and fairness permit.
8. Model attrition as a range; do not depend on it as the primary plan.
9. Use involuntary reductions only for residual roles that demonstrably no longer exist.

Do not set a predetermined headcount reduction.

### Future-role catalogue

For each future role, define:

- Number of positions
- Qualifications and competencies
- Training duration and cost
- Certification requirements
- Pay range and progression
- Shift and schedule requirements
- Accommodation requirements
- Location and transfer implications
- Internal and external labor availability
- Expected retention

Priority roles include:

- Automation operators and control-room coordinators
- Maintenance and electrical/mechatronics technicians
- WMS/WES support
- Data and inventory analysts
- Exception and quality-resolution specialists
- Replenishment and problem-solving teams
- Safety, training, and continuous-improvement leads
- Customer-specific fulfillment specialists

### Workforce model

\[
\text{Future FTE requirement}
=
\frac{\text{Forecast workload}\times\text{Future labor hours per unit}}
{\text{Productive hours per FTE}}
+
\text{Peak and exception buffer}
\]

Model low, expected, and high attrition cases. Show the resulting requirements for:

- Redeployment
- Retraining
- External hiring
- Overtime
- Agency labor
- Residual positions
- Service consequences
- Transition costs
- Timing of any reductions

The role-by-role bridge must distinguish current roles, future roles, vacancies not filled, new roles, redeployments, voluntary exits, and residual reductions.

### Training and fairness controls

Training must be:

- Paid and conducted during work time where feasible
- Accessible across shifts, languages, ages, educational backgrounds, and disabilities
- Based on transparent, job-related criteria
- Supported by remediation
- Linked to actual vacancies or credible future roles

Establish a written process for application, denial, accommodation, appeal, and independent review, including:

- Application deadlines
- Decision timelines
- Written reasons for denial
- Appeal timelines
- Accommodation response standards
- Monitoring by site, shift, role, tenure, and legally permitted demographic categories

Do not use individual productivity data for punitive performance management during stabilization except for documented safety, conduct, or quality matters.

Track:

- Training access and completion
- Certification and observed competence
- Adverse impact
- Accommodation requests
- Training denials and appeals
- Redeployment outcomes
- Grievances and resolution time
- Work pace, fatigue, break compliance, schedule stability, and job quality
- Musculoskeletal and other occupational-health indicators

### Involuntary reductions

If unavoidable:

- Confirm that the role genuinely no longer exists.
- Complete consultation and legal review.
- Use objective, job-related, consistently applied criteria.
- Conduct adverse-impact review.
- Provide notice, severance, benefits continuation, appeal mechanisms, and outplacement.
- Maintain complete decision records.

### Largest-site community plan

Before material automation:

- Engage employees, representatives, local officials, and community institutions.
- Communicate what is decided, undecided, and contingent on pilot results.
- Establish technical apprenticeships with colleges or workforce agencies.
- Assess transportation, childcare, language, schedule, age, disability, and education barriers.
- Locate training, maintenance, repair, inventory, or shared-support work there where feasible.
- Review employment and community impacts quarterly.

HR/Labor Relations and Communications jointly own the communication plan, using one approved fact base and a defined cadence for diagnostic launch, site selection, pilot design, training selection, go-live, material service incidents, workforce decisions, and major scope changes.

## 7. Service continuity and fallback

Before pilot approval, establish numerical thresholds based on contractual obligations and baseline variability for:

- On-time shipment
- Key-account service
- Order-cycle time
- Accuracy
- Maximum backlog
- Maximum outage duration
- Recovery-time objective
- Manual-fallback throughput
- Peak capacity
- Customer-credit and penalty triggers

Use average, percentile, and maximum-consecutive-breach measures for normal and peak operations.

### Threshold table

The Gate 2 approval package must include:

| Measure | Baseline | Normal target | Peak target | Stabilization tolerance | Maximum breach duration | Source | Owner | Consequence |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|---|---|

### Conversion controls

- Convert one zone or process at a time.
- Schedule cutovers outside peak periods.
- Test actual SKU and order profiles.
- Conduct a complete dress rehearsal.
- Freeze nonessential master-data changes.
- Build inventory, labor, carrier, and alternate-site buffers.
- Operate manual and automated paths in parallel.
- Maintain a daily command center.
- Define stop/go and rollback owners.
- Retain manual bypass until peak testing and steady-state criteria are met.

### Fallback sizing

For two-hour, eight-hour, and 24-hour outages, quantify:

- Orders and lines at risk
- Manual throughput
- Required labor under lawful hours and break rules
- Absenteeism and supervision
- Fatigue and ergonomic limits
- Available inventory
- Alternate-site capacity
- Transportation and lead-time effects
- Key-account priority volume
- Carrier and 3PL availability
- Expected backlog and recovery time

Predefine customer-priority tiers, allocation rules, notification triggers, and authority for substitutions, split shipments, delays, alternate-site use, and 3PL activation. Record these decisions during each incident.

Pause or roll back for:

- Material service deterioration
- Serious safety risk
- Sustained quality failure
- Insufficient fallback
- Unresolved critical cyber or integration defects
- Vendor failure to meet acceptance or support obligations
- Failure at peak volume

## 8. Financial and capital discipline

### Total cost of ownership

Include:

- Equipment, robotics, storage, conveyance, sortation, and packing
- WMS/WES/ERP changes, interfaces, and data remediation
- Engineering, simulation, integration, and project management
- Installation, commissioning, and acceptance testing
- Building, power, fire, network, and structural work
- Training and backfill
- Duplicate manual and automated operations
- Inventory, labor, carrier, and overflow buffers
- Severance, redeployment, and outplacement
- Maintenance, spares, software, cybersecurity, energy, technical labor, and exception labor
- Equipment refresh and obsolescence
- Financing, decommissioning, lease reinstatement, and residual-value risk

### Financial definition

Use a consistent cash-flow perimeter:

\[
\text{Verified net benefit}
=
\text{Counterfactual cash operating cost at actual workload}
-
\text{Actual cash operating cost including automation}
\]

Actual automation costs must include maintenance, energy, software, technical labor, exception labor, and support. Show depreciation and other non-cash accounting effects separately.

Include all transition cash costs in NPV and discounted payback, including training backfill, duplicate operations, severance, temporary capacity, consulting, construction disruption, and cutover losses.

Finance must issue the approved cash-flow policy covering operating benefits, transition investment, financing, taxes, working capital, depreciation, corporate support, and strategic benefits.

### Required outputs

For every alternative:

- Annual cash flows
- NPV
- IRR or approved return measure
- Simple and discounted payback
- Cost per order and order line
- Break-even volume and utilization
- Cash savings versus capacity benefits
- Base, downside, and stress cases

Classify benefits as:

1. Realized recurring cash savings
2. Contracted incremental revenue
3. Documented avoided cost
4. Operational capacity without monetized value
5. Strategic or resilience benefit

Only categories 1–3 enter the base-case cash return unless a formal valuation method is approved. Unquantified resilience, goodwill, and customer-retention benefits remain separate.

### Financial hurdles

Before vendor selection, management must set:

- Minimum NPV
- Minimum IRR or ROIC
- Maximum simple payback
- Maximum discounted payback
- Minimum downside-case return
- Minimum proportion of recurring cash benefits
- Maximum capital exposure per site
- Approval route for strategic exceptions

The vendor’s three-year payback must be recalculated under:

- Lower volume
- Lower uptime
- Higher maintenance
- Higher exception rates
- Slower labor realization
- Retained manual fallback
- New technical roles
- Transition costs
- No-immediate-layoff assumptions

### Capital waterfall

Use mutually exclusive funding categories:

1. Separately approved diagnostic and design budget
2. Separately approved pilot budget with contingency
3. Uncommitted scale reserve released only after pilot acceptance
4. Uncommitted largest-site reserve released by zone
5. Targeted budget for seasonal and specialized sites
6. Workforce-transition costs assigned to the relevant project
7. Central contingency held outside project base budgets

For each tranche, specify:

- Maximum amount
- Release evidence
- Approver
- Stop condition
- Treatment of overruns
- Whether unused reserve returns to the portfolio

Contingency should be risk-based, covering construction, integration, vendor delay, downtime, labor transition, and service protection. Its calculation must show probability, impact, mitigation, residual exposure, and reserve requirement.

## 9. Benefits realization

### Baseline and causal method

Finance should establish a six- to twelve-month baseline where possible, normalized for:

- Volume
- SKU and order mix
- Seasonality
- Wage rates
- Shift mix
- Customer mix
- Peak versus normal periods

Select and lock the measurement method before go-live:

- Matched manual control zones
- Difference-in-differences
- Engineered labor standards reconciled to payroll
- Controlled pilot cohorts
- Time-and-motion studies

Use the method best suited to the site and process. Do not change it after results are known without independent approval.

### Benefits bridge

\[
\text{Counterfactual cash cost at actual workload}
-
\text{Actual post-change cash cost}
=
\text{Gross operating benefit}
\]

Explain the variance through:

- Volume
- Mix
- Wage rates
- Productivity
- Automation
- Overtime and agency
- Quality and claims
- Maintenance and energy
- Transition costs
- Other operational changes

No benefit is recognized without:

- A named owner
- A defined metric
- A baseline
- A start date
- A source system
- A validation method
- A financial treatment

### Benefit owners

| Benefit | Owner | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Direct labor and productivity | Operations and Finance | Payroll, WMS, labor standards |
| Overtime and agency | Operations | Payroll and invoices |
| Errors, returns, credits, and rework | Quality and Customer Service | Claims, returns, credits, rework |
| Capacity value | Network Planning and Finance | Throughput and documented avoided cost |
| Turnover savings | HR and Finance | Hiring, training, vacancy, attrition |
| Maintenance and energy | Engineering | Work orders, parts, invoices |
| Service performance | Commercial and Customer Service | Shipment and account data |

Turnover savings count only after observed reductions in recruiting, onboarding, vacancy, overtime, ramp-up losses, and attrition are sustained.

### Benefit-retention test

Scale decisions require:

- At least three consecutive steady-state months for stable sites
- A representative seasonal cycle, or justified equivalent, for volatile sites
- Approved peak-performance testing
- Benefits within approved tolerance
- Performance across an agreed workload range
- Evidence that temporary transition staffing has been removed or separately identified
- No material service, safety, cyber, or workforce deterioration

Missed benefits require a corrective plan, scope freeze, vendor remedy, redesign, or project closure.

## 10. Technology, vendor, safety, and cyber controls

### Target support operating model

Before go-live, define whether each capability is site-based, centralized, shared, vendor-provided, or hybrid:

- Mechanical and electrical maintenance
- Controls and robotics support
- WMS/WES support
- Cybersecurity monitoring
- Spare-parts management
- Incident response
- Lifecycle upgrades
- 24/7 escalation

Set minimum coverage, competency, response times, restoration targets, training requirements, and handover responsibilities.

### Vendor requirements

Evaluate:

- Comparable reference sites, including delayed or failed deployments
- Financial viability
- Peak performance and uptime
- Integration capability
- Maintenance and spare-parts support
- Cybersecurity maturity
- Product roadmap and obsolescence
- Training and knowledge transfer
- Subcontractor dependence
- Business continuity

Reference sites must be compared with the proposed site on:

- SKU cube, weight, velocity, and fragility
- Order lines per order
- Peak-to-average volume
- Exception rates
- Service commitments
- WMS and controls architecture
- Building configuration
- Labor model
- Maintenance model
- Automation scope and maturity

Contracts should include:

- Functional specifications
- Factory and site acceptance tests
- Throughput, accuracy, uptime, and recovery standards
- Staged payments and performance holdbacks
- Warranty and defect remediation
- Spare-parts and obsolescence commitments
- Data ownership and portability
- Cybersecurity standards and incident notification
- Controlled vendor remote access
- Change-order thresholds
- Termination and transition assistance
- Source-code or escrow protections where operationally critical

### Safety

Complete before go-live:

- Job-hazard and ergonomic assessment
- Traffic separation
- Guarding, interlocks, and emergency stops
- Lockout/tagout
- Fire, egress, charging, battery, and electrical reviews
- Accessibility and accommodation assessment
- Maintenance-access review
- Stop-work authority
- Training and certification
- Near-miss and corrective-action review

Assess job quality, fatigue, work pace, ergonomics, and staffing for automated operators, exception teams, replenishment, manual fallback, maintenance, and supervisory roles.

No unresolved critical safety issue may be overridden by financial or schedule pressure.

### Cybersecurity

Require:

- Segmentation of corporate IT, warehouse controls, safety systems, and vendor access
- Multifactor authentication
- Least-privilege access
- Logged, time-limited remote access
- Asset inventory and secure configuration
- Patch and vulnerability management
- Backups of software, configurations, and master data
- Tested recovery for ransomware, network, WMS, WES, and controls outages
- Manual procedures for system loss
- Incident response connecting IT, Operations, Safety, Legal, HR, and Communications

## 11. Governance and accountability

### Steering committee

The COO-sponsored committee should include:

- Operations
- Finance
- Engineering and automation
- IT and cybersecurity
- HR and Labor Relations
- Safety
- Commercial and Customer Service
- Legal and Procurement
- Site leadership

### Assurance and decision rights

- **Owner’s engineer:** technical design, integration, commissioning, and acceptance assurance
- **Independent assurance committee:** challenges economics, workforce impact, safety, cyber, service, and gate evidence
- **Steering committee:** capital, sequence, scope, and risk acceptance
- **Site leader:** local execution and readiness
- **Finance:** baseline and benefit verification
- **HR/Labor Relations:** consultation and transition
- **Frontline representatives and safety specialists:** practical workforce and safety review

Critical safety, cyber, and service risks have binding go-live veto authority. Before Gate 2, document:

- Who may invoke a veto
- Required evidence
- Whether invocation automatically stops go-live
- Who resolves disputes
- Who may lift the veto
- Required revalidation before lifting it
- Documentation and communication requirements

Frontline review is mandatory before go-live. Material unresolved objections must be escalated and cannot be silently closed.

### Escalation matrix

Set thresholds before Gate 2 for:

- Capital overrun
- Schedule delay
- Service deterioration
- Safety incidents
- Cyber findings
- Vendor underperformance
- Workforce-impact variance
- Benefits shortfall
- Reduction in compatible volume
- Increase in exception rate
- Any change affecting legal, safety, cyber, service, or workforce commitments

Missed benefits require a corrective plan, scope freeze, vendor claim, redesign, or project closure.

## 12. First 90 days

### Days 0–30

- Appoint the program office.
- Establish steering and independent assurance structures.
- Issue a common data template and data dictionary.
- Begin full-flow, labor, service, safety, infrastructure, and workforce baselines.
- Identify key-account constraints.
- Begin employee and representative engagement.
- Freeze unsupported vendor commitments.
- Define the baseline acceptance standard and confidence-rating process.

### Days 31–60

- Score and classify all five sites.
- Select preferred and backup pilot candidates.
- Complete bottleneck, error, time-and-motion, returns, and network-capacity analyses.
- Segment compatible SKUs and order profiles.
- Develop automation alternatives and downside cases.
- Begin vendor due diligence and reference checks.
- Develop role-by-role workforce scenarios and future-role catalogue.
- Define draft numerical service, safety, cyber, financial, and rollback thresholds.
- Establish the maximum number of concurrent deployments.
- Define the preliminary support operating model.

### Days 61–90

- Approve or reject pilot-design funding.
- Complete independent TCO and return analysis.
- Confirm building, integration, maintenance, safety, cyber, and support feasibility.
- Finalize consultation, training, redeployment, and community plans.
- Establish benefit owners, baselines, source systems, and causal methods.
- Build the critical-path, commissioning, hypercare, and fallback plans.
- Complete the capital tranche and escalation matrix.
- Publish decision principles and the roadmap internally.

## Final authorization

### Approve now

- Network diagnostics and data governance
- No-regret process, quality, and ergonomic improvements
- Network-capacity and fallback modeling
- Workforce consultation and skills mapping
- Pilot design and simulation
- Vendor due diligence
- Independent technical, safety, cyber, and financial assurance

### Defer

- Broad equipment procurement
- Final site commitment
- Predetermined headcount reductions
- Full conversion of the largest warehouse
- Scale deployment before sustained pilot evidence

The next investment decision requires:

- Completed site evidence and scorecards
- A completed network model
- Approved financial hurdles
- Numerical stage-gate thresholds
- Quantified fallback capacity
- A defined support operating model
- A fair, role-specific workforce-transition plan
- Completed commissioning, hypercare, and escalation procedures
- Independently reviewed benefits methodology
- Jurisdiction-specific labor and employment compliance

The program should proceed only when it can demonstrate that automation will improve the network—not merely move labor, risk, and errors to another part of the operation.
