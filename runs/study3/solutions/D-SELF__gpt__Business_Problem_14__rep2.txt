# Global Inclusive Product Guidance

## Purpose and operating standard

Design a dependable product for people with different languages, scripts, abilities, identities, ages, economic circumstances, devices, legal situations, and safety environments.

A global product does not need to provide one identical experience everywhere. It should provide:

- **A consistent global floor:** safety, accessibility, privacy, transparency, user control, account recovery, and remedy.
- **Configurable experiences:** language, formatting, payments, calendars, content, support, and workflows based on user preference or validated requirements.
- **Locally validated decisions:** legal identity, cultural materials, moderation, safety, religious or political content, and high-impact automation.
- **Honest disclosure:** clearly identify unsupported languages, devices, features, or regions.

Commercial value must not determine whether users receive basic safety, accessibility, privacy, recovery, or appeal rights.

---

## 1. Decision framework

Use these categories consistently:

- **Global baseline:** Applies to all users unless an approved exception exists.
- **Configurable behavior:** Selected by users or configured through validated locale requirements.
- **Research-dependent:** Requires input from affected communities or subject-matter experts.
- **Jurisdiction-dependent:** Determined by law, regulation, infrastructure, or payment systems.
- **Unsupported scenario:** Not currently supported, with a clear explanation, safe alternative where possible, owner, and remediation date.

Define key terms before implementation:

- **Essential task:** A task required to access the product’s primary value, protect safety or privacy, complete a transaction, maintain account access, or exercise a user or legal right.
- **High-impact system:** A system that can affect access, identity, eligibility, pricing, finances, safety, legal status, employment, visibility, or account enforcement.
- **Supported experience:** An experience that meets defined requirements for essential-task completion, language quality, accessibility, security, support, recovery, and remedy—not merely one that has been translated.
- **Viable alternative:** A usable path that provides comparable access without requiring the same inaccessible, unavailable, or unsafe method.

For every material inclusion decision, record:

- Affected users or communities
- Assumptions and evidence
- Potential harms
- Research or expert input
- Acceptance criteria
- Owner and budget
- Residual risk
- Review date
- Incident and rollback plan

---

## 2. Product principles

### Design for variation without stereotyping

- Treat language, nationality, ethnicity, religion, caste, indigeneity, migration status, gender, sexuality, disability, age, income, and location as distinct dimensions.
- Segment by task, access, risk, and user need—not assumptions about national or cultural character.
- Do not encode assumptions about trust, directness, family structure, dress, religion, eye contact, silence, humor, or ability as universal rules.
- Use country as an operational unit only where justified by law, infrastructure, payments, or documented user needs.
- Include relevant minority, Indigenous, migrant, disabled, LGBTQ+, rural, displaced, and economically marginalized perspectives.

### Provide safe, reversible control

Offer controls for:

- Interface and content language
- Date, time, number, currency, and unit formats
- Calendars and time zones
- Notification channels and quiet hours
- Accessibility preferences
- Display names and forms of address
- Privacy, visibility, discoverability, and personalization
- Data storage or account region where applicable

Settings should be understandable, safe if misunderstood, reversible, available after onboarding, and changeable without losing progress. Use progressive disclosure rather than requiring users to complete a demographic or cultural profile before using core features.

### Minimize identity and cultural disclosure

Do not collect religion, ethnicity, caste, political views, sexuality, health information, or gender unless necessary for a defined purpose.

For each sensitive field, document:

- Purpose and necessity
- User benefit
- Legal basis
- Retention period
- Access controls
- Sharing and cross-border transfers
- Deletion and correction processes
- Risks of exposure
- Whether the product can be used without providing it

Do not infer sensitive traits from names, language, location, contacts, photos, religious dates, browsing, purchases, social graphs, or device patterns for personalization, advertising, ranking, or eligibility without exceptional legal, privacy, discrimination, security, and ethics review.

### Design with affected communities

Affected people must influence assumptions, priorities, terminology, imagery, safety models, and launch decisions early enough to change the product.

- Pay participants, advisers, translators, and reviewers fairly.
- Compensate community organizations for institutional expertise.
- Include diverse perspectives within communities; do not rely only on national, majority, urban, government, corporate, or formally recognized representatives.
- Record disagreement and uncertainty.
- Give qualified reviewers authority to require changes or delay an unsafe launch.
- Protect contributors from retaliation, unwanted disclosure, and extractive data practices.

## 3. Account, identity, and documentation features

### Names

Support:

- Mononyms and multi-part names
- Multiple family names
- Patronymics and matronymics
- Prefixes, suffixes, particles, hyphens, spaces, punctuation, and long names
- Diacritics, combining marks, non-Latin scripts, and multiple scripts
- Legal, preferred, display, and pronunciation names separately
- Name changes over time
- User-selected display and ordering conventions

Do not assume:

- The first token is a given name.
- The last token is a family name.
- A name reveals gender, nationality, religion, or legal status.
- Everyone has a middle name.
- A display name must match a legal name.

Technical requirements:

- Preserve the original entered form.
- Do not silently remove diacritics or transliterate names.
- Use Unicode-aware storage, comparison, search, sorting, and duplicate detection.
- Let users preview how names will appear.
- Test fraud, verification, and account systems for disproportionate rejection of unfamiliar names or scripts.
- Collect or expose legal names only for a specific operational or legal purpose.

### Gender, pronouns, and forms of address

- Omit gender when it is unnecessary.
- Avoid binary-only requirements.
- Use neutral wording or language-specific restructuring where possible.
- Handle grammatical gender through language rules, neutral phrasing, or explicit user preferences—not inferred personal gender.
- Offer pronouns, titles, honorifics, or self-description only when they serve a clear purpose.
- Consider whether displaying a chosen name or identity could create danger.
- Validate formality, politeness, titles, and address conventions by language and community.

### Addresses, phone numbers, and documents

- Support variable address structures, including locations without formal street addresses.
- Do not require a fixed number of address lines or a postal code unless necessary.
- Accept local phone-number formats and numbers that change across borders.
- Support people without stable addresses, conventional documents, or permanent phone numbers where law and risk permit.
- Provide safe alternatives for refugees, displaced people, migrants, and people with expired or missing documents.
- Do not expose nationality, immigration status, displacement, or location unless necessary.
- Allow users to correct information without recreating their account.

### Delegated and caregiver access

Where users may rely on caregivers, interpreters, family members, or organizational intermediaries:

- Provide narrowly scoped permissions.
- Separate helper access from account ownership.
- Require explicit consent where possible.
- Provide expiration, revocation, and user-visible access logs.
- Protect private information from unnecessary helper access.
- Offer safe recovery if a caregiver or intermediary becomes unsafe.
- Do not require forced delegation as the only way to access essential features.

### Shared devices and privacy

Support:

- Discreet notifications and hidden previews
- Quick sign-out and session management
- Adjustable profile visibility
- Protection against automatic contact syncing or discovery
- Privacy controls for workplace, family, caregiver, and shared-device environments

Consider domestic abuse, coercive control, government surveillance, limited privacy at home or work, and users who rely on intermediaries.

## 4. Accessibility and resilience

### Accessibility baseline

Use WCAG 2.2 AA as a minimum reference and meet the highest applicable legal or sector requirement. Accessibility must cover the product, support, emails, PDFs, invoices, receipts, help content, consent dialogs, authentication, recovery, and third-party components.

Support:

- Keyboard, switch, and alternative-input access
- Screen readers and semantic structure
- Voice control and speech input
- Text resizing, zoom, reflow, and magnification
- Sufficient contrast and visible focus
- Logical reading order and labels
- Captions, transcripts, audio descriptions, and meaningful alternatives
- Reduced motion and reduced flashing
- Predictable navigation
- Clear, specific error messages
- Save-and-return capability
- Pause, review, undo, and adequate time
- Accessible customer-service channels

Do not rely solely on color, sound, animation, position, gestures, facial recognition, or visual CAPTCHAs.

### Accessibility support matrix

Maintain a tested support matrix covering, where relevant:

- Operating systems and browsers
- Desktop and mobile platforms
- Screen readers
- Magnification tools
- Voice-control tools
- Switch devices
- Keyboard-only use
- Caption and audio-description workflows
- Payment, identity, analytics, consent, and other third-party components

Disclose unsupported combinations and provide an alternative where possible. Track unresolved barriers in an accessibility debt register with severity, affected users, workaround, owner, deadline, and release impact.

### Multilingual accessibility

- Translate screen-reader labels, captions, transcripts, alternative text, errors, PDFs, and support content.
- Test screen readers, text-to-speech, speech input, and OCR across supported languages, accents, and scripts.
- Support right-to-left and bidirectional reading.
- Ensure captions accurately represent names, dialects, code-switching, and relevant sounds.
- Do not present automated speech, OCR, or translation as equally reliable across languages when it is not.

### Authentication and verification

Provide alternatives to:

- Visual-only CAPTCHAs
- Voice-only verification
- Biometrics
- Government-ID checks
- High-end cameras or sensors
- Continuous location access

Test systems with diverse names, scripts, skin tones, facial features, clothing and head coverings, mobility aids, documents, accents, devices, and network conditions.

Provide accessible human-assisted recovery, explanations, and appeals. Review the accessibility and privacy of the full security lifecycle, including suspicious-login challenges, device verification, lockouts, fraud appeals, and password recovery.

### Literacy, cognition, and age

- Offer read-aloud, audio, visual, and voice alternatives.
- Pair icons with explanatory text.
- Test comprehension through actual tasks rather than reading level alone.
- Provide clear recovery from mistakes and persistent orientation.
- Avoid ageist assumptions about digital ability.
- Test with older adults and people with memory, attention, dexterity, intellectual, psychosocial, or cognitive disabilities.
- Offer adjustable complexity and notification density where appropriate.

### Connectivity, environment, and emergencies

- Provide low-bandwidth modes, compressed media, offline drafts, resumable uploads, and recovery after interruptions.
- Support low-cost devices and older operating systems where feasible.
- Make data usage visible.
- Avoid requiring continuous connectivity for essential tasks.
- Test battery constraints, power outages, glare, poor lighting, heat, dust, disaster conditions, and prolonged network disruption where relevant.
- For emergency use, define requirements for offline access, battery conservation, delayed synchronization, resource verification, and safe handling of location data.

## 5. Localization and language architecture

### Localize meaning

Use qualified translators and relevant community reviewers. For higher-risk content, reviewers must have appropriate subject-matter expertise.

Provide:

- Context and screenshots
- Character limits and layout constraints
- Variables and technical instructions
- Terminology and tone guidance
- Accessibility requirements
- Intended audience and consequences of error

Allow local adaptation of examples, humor, formality, politeness, calls to action, idioms, dates, units, and safety instructions.

### Content-risk review

| Content type | Minimum review |
|---|---|
| General interface text | Professional translation and linguistic QA |
| Transactional and account content | Professional translation, in-product QA, and local review |
| Legal, financial, medical, emergency, or safety content | Domain-qualified translation, independent local specialist review, and documented sign-off |
| Identity, political, religious, or vulnerable-group content | Subject-matter and affected-community review, with disagreement documented |
| Machine-generated or translated content | Clear labeling, relevant uncertainty, correction path, and no use as the sole source for high-risk instructions |

### Technical requirements

- Externalize all user-facing strings.
- Avoid string concatenation and hard-coded word order.
- Support plural categories, grammatical gender, noun cases, honorifics, formality, and pronoun agreement.
- Use locale-aware dates, numbers, currencies, units, calendars, names, addresses, sorting, and collation.
- Do not guess ambiguous dates, calendars, currencies, or time zones without confirmation.
- Support Unicode normalization, combining marks, complex shaping, mixed scripts, RTL, and bidirectional text.
- Protect bidirectional text against spoofing and display confusion.
- Test text expansion, wrapping, truncation, and large-text settings.
- Preserve original scripts when providing transliteration.
- Make search tolerant of reasonable spelling and diacritic variation without weakening privacy or security.
- Keep language preference independent from country, IP address, and account region.

Grammatical agreement must not require automatic inference of personal gender. Use neutral wording, sentence restructuring, language rules, or explicit user preference.

Do not treat “neutral terminology” as automatically preferable. Choose terms based on comprehension, community preference, safety, legal accuracy, and local usage.

### Language prioritization and service equivalence

Use a documented prioritization model that considers:

- Safety and essential-task criticality
- User need
- Underserved-language gaps
- Accessibility
- Support demand
- Language-model performance
- Legal and operational importance
- Community input
- Commercial relevance, but not revenue alone

The minimum global service floor includes:

- Safe access to essential tasks
- Accessible essential flows
- Privacy and consent controls
- Account recovery
- Critical safety, legal, financial, and health information
- Transaction transparency
- Appeals and human escalation
- Accessible support or a documented equivalent

Where exact parity is impossible:

1. Disclose the limitation.
2. Prioritize essential and safety-critical functions.
3. Provide a human or safe alternative.
4. Document the reason.
5. Assign an owner, budget, and remediation date.
6. Monitor error, abandonment, support, appeal, and harm rates.
7. Review whether the product should continue to claim support for that locale.

A locale should not be called supported if it fails defined service thresholds.

### Maintenance

Set review schedules and re-review triggers for:

- Legal or regulatory changes
- Political or safety changes
- Emergency contacts
- Product changes
- Terminology changes
- Moderation rules
- Translation defects
- Community complaints
- New model or vendor deployment
- New language or region launch

## 6. Visual design, imagery, and content

### Symbols and interaction

- Validate icons, gestures, colors, maps, and symbols with affected users.
- Pair unfamiliar icons with text.
- Do not use national flags as language selectors.
- Avoid political, religious, or national imagery unless directly relevant.
- Do not rely on idioms, humor, sports references, eye contact, smiles, silence, or communication style as universal signals.
- Do not encode meaning solely through color, position, or direction.
- Make public, private, organization-visible, and follower-only states explicit.
- Provide review, confirmation, pause, and undo before consequential actions.

### Imagery and representation

Use purposeful, authentic representation across ages, skin tones, body types, abilities, assistive devices, family structures, clothing, work, and living environments.

Avoid reducing communities to poverty, conflict, religion, traditional clothing, disability, social problems, or “exotic” scenery.

For photographs, illustrations, maps, community submissions, and cultural materials:

- Obtain informed consent and appropriate licenses.
- Protect dignity and contextual accuracy.
- Do not treat publicly available material as freely reusable.
- Do not use sacred, restricted, or collectively owned cultural material without documented permission.
- Respect cultural intellectual property and Indigenous protocols.
- Review gestures, clothing, food, body positioning, medical imagery, animals, currencies, maps, and disputed borders.
- Do not use AI-generated diversity without checking for distortion, tokenism, and erasure.
- Document who selected and approved high-visibility imagery.

Representation is not a substitute for participation. Record the purpose, risks, affected reviewers, and resulting product changes for significant representation decisions.

### Respectful language

Use language that is clear without being childish, simplistic, or patronizing. Avoid unnecessary idioms, slang, sarcasm, metaphors, and culturally specific references in essential flows.

Avoid terms such as:

- “Normal users”
- “Foreign users”
- “Primitive”
- “Exotic”
- “Illegal user”
- “Special needs”
- “Developing countries”
- “Low-literacy users”
- “Traditional families”
- “Non-native speakers,” where a more precise description is available

Prefer task-specific descriptions such as “users with limited connectivity,” “people who use screen readers,” or “people without a stable address.”

Use person-centered or identity-first language according to relevant community preference. Do not impose one style universally.

### Examples and education

- Use varied names, occupations, locations, households, and living arrangements.
- Do not treat one diet, holiday calendar, family model, dress style, or communication style as universal.
- Do not use minority identities only in examples about harm or difficulty.
- Clearly mark fictional examples.
- Never use real personal data.
- Review educational content for legal, medical, financial, cultural, and accessibility risks.

## 7. Children, vulnerable users, and Indigenous communities

### Children and young people

Where children or young people may use or be affected by the product:

- Apply age-appropriate privacy and safety principles.
- Explain data use, consent, and safety in age-appropriate language.
- Avoid manipulative engagement, unnecessary profiling, and inappropriate targeted advertising.
- Protect against unwanted contact, exploitation, and public exposure.
- Provide guardian controls without assuming every household is safe.
- Offer confidential support where permitted and appropriate.
- Use appropriate consent, assent, safeguarding, and compensation procedures in research.

### Vulnerable contexts

Consider people in conflict, disaster, detention, institutional care, displacement, domestic abuse, coercive control, surveillance, or politically restrictive environments.

Do not require unsafe disclosure of identity, location, legal status, disability, or household relationships.

### Indigenous communities and cultural materials

For Indigenous languages, communities, knowledge, names, symbols, or imagery:

- Engage Indigenous-led organizations and diverse community members where appropriate.
- Respect collective consultation and consent practices.
- Follow protocols for sacred, restricted, or sensitive material.
- Document ownership, licensing, permitted uses, storage, access, and reuse.
- Support Indigenous languages and naming systems without forcing them into majority-language models.
- Assess data sovereignty and community control.
- Do not use cultural knowledge for personalization, commercial exploitation, or model training without appropriate authorization.
- Do not assume one formally recognized authority represents all community members.

## 8. Privacy, safety, payments, and trust

### Safe defaults

Do not default to:

- Public profiles or broad discoverability
- Precise location collection
- Contact syncing or contact publication
- Biometric authentication where alternatives exist
- Marketing consent
- Autoplay, flashing, or high data usage
- Automatic publication of imported content
- Gendered language or binary identity fields
- Silent IP-based selection of consequential currency, calendar, time zone, or privacy settings

Defaults must be safe if misunderstood, clearly labeled, reversible, and tested in shared-device and low-privacy environments.

### Region-sensitive safety

Assess whether the product may expose users to surveillance, political repression, identity criminalization, religious persecution, gender-based violence, employer or family monitoring, doxxing, or retaliation.

Before launch, determine whether to collect or expose precise location, contacts, group membership, nationality, legal status, public profiles, identity attributes, activity history, device information, or network information.

Do not assume all users in one jurisdiction face the same risk. Offer user-controlled visibility, discreet notifications, hidden previews, session management, and safe sign-out where appropriate.

### Cross-border data

Document:

- Data residency and transfers
- Encryption and key management
- Government access processes
- Support-staff access
- Support transcript retention
- Local-language recovery
- Deletion and access limits imposed by law
- Vendor and subprocesser locations

Legal compliance must be supplemented by security, human-rights, and local-risk review. Escalate laws that would require discriminatory treatment, unsafe disclosure, or denial of essential access.

### Payments and financial inclusion

Where transactions are part of the product:

- Support locally available payment methods, currencies, taxes, invoices, and address formats.
- Do not assume access to international cards, bank accounts, formal addresses, or conventional identification.
- Clearly show fees, taxes, exchange rates, recurring charges, refund rules, and cancellation conditions.
- Provide accessible receipts and transaction history.
- Test fraud and identity systems for disparate rejection by name, script, document type, country, device, or payment method.
- Provide human review for blocked essential transactions where feasible.
- Explain regional feature differences without stigmatizing users.
- Provide clear alternatives when a payment method or identity document is unavailable.

### Safety-critical content

Emergency, health, abuse, self-harm, financial, and legal guidance must receive professional and local review.

- Confirm location before presenting location-specific resources where possible.
- Let users correct location.
- Do not rely on automated translation alone.
- Avoid sensational warnings.
- Explain uncertainty and limitations.
- Review emergency contacts and resources on a defined schedule.

## 9. AI and automated systems

Classify each system separately as personalization, translation, moderation, fraud prevention, identity verification, eligibility, pricing, safety triage, or generative assistance.

For each system:

- Record model, data, vendor, and evaluation provenance.
- Classify impact as low, medium, or high.
- Set accuracy, false-positive, false-negative, and disparity thresholds before launch.
- Use stricter, harm-weighted thresholds for essential and high-impact tasks.
- Involve domain experts and affected communities in threshold setting.
- Restrict, suspend, or do not deploy systems that fail thresholds or lack a safe alternative.
- Test languages, dialects, scripts, accents, names, clothing, skin tones, disability-related behavior, documents, and cultural contexts.
- Preserve uncertainty rather than implying reliability.
- Clearly identify generated, translated, summarized, or synthesized content.
- Prevent invented local laws, emergency services, medical guidance, or cultural practices.
- Do not train on user content without clear notice, appropriate consent where required, and user controls.
- Provide explanation, correction, appeal, and human escalation.
- Audit human reviewers for consistency, cultural competence, workload, conflicts of interest, and bias.
- Do not deploy unsupported-language automation for high-impact decisions.
- Restrict proxy use of names, language, location, contacts, or social graphs for sensitive inference.
- Maintain access logs and conduct periodic proxy-inference reviews.

Human review is not automatically fair. Reviewers need language and contextual expertise, calibration, accessible tools, quality audits, workload limits, and authority to reverse automated decisions.

## 10. Research and testing

### Research coverage

Conduct research before design lock and throughout the lifecycle. Include relevant variation in:

- Language, dialect, and script
- Minority and Indigenous communities
- Disability and assistive technology
- Age, including children where relevant
- Literacy and preferred communication mode
- Rural, urban, remote, and low-connectivity settings
- Income and banking access
- Migrant, refugee, and displaced circumstances
- Shared-device and low-privacy environments
- Conflict, disaster, or surveillance contexts
- Deafblind, cognitive, psychosocial, and intellectual accessibility
- Users relying on caregivers or intermediaries
- People without stable phone numbers, addresses, or documents

### Safe participation

Use:

- Safe recruitment channels
- Neutral study descriptions
- Private, asynchronous, or low-bandwidth participation options
- Minimal collection of identity and location
- Safe contact and payment methods
- Preferred-language consent and assent
- Deletion of identifying recordings when no longer necessary
- Researcher escalation procedures for safety concerns

Do not require unsafe disclosure. Compensate individuals and community organizations fairly using locally practical methods, accounting for currency, taxes, payment access, and power imbalances.

### Testing plans

Each material feature must have a risk-based test plan specifying:

- Variants to test
- Participants or test cases required
- Accessibility and platform coverage
- Blocking-failure criteria
- How contradictory findings will be handled
- When testing must be repeated

Test actual tasks, not only opinions. Ask what would make the product confusing, inaccessible, unsafe, embarrassing, politically risky, or likely to expose private information.

Use minimum group sizes, aggregation, access controls, and privacy review for disaggregated metrics. Use restricted-access expert review where small communities might otherwise become invisible.

## 11. Moderation and community safety

### Context-aware moderation

Support:

- Dialects and code-switching
- Transliteration and non-Latin scripts
- Slang and reclaimed language
- Caste-, ethnicity-, religion-, and migration-related terminology
- Political speech and religious expression
- Names resembling prohibited terms
- Sarcasm, indirect threats, and image or audio context

Do not use a universal keyword list as the sole moderation method.

### Fair enforcement

- Publish rules with understandable examples and exceptions.
- Give notice and reasons where safe.
- Provide evidence where appropriate.
- Offer human review, appeal, restoration, and published response times.
- Audit removals, suspensions, recommendations, and verification outcomes by language and relevant lawful fairness dimensions.
- Apply privacy safeguards to small or vulnerable communities.
- Train and support moderators, protect them from traumatic content, and audit automated and human decisions.

### Safety tools

Offer:

- Blocking and muting
- Message and comment controls
- Private accounts
- Reporting and escalation
- Discoverability controls
- Anti-doxxing protections
- Discreet safety settings where revealing their use could create danger

---

## 12. Error prevention and recovery

- Accept legitimate local name, address, phone, date, number, payment, and document formats.
- Do not guess ambiguous dates, currencies, calendars, or time zones without confirmation.
- Explain errors in the selected language.
- Identify exactly what needs correction and preserve valid input.
- Avoid blame-oriented messages.
- Provide locale-appropriate examples.
- Support autosave, drafts, undo, confirmation for irreversible actions, and connection recovery.
- Provide human escalation when automation blocks essential access.
- Show users how consequential information will be displayed before submission.
- Monitor false-rejection and false-acceptance rates across languages, scripts, devices, documents, and contexts.

---

## 13. Support, service levels, and remedy

Define service levels for:

- Supported languages
- Essential help and recovery
- Accessible contact channels
- Safety reports
- Discrimination complaints
- Appeals
- Translation defects
- Privacy requests
- High-impact automated decisions

Set product-specific elapsed-time targets before launch. At minimum:

- **Critical safety or identity exposure:** immediate containment and same-day escalation.
- **High-impact access denial:** expedited human review within a defined period.
- **Appeals:** a published maximum response time.
- **Critical translation or safety defects:** correction or rollback as soon as possible, with a documented deadline.
- **Privacy requests:** response within the applicable legal deadline and a shorter internal target where feasible.

Do not force users through inaccessible chatbots when they cannot complete the task. Support transcripts and case data must follow the same privacy, retention, localization, and cross-border controls as product data.

Possible remedies include:

- Restored access
- Corrected records
- Fee refunds
- Reversal of erroneous decisions
- Expedited human review
- Retraction or correction of harmful content
- Direct notification
- Additional privacy protection
- Compensation where legally and ethically appropriate

Define eligibility, decision owners, evidence standards, appeal rights, and response times for remedies. Consider foreseeable harm to non-users, such as people exposed through shared devices, uploaded content, or generated material.

## 14. Governance and accountability

### Inclusion council

Create a cross-functional body with representatives from:

- Product and engineering
- Design and accessibility
- Content and localization
- Research and data science
- Privacy, security, legal, and policy
- Safety and moderation
- Regional operations
- Community and subject-matter advisers

Define:

- Decision rights
- Budget access
- Quorum
- Conflict-of-interest rules
- Direct executive escalation
- Authority to delay or stop a launch
- Dissent-recording procedures
- Independent review
- Transparency obligations
- Confidential reporting and non-retaliation protections

The council must not be merely advisory for high-risk features.

### Risk register and ownership

For every feature, record:

- Affected users and communities
- Assumptions
- Cultural, accessibility, privacy, safety, and discrimination risks
- Research gaps
- Mitigations
- Residual risk
- Named owner
- Budget and staffing
- Acceptance criteria
- Review date
- Incident and rollback plan

Require inclusion impact assessments for identity verification, eligibility, pricing, advertising, ranking, recommendations, moderation, fraud detection, payments, support automation, and other high-impact systems.

### Incident severity and response

- **Critical:** Immediate safety threat, identity exposure, systemic exclusion, or severe discrimination.
- **High:** Repeated harmful failures affecting essential tasks or a vulnerable group.
- **Medium:** Localized offensive, misleading, or inaccessible content with a workaround.
- **Low:** Minor defect with limited impact.

Every incident plan must specify detection, triage, containment, rollback authority, user notification, investigation, correction, prevention, and review.

For major incidents, communicate what happened, who may be affected, temporary mitigation, user actions, permanent correction, and prevention measures while protecting privacy and security.

### Remedy and restitution

Define remedy triggers for:

- Incorrect charges
- Blocked access
- Identity errors
- Unwanted disclosure
- Discriminatory automated decisions
- Harmful safety information
- Repeated moderation errors

Review remedy outcomes for systemic patterns and notify affected users directly where feasible and safe.

---

## 15. Measurement, resourcing, and maintenance

Track more than aggregate conversion:

- Completion and abandonment by locale, language, device, and access context
- Error and false-rejection rates
- Accessibility defects and debt
- Translation and terminology defects
- Support coverage, response times, and unresolved complaints
- Appeal response times and reversals
- Moderation removals, suspensions, restoration, and disparities
- Verification, fraud, payment, and identity outcomes
- Performance on low-end devices and slow networks
- Privacy incidents and unintended disclosure
- Trust, comprehension, perceived respect, and belonging
- Service-quality differences between high- and low-revenue regions

Use privacy-preserving aggregation, minimum group sizes, and restricted access. Investigate disparities rather than treating them as user failure.

Every launch plan must budget and staff:

- Translation and linguistic review
- Community consultation and compensation
- Accessibility testing and remediation
- Local-language support
- Moderation and appeals
- Privacy, security, and legal review
- AI evaluation and monitoring
- Incident response
- Ongoing engineering maintenance

Assign maintenance owners for locale data, translations, accessibility components, emergency resources, models, moderation rules, support content, and vendor relationships. A feature is not supported if it cannot be maintained, reviewed, and remedied after launch.

---

## 16. Launch checklist

Before launch or regional expansion, confirm:

- [ ] Essential tasks and the minimum service floor are defined.
- [ ] Requirements are classified as global, configurable, research-dependent, jurisdiction-dependent, or unsupported.
- [ ] Names, addresses, phone numbers, dates, numbers, currencies, units, calendars, and documents support target contexts.
- [ ] Language and regional settings change without lost progress.
- [ ] RTL, bidirectional text, long translations, diacritics, mixed scripts, and complex shaping are tested.
- [ ] Accessibility testing includes disabled users and a documented platform and assistive-technology matrix.
- [ ] Third-party components and support channels meet accessibility requirements.
- [ ] Children, Indigenous communities, displaced users, caregivers, older adults, and other relevant groups have been considered.
- [ ] Professional translation and appropriate community review are complete.
- [ ] Translation review meets the content-risk level.
- [ ] Safety-critical, legal, financial, medical, and emergency content has specialist review.
- [ ] Imagery, maps, symbols, examples, colors, gestures, and cultural materials have consent, licensing, dignity, and community review.
- [ ] Privacy defaults are safe on shared devices and in high-risk contexts.
- [ ] Sensitive data collection has a documented purpose, retention policy, access control, and deletion process.
- [ ] Marketing consent is separate from essential communication.
- [ ] High-impact automation has thresholds, alternatives, explanations, appeals, and human review.
- [ ] Unsupported languages and weak model capabilities are restricted or clearly disclosed.
- [ ] Low-bandwidth, low-end-device, power-loss, and interrupted-session scenarios have been tested where relevant.
- [ ] Moderation supports dialect, transliteration, context, and escalation.
- [ ] Support-language coverage and response targets are defined and staffed.
- [ ] Launch blockers have been assessed and resolved or formally rejected.
- [ ] Owners, budgets, metrics, incident procedures, rollback authority, and review dates are assigned.
- [ ] A service-equivalence, remedy, and remediation plan exists.
- [ ] Known limitations are disclosed honestly.

## Guiding standard

**Standardize safety, accessibility, privacy, transparency, data minimization, user control, account recovery, and remedy globally. Configure language, formats, content, legal workflows, payments, imagery, moderation, support, and risk controls only after validating them with the people who will use and be affected by them.**

Global inclusion is an ongoing operating responsibility. The product should respect individual choice, reflect real variation, avoid stereotypes and harmful disclosure, provide equitable essential service, and remain accountable when it fails.
