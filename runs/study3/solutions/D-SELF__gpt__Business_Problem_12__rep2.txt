# Sustainable International Tourism Development Plan

## Executive decision

The region will pursue **managed, rights-based, conservation-led tourism**. It will attract international visitors through protected landscapes, historical sites, living heritage, local food, and community-approved experiences—not through unrestricted visitor growth.

Tourism expansion, new accommodation, infrastructure investment, and major marketing will proceed only when:

- Critical ecosystems and heritage assets are protected.
- Indigenous, customary, and community rights are respected.
- Residents retain housing, livelihoods, public access, and essential services.
- Water, wastewater, waste, transport, health, safety, and emergency systems can accommodate demand.
- Tourism businesses meet environmental, cultural, labor, accessibility, safety, and consumer standards.
- Local communities receive transparent and meaningful benefits.
- Tourism emissions and resource use remain within approved limits.
- Monitoring and enforcement are funded and operational.

Failure to meet an arrival or spending target will not constitute policy failure if environmental, cultural, social, or infrastructure safeguards are being maintained.

> **Binding growth rule:** Tourism growth may proceed only when approved thresholds remain within limits and responsible authorities have the legal power, resources, and capacity to enforce them.

---

## 1. Policy hierarchy and principles

Tourism decisions will follow this hierarchy:

1. Protection of critical ecosystems, cultural rights, heritage integrity, resident safety, essential water, and public access.
2. Adequate infrastructure, governance, monitoring, and enforcement capacity.
3. Resident well-being, decent work, local ownership, and equitable benefit sharing.
4. Visitor quality, safety, accessibility, and satisfaction.
5. Tourism growth, only where the preceding conditions are met.

### Guiding principles

The government will:

- Apply conservation before promotion.
- Use precaution where information is incomplete or harm may be irreversible.
- Respect Indigenous, customary, community, labor, and human rights.
- Apply free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) where applicable.
- Treat culture as living heritage, not an unrestricted commercial product.
- Prioritize locally retained value over visitor volume.
- Protect public and customary access to land, water, heritage, and cultural places.
- Require evidence for environmental, carbon, cultural, accessibility, and community-benefit claims.
- Use adaptive management, including the ability to reduce, pause, or reverse tourism growth.
- Publish key contracts, revenues, impacts, threshold breaches, and enforcement actions.
- Maintain tourism as one component of a diversified regional economy.

---

## 2. Planning assumptions and conditions for proceeding

### Provisional assumptions

The plan assumes that the region:

1. Contains protected ecosystems, important habitats, historical or archaeological sites, and living cultural traditions.
2. Has, or could obtain, international access through an airport, port, road, rail connection, or neighboring destination.
3. Has underdeveloped, concentrated, or unevenly distributed tourism.
4. Has incomplete data on biodiversity, heritage, tourism impacts, housing, infrastructure, and local economic benefits.
5. Has relevant government mandates but may need stronger coordination, staffing, funding, and enforcement.
6. Faces climate risks such as drought, heat, flooding, wildfire, erosion, storms, or ecosystem change.

These assumptions must be tested through the baseline and feasibility program.

### Minimum conditions

Promotion or capacity expansion will not proceed where the region cannot establish:

- Legally designated no-go and restricted areas.
- Reliable environmental, heritage, social, infrastructure, and market data.
- A credible community participation and consent process.
- Funded monitoring, enforcement, maintenance, restoration, and grievance systems.
- Adequate water, wastewater, waste, transport, health, safety, and emergency services.
- Housing and anti-displacement safeguards.
- Licensed operators meeting minimum standards.
- A legally governed conservation and community-benefit fund.
- A binding system to pause, reduce, close, restore, and reopen tourism activities.

## 4. Baseline and feasibility program

A coordinated assessment will be completed during the first 12–18 months.

### 4.1 Natural environment and climate

Assess and map:

- Critical and irreplaceable habitats.
- Ecological corridors and protected areas.
- Wetlands, forests, coasts, rivers, mountains, caves, and other sensitive ecosystems.
- Threatened, endemic, migratory, and disturbance-sensitive species.
- Water availability, quality, ecological flows, drought reserves, and competing uses.
- Wastewater, solid-waste, energy, noise, and pollution pressures.
- Flood, wildfire, heat, drought, erosion, storm, landslide, and sea-level-rise risks.
- Existing visitor impacts and cumulative impacts from proposed development.
- Tourism emissions from international transport, domestic transport, accommodation, food, activities, construction, and maintenance.

Critical or irreplaceable habitats, ecological corridors, sacred places, and areas where impacts cannot be effectively mitigated will be designated no-go or highly restricted areas.

### 4.2 Heritage and culture

Create a participatory inventory covering:

- Archaeological sites and monuments.
- Historic buildings, towns, routes, and landscapes.
- Museums, archives, and community collections.
- Sacred and culturally restricted places.
- Traditional architecture and repair skills.
- Foodways, crafts, music, dance, oral history, and other intangible heritage.
- Ownership, custodianship, legal status, condition, vulnerability, and acceptable visitor access.
- Practices that communities do not wish to commercialize, record, or disclose.

Sensitive ecological, archaeological, and sacred locations will not be publicly mapped without authorization.

### 4.3 Community and social conditions

Assess:

- Resident support, opposition, and perceived tourism impacts.
- Housing supply, rents, evictions, land prices, and short-term rentals.
- Customary land and resource use.
- Public access to beaches, forests, rivers, trails, heritage sites, and public services.
- Employment, wages, labor conditions, seasonal work, and worker housing.
- Effects on women, youth, low-income households, migrants, Indigenous peoples, customary-rights holders, and persons with disabilities.
- Local business ownership, procurement capacity, tourism leakage, and distribution of benefits.

### 4.4 Visitor, market, infrastructure, and financial conditions

Establish:

- Arrivals, overnight stays, day visitors, length of stay, expenditure, and seasonality.
- Visitor flows by site, hour, group size, purpose, and transport mode.
- Accommodation, guiding, transport, healthcare, safety, and emergency capacity.
- Airport, port, road, rail, visa, border, and last-mile access.
- Visitor satisfaction, accessibility barriers, safety concerns, and cultural impacts.
- Market size, marketing acquisition cost, tour-operator interest, competitors, and transport emissions.
- Capital, operating, maintenance, monitoring, enforcement, and fiscal costs.

Three scenarios will be prepared:

| Scenario | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Conservative | Tests resilience under limited promotion and slower demand |
| Managed growth | Preferred planning case, subject to approved thresholds |
| High growth | Stress test only; not an automatic target |

Each scenario must estimate visitor numbers, nights, expenditure, employment, water demand, waste, emissions, housing effects, infrastructure needs, public costs, and site pressure.

## 5. Priority markets and destination proposition

### 5.1 Priority visitor segments

The initial strategy should focus on:

- Nature and wildlife travelers.
- Heritage and cultural travelers.
- Soft-adventure travelers.
- Slow and experiential travelers.
- Educational, scientific, conservation, and heritage groups.
- Diaspora and ancestral travelers.
- Carefully managed premium travelers.

No segment is inherently sustainable. Market selection will use **net destination value**:

> Local value retained minus public service costs, environmental impacts, emissions, housing pressure, and cultural or social costs.

Premium products will be supported only where they meet strict requirements for water, land use, labor, public access, biodiversity, and community benefits.

### 5.2 Initial source markets

Following feasibility studies, the agency should select no more than three initial markets:

1. A nearby or regionally connected market with lower transport impacts and repeat-visit potential.
2. A culturally, historically, or diaspora-connected market.
3. A long-haul specialist market with interest in nature or heritage and a propensity for longer stays.

Selection criteria will include:

- Connectivity and visa requirements.
- Average length of stay and expenditure.
- Seasonality.
- Marketing acquisition cost.
- Tour-operator interest.
- Visitor behavior.
- Infrastructure and water demand.
- Transport emissions.
- Competitive positioning.
- Resilience to economic, political, health, and climate shocks.

### 5.3 Destination proposition

The destination should be positioned as:

> **A carefully protected region where remarkable nature, living heritage, and local communities can be experienced responsibly and at an unhurried pace.**

The brand will emphasize:

- Protected landscapes and biodiversity.
- Community-approved cultural experiences.
- Historic places and credible interpretation.
- Local food, crafts, and hospitality.
- Longer stays and multi-site itineraries.
- Small-group and lower-impact travel.
- Reliable safety, accessibility, and visitor information.

The agency will not use terms such as “untouched,” “pristine,” “exotic,” “authentic,” “eco-friendly,” or “carbon neutral” unless each claim is precisely defined, evidenced, and independently verified.

## 6. Spatial planning and tourism products

The region will be managed as a network of complementary destinations.

### 6.1 Tourism zones

#### Strict conservation zones

- No major new tourism construction.
- Limited or guided access.
- Seasonal closures where required.
- Protection of critical habitat, breeding areas, ecological corridors, and water resources.
- No biodiversity offsets for damage to irreplaceable ecosystems.

#### Heritage conservation zones

- Controlled visitor flows.
- Conservation-led restoration.
- Protection of resident access and historic character.
- Controls on events, signage, photography, adaptive reuse, and commercial activity.

#### Community tourism zones

- Community-approved accommodation, guiding, food, craft, and landscape experiences.
- Local or community ownership preferred.
- Visitor numbers tied to water, housing, cultural, ecological, and service capacity.
- No promotion without documented community agreement and an approved management plan.

#### Gateway and service zones

- Concentrated accommodation, transport, healthcare, waste, wastewater, retail, and visitor services.
- Development subject to water, housing, climate, fiscal, and public-access controls.
- Infrastructure must serve residents as well as visitors.

Dispersal will not simply transfer overcrowding to less-prepared communities. Secondary destinations may be promoted only after passing the same readiness tests as established sites.

### 6.2 Priority tourism products

The agency will support:

- Protected landscape and wildlife journeys.
- Linked heritage and archaeological routes.
- Community-approved food, craft, farming, fishing, and storytelling experiences.
- Community-owned homestays and guesthouses.
- Low-carbon scenic itineraries.
- Supervised conservation and heritage-learning activities.
- Shoulder-season cultural, educational, and wellness itineraries.

Products should be small-group, longer-stay, locally beneficial, and compatible with site-specific limits.

## 7. Environmental, climate, and visitor-management safeguards

### 7.1 Site-specific limits of acceptable change

Every priority natural, heritage, and community destination will have an approved management plan containing:

| Element | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Baseline | Current ecological, heritage, social, and infrastructure condition |
| Desired condition | Condition to maintain or restore |
| Indicators | Pressure, condition, and social indicators |
| Warning threshold | Trigger for corrective action |
| Critical threshold | Trigger for access reduction, closure, or restoration |
| Monitoring | Method, frequency, and quality controls |
| Authority | Institution with legal enforcement power |
| Funding | Operating, monitoring, enforcement, and recovery budgets |
| Response | Pre-approved management action |
| Reopening | Evidence required before access resumes |

Indicators may include:

- Peak and daily visitor numbers.
- Trail erosion and vegetation loss.
- Wildlife disturbance and breeding disruption.
- Water availability and ecological flows.
- Wastewater compliance and litter.
- Noise and traffic.
- Heritage deterioration.
- Resident crowding and access.
- Housing pressure.
- Emergency capacity.
- Visitor safety and accessibility.

Missing or unreliable data will trigger precautionary restrictions, not permission to expand.

### 7.2 Traffic-light controls

- **Green:** Conditions are within limits; targeted growth may proceed.
- **Amber:** A warning threshold is exceeded; corrective action is mandatory and new promotion or capacity increases require review.
- **Red:** A critical threshold is exceeded; access, new development, and marketing are reduced or paused until recovery.

Responses may include:

- Reservations and timed entry.
- Group-size and vehicle limits.
- Seasonal closures.
- Mandatory guides.
- Differential pricing.
- Shuttle systems.
- Rerouting.
- Event restrictions.
- Short-term-rental controls.
- License or concession suspension.
- Restoration.
- Full or partial site closure.

At red status, the responsible authority must publish the reason, affected activities, restrictions, expected duration, recovery actions, reopening criteria, and available support for affected workers and small businesses where feasible.

### 7.3 Development assessment

Major tourism plans and projects must undergo, as applicable:

- Strategic environmental assessment.
- Environmental and social impact assessment.
- Biodiversity assessment.
- Heritage-impact assessment.
- Climate-risk screening.
- Cumulative-impact assessment.
- Housing and displacement assessment.
- Public consultation.
- Indigenous or customary consent processes.
- Review of no-build and lower-impact alternatives.

No tourism development will be approved where impacts on critical habitat, sacred places, public access, or heritage integrity cannot be avoided or effectively mitigated.

### 7.4 Water, waste, and energy

Seasonal destination water budgets will protect:

1. Resident household needs.
2. Ecological flows.
3. Existing farming, fishing, gathering, and other livelihoods.
4. Drought and climate reserves.
5. Tourism demand.

No accommodation expansion will be approved where projected tourism demand would compromise essential resident, livelihood, or ecosystem needs.

Licensed businesses must, where feasible:

- Meter water use.
- Maintain drought plans.
- Reduce leaks.
- Report water use per guest night.
- Reuse water safely.
- Restrict pools and water-intensive landscaping in stressed areas.

Minimum standards will cover wastewater treatment, solid-waste management, waste reduction, energy efficiency, renewable energy where feasible, single-use plastics, lighting, noise, pollution prevention, and sustainable construction materials.

### 7.5 Climate resilience and emissions

Tourism infrastructure must assess:

- Future hazard exposure.
- Design life and climate assumptions.
- Maintenance costs.
- Insurance and insurability.
- Evacuation and emergency access.
- Business continuity.
- Residual risk.
- Relocation, decommissioning, or abandonment options.
- Recovery financing.

Nature-based adaptation will be prioritized where appropriate. Projects must not transfer flood, fire, erosion, or water risks to neighboring communities.

Emissions will be reported separately for:

- International transport.
- Domestic and local transport.
- Accommodation.
- Food and goods.
- Activities and attractions.
- Construction and maintenance.

Emissions will also be reported per visitor, per visitor night, per complete trip, by market, by transport mode, and for the destination as a whole. Longer stays must not be used to conceal rising total emissions.

## 8. Cultural heritage and rights

### 8.1 Conservation-led heritage management

Tourism revenue will support:

- Funded conservation plans.
- Routine condition monitoring.
- Fire, flood, earthquake, and climate protection.
- Professional site management.
- Traditional repair skills.
- Community museums and archives.
- Cultural practitioners and apprenticeships.
- Visitor facilities that reduce physical wear.

Conservation will take precedence over events, commercial activity, visitor access, or adaptive reuse where necessary.

### 8.2 Cultural authority and protocols

Communities and legitimate cultural custodians will determine:

- What may be presented publicly.
- Whether practices are public, restricted, or sacred.
- Appropriate visitor conduct and dress.
- Photography, filming, recording, and drone rules.
- Use of stories, songs, designs, images, food knowledge, and traditional knowledge.
- Frequency and timing of performances.
- Prices, licensing, compensation, and benefit sharing.
- Circumstances in which an experience may be withdrawn.

Every cultural tourism product will have a written protocol covering custodianship, consent, withdrawal rights, intellectual property, recording, visitor conduct, compensation, confidentiality, and dispute resolution.

Cultural content must not portray communities as primitive, exotic, frozen in time, or attractive because of poverty or isolation. Relevant histories of colonization, displacement, conflict, and exclusion must not be erased or romanticized.

### 8.3 Indigenous and customary rights

Where tourism affects Indigenous or customary lands, waters, resources, heritage, cultural practices, or traditional knowledge, applicable FPIC procedures will be followed.

Processes must be:

- Completed before approvals, contracts, or land transfers.
- Free from coercion, retaliation, and conflicts of interest.
- Conducted in relevant languages and accessible formats.
- Supported by independent legal and technical advice.
- Inclusive of women, youth, elders, people with disabilities, and less powerful households.
- Documented and reviewable.
- Capable of resulting in refusal, modification, renegotiation, or withdrawal of support.

Consent must be revisited where a project changes materially in scale, location, ownership, environmental impact, or cultural use.

## 9. Community participation, local benefits, and housing

### 9.1 Meaningful participation

Regional and local tourism bodies will include:

- Residents and customary-rights holders.
- Indigenous peoples where applicable.
- Cultural custodians and practitioners.
- Workers and small businesses.
- Women, youth, elders, and people with disabilities.
- Municipal, heritage, conservation, and civil-society representatives.
- People who oppose tourism or prefer not to participate publicly.

Representation rules, selection methods, voting rights, conflicts of interest, meeting records, and rotation procedures will be published.

Participation will occur before decisions, approvals, contracts, or land transfers through:

- Early consultation.
- Community mapping.
- Public hearings.
- Accessible information in relevant languages.
- Participatory budgeting.
- Community involvement in monitoring.
- Independent grievance and appeal mechanisms.

A tourism council must not automatically be treated as representing every affected group.

### 9.2 Local enterprises and employment

Support will include:

- Community-owned accommodation and guiding.
- Cooperative transport, food, and craft enterprises.
- Community museums and cultural centers.
- Conservation and heritage trusts.
- Joint ventures in which communities retain equity and decision rights.
- Working capital, insurance, mentoring, licensing support, digital booking, and market access.

Tourism licenses and concessions will require:

- Written contracts.
- Fair and timely wages.
- Safe working conditions.
- Reasonable working hours.
- Protection from harassment and discrimination.
- Safe worker accommodation where needed.
- Paid training and career progression.
- Accessible grievance channels.
- Protection for seasonal, migrant, informal, and contract workers.

### 9.3 Local procurement and leakage

Procurement targets will be sector-specific and based on verified supply capacity. Monitoring will cover:

- Food and beverage sourcing.
- Local guiding and transport.
- Crafts and maintenance.
- Construction.
- Specialist goods and equipment.
- Foreign ownership.
- External booking commissions.
- Profits retained or repatriated.
- Taxes and value retained locally.

Procurement must not cause overharvesting, water stress, food inflation, unsustainable extraction, or false local-content claims.

### 9.4 Housing, land, and public access

The government will monitor and regulate:

- Short-term rentals in housing-stressed areas.
- Conversion of primary residences.
- Land speculation.
- Tourism-related evictions.
- Beneficial ownership of tourism land and businesses.
- Worker and resident housing.
- Public and customary access to beaches, forests, rivers, trails, and heritage sites.

Measures may include short-term-rental registration and caps, primary-residence protections, community land trusts, cooperative ownership, anti-speculation measures, and worker-housing requirements for major developments.

Projects causing unacceptable displacement, access loss, or livelihood damage will be modified, rejected, or required to provide enforceable remedies.

### 9.5 Conservation and community-benefit fund

A legally established fund will receive defined revenues from:

- Park and heritage entry fees.
- Accommodation or visitor levies.
- Concessions and licenses.
- Parking and visitor transport charges.
- Voluntary conservation contributions.
- Eligible external climate, biodiversity, and heritage finance.

The fund’s legal instrument will define:

- Eligible communities, including indirectly affected communities.
- Allocation formula.
- Eligible uses.
- Direct support for cultural practitioners where appropriate.
- Payment timelines.
- Independent audits.
- Public disclosure.
- Conflict-of-interest rules.
- Complaint and appeal procedures.

A provisional planning assumption is that at least **20% of eligible site and visitor revenues** will support communities and site protection. This percentage must be validated through fiscal and legal analysis before adoption.

Resident and domestic access will be protected through free or discounted entry arrangements where fees could cause exclusion.

## 10. Infrastructure, mobility, and accessibility

### 10.1 Essential services first

Before major promotion, the government will prioritize:

- Potable water.
- Wastewater treatment.
- Solid-waste collection and disposal.
- Reliable electricity.
- Broadband and mobile connectivity.
- Medical and emergency services.
- Public toilets.
- Safe roads, trails, and signage.
- Border, visa, and visitor information systems.

### 10.2 Sustainable mobility

The destination mobility plan will include, where feasible:

- Public transport from gateways.
- Electric or low-emission shuttles.
- Park-and-ride facilities.
- Safe walking and cycling routes.
- Traffic controls near heritage sites.
- Coach and event-arrival management.
- Integrated route and ticket information.
- Services that improve resident mobility as well as visitor access.

Transport investments must not create unacceptable ecosystem fragmentation or displace resident mobility priorities.

### 10.3 Universal accessibility

A Universal Access and Inclusive Tourism Standard will cover:

- Step-free access where feasible.
- Accessible trails, viewpoints, entrances, and toilets.
- Accessible transport and accommodation information.
- Audio, tactile, captioned, and sign-language interpretation where appropriate.
- Plain-language, high-contrast, and digitally accessible information.
- Assistance animals.
- Sensory-considerate options.
- Accessible emergency evacuation.
- Staff and guide training.
- Accurate disclosure of route surfaces, gradients, facilities, assistance, and limitations.

Accessibility audits and costed improvement schedules will be completed for pilot destinations. Where physical access would harm heritage or ecosystems, equivalent alternatives will be provided, such as accessible viewpoints, replicas, digital interpretation, assisted access, or approved remote experiences.

Accessibility claims will be independently checked.

## 11. Responsible visitors and ethical marketing

### 11.1 Visitor code

A multilingual and accessible visitor code will be provided during booking, on arrival, at accommodation, and at sites. Visitors will be expected to:

- Respect residents, custodians, and local authorities.
- Obtain consent before photographing people.
- Follow cultural dress and conduct rules.
- Avoid sacred, residential, ceremonial, and restricted areas.
- Keep a safe distance from wildlife.
- Avoid feeding, touching, chasing, or harassing animals.
- Avoid littering, fires, unauthorized drones, and off-road driving.
- Purchase only legal cultural and wildlife products.
- Conserve water and energy.
- Use licensed operators and legal accommodation.
- Follow safety and emergency procedures.
- Report harassment, exploitation, environmental damage, or unsafe conditions.

Violations will carry proportionate consequences under site and licensing rules.

### 11.2 Marketing readiness and compliance

Marketing will be linked to a destination-readiness score covering:

- Site-management capacity.
- Water, wastewater, and waste systems.
- Transport and emergency services.
- Housing and resident impacts.
- Community participation and consent.
- Cultural protocols.
- Accessibility.
- Licensed operator capacity.
- Monitoring and enforcement.

Official campaigns and marketing partners must:

- Maintain evidence files for environmental, carbon, cultural, accessibility, and community-benefit claims.
- Obtain consent for community images, narratives, and cultural materials.
- Avoid sensitive ecological, archaeological, and sacred-site locations.
- State seasonal restrictions, visitor limits, access conditions, and site limitations.
- Follow the visitor code.
- Remove misleading or harmful content.
- Accept sanctions for repeated violations.

Marketing partners, influencers, platforms, and tour operators will be subject to contractual requirements, takedown procedures, corrective statements, and license or partnership sanctions where appropriate.

## 12. Governance and accountability

A legally supported **Destination Management Unit** will coordinate tourism, environment, heritage, transport, water, housing, labor, finance, emergency management, and local government. It will not override the statutory authority of environmental, heritage, Indigenous, customary, or municipal institutions.

### Core responsibilities

The unit will coordinate:

- Spatial planning and zoning.
- Visitor limits, closures, restoration, and recovery.
- Marketing and market partnerships.
- Licensing and enforcement.
- Data and public reporting.
- Investment screening.
- Participation and consent processes.
- Revenue collection and benefit sharing.
- Climate, emergency, and post-disaster management.

### Accountability safeguards

The government will establish:

- Transparent procurement and concessions.
- Beneficial-ownership disclosure.
- Conflict-of-interest declarations.
- Independent technical review.
- Independent audits.
- Whistleblower protection.
- An independent grievance and ombudsperson mechanism.
- Public reporting of contracts, fund allocations, threshold breaches, closures, and enforcement actions.
- Community participation in monitoring.

Every major action will identify:

- Lead institution.
- Supporting institutions.
- Legal basis.
- Capital and operating budget.
- Procurement route.
- Delivery date.
- Indicator.
- Risk.
- Escalation procedure.
- Public reporting date.

## 13. Financing and investment controls

A five- and ten-year financial model will cover:

- Capital expenditure.
- Operating and maintenance costs.
- Monitoring and enforcement.
- Accessibility improvements.
- Community participation and consent.
- Benefit sharing.
- Digital systems.
- Revenue collection.
- Disaster recovery.
- Closure and restoration.
- Worker and small-business support.
- Public subsidies.
- Debt, guarantees, and contingent liabilities.

### Funding hierarchy

1. **Core public funding:** rights protection, conservation, heritage, safety, utilities, and essential services.
2. **Earmarked tourism revenues:** site management, maintenance, visitor services, conservation, and community benefits.
3. **External finance:** climate, biodiversity, heritage, research, and development funding.
4. **Private capital:** only for projects passing environmental, social, fiscal, labor, accessibility, public-access, and community-benefit tests.

No visitor facility will be approved without funded operations, maintenance, monitoring, enforcement, and eventual decommissioning or restoration.

Public-private partnerships must include:

- Value-for-money assessment.
- Transparent procurement.
- Demand- and climate-risk allocation.
- Public-access protections.
- Beneficial-ownership disclosure.
- Fiscal-risk controls.
- Termination provisions for nonperformance.

A conservation and site-maintenance reserve will be established so essential protection is not wholly dependent on visitor revenue.

## 14. Monitoring and reporting

A public annual dashboard will distinguish among condition, pressure, and response indicators.

### Condition indicators

- Habitat and ecosystem condition.
- Indicator-species trends.
- Water quality and ecological flows.
- Heritage condition.
- Cultural integrity and practitioner control.
- Resident well-being and support.
- Housing affordability.
- Accessibility outcomes.

### Pressure indicators

- Arrivals, overnight stays, and day visitors.
- Peak-site attendance.
- Water and energy use.
- Waste and wastewater.
- Emissions by source, market, and transport mode.
- Traffic and noise.
- Housing conversion.
- Wildlife disturbance.
- Heritage wear.
- Local procurement and external leakage.

### Response indicators

- Closures and closure duration.
- Enforcement actions and compliance.
- Conservation expenditure.
- Complaints received and resolved.
- Community payments made.
- Accessibility improvements completed.
- Restoration progress.
- Marketing changes.
- Businesses receiving closure or transition support.

For every core indicator, the agency will specify:

- Definition and unit.
- Baseline.
- Threshold or target.
- Data source.
- Sampling or calculation method.
- Collection frequency.
- Responsible institution.
- Verification method.
- Public reporting date.
- Corrective action.

Data will be geographically disaggregated and, where lawful and ethical, reported by gender, age, disability, income, community, visitor type, and transport mode. An independent auditor, university, statistical authority, or accredited technical body will review data quality.

Missing, delayed, or unreliable data will trigger precautionary restrictions.

### Provisional five-year targets

These targets must be validated after baseline and feasibility work:

- At least 50% regional procurement where supply exists and sourcing is sustainable.
- At least 30% of priority businesses meeting independently verified sustainability standards.
- Reduced water, energy, waste, and emissions intensity per visitor night.
- No net loss of critical habitat attributable to tourism.
- Improved condition of priority heritage assets.
- At least 20% of eligible site and visitor revenues directed to communities and site protection.
- Improved resident support compared with the baseline.
- Increased average length of stay without exceeding site limits.
- All priority sites operating with approved threshold-and-response plans.
- All licensed operators meeting minimum safety, labor, environmental, cultural, accessibility, and consumer standards.

## 15. Implementation roadmap

### Gate 1: Protection and diagnosis — 0–6 months

| Action | Lead responsibility |
|---|---|
| Approve the binding growth rule and policy hierarchy | Cabinet or responsible minister |
| Establish the Destination Management Unit and interim council | Tourism agency and central government |
| Identify rights holders, cultural custodians, workers, and vulnerable groups | Local government and community liaison bodies |
| Identify provisional no-go and restricted areas | Environment and heritage authorities |
| Restrict development where information is inadequate | Planning and permitting authorities |
| Select two or three potential pilot destinations | Destination Management Unit and local authorities |
| Begin rapid biodiversity, heritage, water, waste, housing, accessibility, and infrastructure assessments | Relevant technical agencies |
| Establish the independent grievance and ombudsperson mechanism | Justice or oversight authority |
| Begin legal design of the tourism levy and benefit-sharing fund | Finance, tourism, and justice authorities |
| Complete the institutional capacity audit | Public administration authority and independent experts |

### Gate 2: Readiness and detailed planning — 6–18 months

- Complete baseline, market, demand, infrastructure, housing, and financial studies.
- Approve zoning and site-management plans.
- Set site-specific thresholds and legal response powers.
- Establish water budgets and climate-risk profiles.
- Complete consent and cultural-protocol processes.
- Adopt licensing standards for environment, labor, safety, accessibility, culture, and consumer protection.
- Establish the benefit-sharing fund.
- Complete accessibility audits and costed improvement plans.
- Prepare infrastructure investment and maintenance plans.
- Test limited marketing in no more than three markets.

### Gate 3: Pilot implementation — years 1–3

- Introduce reservations, timed entry, group limits, and closures where required.
- Upgrade water, wastewater, waste, transport, safety, and accessibility systems.
- Launch community-owned and community-approved products.
- Implement conservation and heritage revenue sharing.
- Train and license guides and operators.
- Pilot low-carbon mobility.
- Operate the official multilingual visitor platform.
- Publish monitoring, financial, and benefit-sharing results.
- Independently evaluate ecological, cultural, social, financial, and visitor outcomes.
- Continue, modify, pause, or terminate pilots based on evidence.

### Gate 4: Managed expansion — years 3–5

Expansion requires independent confirmation that:

- Environmental, heritage, and social thresholds remain within limits.
- Infrastructure is functioning and funded.
- Community participation and consent requirements are satisfied.
- Housing and public-access risks are controlled.
- Local benefits are being delivered.
- Cultural protocols are respected.
- Accessibility information is accurate.
- Monitoring and enforcement are reliable.
- Marketing claims are substantiated.

If the gate is not passed, the response will be safeguard improvement or non-expansion—not increased promotion.

### Long-term consolidation — years 5–10

- Review markets, fees, quotas, zoning, and visitor products.
- Restore or close sites where thresholds have been exceeded.
- Update climate and infrastructure plans.
- Strengthen conservation and maintenance funds.
- Review housing, labor, ownership, and benefit distribution.
- Reassess whether additional tourism growth is justified.
- Maintain tourism as one component of a diversified regional economy.

## 16. Key risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Marketing outpaces capacity | Stage campaigns according to readiness; pause promotion when thresholds are exceeded |
| Crowding at iconic sites | Reservations, timed entry, quotas, pricing, seasonal controls, and rerouting |
| Dispersal transfers impacts | Require receiving-community agreement, minimum services, housing safeguards, and monitoring |
| Water scarcity | Water budgets, metering, reuse, drought restrictions, and no expansion where essential needs are threatened |
| Biodiversity loss | No-go areas, cumulative assessment, species-specific rules, seasonal closures, and independent monitoring |
| Heritage deterioration | Site limits, conservation plans, controlled access, and maintenance funding |
| Cultural commodification | Custodian authority, consent, cultural protocols, compensation, and withdrawal rights |
| Rights or tenure conflict | FPIC where applicable, independent advice, tenure mapping, and access protection |
| Housing displacement | Short-term-rental controls, housing safeguards, worker housing, and community ownership |
| Unequal benefits or elite capture | Statutory revenue sharing, audits, transparent allocations, and participatory budgeting |
| Economic leakage | Local procurement, community equity, ownership monitoring, and disclosure of external commissions |
| Greenwashing | Defined claims, minimum legal standards, independent verification, and public reporting |
| Poor labor conditions | Licensing requirements, inspections, worker grievance systems, and wage monitoring |
| Climate disasters | Climate-screened design, early warnings, emergency plans, insurance, and recovery finance |
| High transport emissions | Longer-stay and regional markets, surface transport, and transparent emissions accounting |
| Weak government capacity | Capacity audit, phased pilots, training, external technical support, and non-expansion where necessary |
| Political or investor pressure | Transparent decisions, conflict-of-interest rules, independent review, and binding thresholds |
| Tourism dependence | Market diversification, emergency planning, reserves, and a broader regional economic base |

## Final policy rule

The government should adopt the following as binding policy:

> Tourism growth may proceed only when environmental condition, heritage integrity, resident well-being, Indigenous and customary rights, community consent where applicable, infrastructure readiness, accessibility, labor standards, housing stability, and local economic retention remain within approved thresholds. When thresholds are exceeded, the government will modify, reduce, redirect, or pause tourism activity, restrict new capacity, and fund recovery before permitting further growth.

This plan provides a credible framework for attracting international visitors while protecting the natural, cultural, and social assets on which the region’s long-term prosperity depends.
