# Five-Warehouse Automation and Workforce-Transition Roadmap

## 1. Executive summary

The company should pursue a **phased, evidence-based automation program**, not a simultaneous network-wide conversion or “lights-out” strategy.

The immediate authorization should cover:

- A 60-day network diagnostic and baseline.
- Low-risk digital and process improvements.
- Paid workforce-transition preparation.
- Competitive vendor diligence.
- One bounded, reversible pilot.

Major fixed automation, full-site conversions, and automation-related involuntary workforce reductions should remain deferred until the company has validated site economics, bottleneck capacity, technical readiness, service protections, workforce plans, and legal requirements.

### Recommended sequence

1. **Warehouse 4:** initial pilot, subject to readiness confirmation.
2. **Warehouse 2:** first major scale deployment if the pilot and site economics support it.
3. **Warehouse 3:** standardized replication of proven solutions.
4. **Warehouse 1:** phased modernization after network proof, with the strongest workforce and community safeguards.
5. **Warehouse 5:** selective automation only where utilization and returns are demonstrated.

### Planning targets, subject to validation

- 20–30% reduction in direct labor hours per order in suitable workflows.
- 30–50% reduction in picking errors.
- 10–20% improvement in effective productivity.
- No sustained deterioration in customer or key-account service.
- Maximum three-year simple payback for fixed automation, including implementation and transition costs.
- Positive base-case and risk-adjusted NPV under company-approved thresholds.
- Involuntary reductions only after redeployment, retraining, attrition, hiring controls, voluntary programs, and other reasonable alternatives are exhausted.

The program’s objective is not simply to remove labor hours. It is to create a safer, more reliable, lower-error network with verified cash returns and a credible transition for affected employees.

---

# 2. Guiding principles

1. **Automate workflows before eliminating jobs.**
2. **Scale only after actual performance validates vendor and internal assumptions.**
3. **Protect service through zone-by-zone conversion and tested manual fallback.**
4. **Count realized cash savings separately from productivity, capacity, redeployment, and strategic benefits.**
5. **Treat safety, legal compliance, workforce readiness, and customer service as hard go/no-go criteria.**
6. **Release capital in tranches rather than committing the full network budget upfront.**
7. **Use modular, reversible solutions where demand, SKU mix, or technology performance is uncertain.**
8. **Give employees a genuine, paid opportunity to qualify for new roles.**
9. **Apply common governance standards while tailoring automation to each site.**
10. **Earn the right to scale through evidence.**

---

# 3. Assumptions and required validation

The following are planning assumptions, not established facts:

- Warehouse 1 is the largest site and the principal employer in its town.
- Warehouse 2 may have the strongest economics for high-utilization automation.
- Warehouse 3 may be suitable for standardized modular solutions.
- Warehouse 4 may be the most suitable pilot site.
- Warehouse 5 may have lower volume or greater operational variability.
- Picker turnover averages approximately 35% annually but varies by site, role, shift, and tenure.
- Capital is available for staged investment but not simultaneous conversion of all five sites.
- Existing WMS, facilities, networks, and maintenance capabilities can support incremental automation, subject to assessment.

Within the first 60 days, validate:

- Orders, lines, units, cube, weight, SKU mix, and seasonality by site.
- 90th- and 95th-percentile hourly and daily demand.
- Labor hours, wages, overtime, agency usage, absenteeism, and turnover.
- Error, damage, rework, claim, and customer-complaint rates.
- Bottlenecks from receiving through carrier handoff.
- WMS/WCS, data, network, power, floor, fire, and building readiness.
- Lease term, remaining facility life, and physical flexibility.
- Workforce skills, job classifications, representation, and transfer opportunities.
- Customer-specific service obligations and carrier constraints.
- Technical, maintenance, cybersecurity, and vendor-support capacity.

No site designation becomes final until the evidence is reviewed by Operations, Finance, HR, Safety, IT, Facilities, Legal/Labor Relations, and the site leader.

# 4. Site-by-site automation sequence

## Warehouse 1 — largest site and principal community employer

### Decision

Begin preparation immediately, but defer major fixed automation until the pilot and first scale deployment demonstrate reliable operating and financial results.

### Initial automation scope

- WMS data-quality remediation.
- Task interleaving and wave-planning improvements.
- RF or voice-directed picking.
- Scan verification.
- Dynamic slotting.
- Dimensioning, cartonization, labeling, and shipping verification.
- Selective conveyance.
- AMRs for travel-intensive movement where utilization is demonstrated.
- A contained goods-to-person module only after validation elsewhere.

### Explicit limits

- No full-site or “lights-out” conversion.
- No major irreversible system before network evidence.
- No automation-related involuntary reductions during the pilot protection period.
- No first-time cutovers during peak season.
- No staffing below approved safe levels for maintenance, safety, quality, supervision, or exception handling.

### Required preparation

- Local automation-skills academy.
- Paid technical career pathways.
- Employee advisory group.
- Community and workforce advisory panel.
- 36-month workforce bridge.
- Local labor-market assessment.
- Zone-level conversion and rollback plan.
- Customer, carrier, and transportation continuity plan.

Warehouse 1 will be converted by process and zone. A manual alternative will remain available until each automated area passes its stabilization gate.

---

## Warehouse 2 — high-volume scale candidate

### Decision

Candidate for the first major deployment after pilot validation and site scoring.

### Suitable scope

- Goods-to-person or shuttle automation for high-volume, small-item SKUs.
- AMRs for replenishment and horizontal movement.
- Conveyance, put walls, and sortation.
- Vision and scan verification.
- Robotic palletizing where pallet patterns are stable.

Exclude oversized, irregular, low-volume, and highly variable products unless separately justified.

Approval requires evidence that automation relieves the actual site bottleneck, not merely that it increases picking speed.

---

## Warehouse 3 — standardized replication site

### Decision

Deploy a proven modular package after pilot benefits are independently validated.

### Suitable scope

- Voice or RF picking.
- Pick-to-light in high-frequency zones.
- Dynamic slotting.
- Put walls.
- Selective conveyance.
- Automated replenishment and cycle counting.
- AMRs where travel reduction and utilization support the case.

Use standardized technology unless a documented site constraint requires customization.

---

## Warehouse 4 — initial pilot candidate

### Decision

Use Warehouse 4 for the initial pilot only if it passes the readiness gate. If it does not, move the pilot to a controlled zone at Warehouse 2 or 3.

### Readiness requirements

- Stable, representative SKU and order mix.
- Meaningful but isolatable volume.
- Capable local leadership.
- Adequate WMS, network, power, floor, and safety readiness.
- No unacceptable risk to critical accounts.
- Manual fallback capacity.
- Comparable manual control zone.
- Completed workforce and labor-relations plan.
- Qualified technical and maintenance coverage.

### Pilot scope

- RF or voice-directed picking.
- Scan validation.
- Dynamic slotting.
- Pick-to-light in one zone.
- Bounded AMR or autonomous-cart workflow.
- Digital exception handling.
- Labor scheduling and workload balancing.

---

## Warehouse 5 — selective improvement site

### Decision

Do not authorize major fixed automation in the initial portfolio.

### Initial scope

- Scanning and verification.
- Slotting.
- WMS and process improvements.
- Ergonomic aids.
- Selective labeling, dimensioning, pallet wrapping, or mobile equipment.

Reconsider fixed automation only if utilization, bottleneck relief, flexibility, and economics meet the same approval standards applied elsewhere.

# 5. Capacity and service model

Automation will be sized to the bottleneck and peak requirement, not average volume.

\[
\text{Net Capacity}_{p,t}
=
\text{Design Rate}_{p}
\times
\text{Availability}_{p,t}
\times
\text{Performance Factor}_{p,t}
\times
\text{Quality Yield}_{p,t}
\]

Capacity must be modeled for:

- Receiving.
- Putaway.
- Replenishment.
- Picking.
- Packing.
- Sortation.
- Dock operations.
- Carrier handoff.
- Returns.
- Exceptions and rework.

The model must include:

- Average, 90th-percentile, and 95th-percentile demand.
- Seasonal and promotional peaks.
- Cutoff-time surges.
- Absenteeism.
- Charging constraints.
- Equipment downtime.
- SKU-mix changes.
- Queueing, starvation, and downstream blocking.
- Manual fallback capacity.
- Recovery after multi-hour and multi-day outages.
- Carrier pickup constraints.
- Cross-site diversion capacity.

Automation is approved only when it relieves a demonstrated bottleneck or avoids a documented future capacity requirement.

# 6. Phased timeline

## Phase 0 — Mobilize and baseline: Months 0–2

### Actions

- Appoint a COO-level executive sponsor.
- Establish an Automation and Workforce Office.
- Form site steering committees.
- Reconcile WMS, payroll, timekeeping, maintenance, quality, and finance data.
- Complete site scoring and bottleneck models.
- Produce a 36-month workforce bridge for each site.
- Establish a separately funded transition budget.
- Begin employee, labor, safety, and community consultation.
- Identify pilot and control zones.
- Issue competitive vendor requests.
- Complete preliminary legal, cyber, facilities, engineering, insurance, transportation, and customer-service reviews.

### Deliverables

- Five site decision packs.
- KPI dictionary and baseline lock date.
- Peak-capacity and bottleneck model.
- Pilot charter.
- Workforce bridge and transition budget.
- Technical staffing plan.
- Service-continuity and rollback plan.
- Jurisdiction-specific compliance matrix.
- Initial implementation critical path.

---

## Phase 1 — Foundational improvements and pilot: Months 2–9

### Network-wide actions

- Correct item, location, and barcode data.
- Improve WMS task interleaving and wave planning.
- Deploy RF or voice picking where appropriate.
- Add scan confirmation at pick and pack.
- Improve slotting and replenishment triggers.
- Establish standard work.
- Launch paid cross-training.
- Train supervisors in mixed manual/automated operations.
- Establish operational, financial, workforce, and safety dashboards.

### Pilot controls

- One process, zone, or shift only.
- Comparable manual control zone.
- Defined SKU and order profile.
- Gradual volume ramp.
- Separate ramp-up and stabilized periods.
- Stress or peak test before scale approval.
- Manual fallback retained throughout evaluation.
- Temporary labor and cross-trained employees available for recovery.
- Formal WMS/WCS integration and user-acceptance testing.
- Daily command-center oversight during stabilization.

### Pilot protection period

During the pilot and initial evaluation period:

- No involuntary reductions directly attributed to the pilot.
- No unsafe work intensification to meet productivity targets.
- No reduction in required maintenance, safety, quality, or supervision.
- Technical and safety hiring allowed through documented exceptions to general hiring controls.

---

## Phase 2 — Scale proven solutions: Months 9–18

### Warehouse 2

Deploy validated solutions in the highest-volume, most repetitive SKU segments. Refresh the business case using actual pilot results for uptime, throughput, errors, maintenance, training, and realized cash savings.

### Warehouse 3

Deploy the standard modular package only after independent Finance and Operations validation.

### Warehouse 1

Complete infrastructure preparation, data cleansing, slotting redesign, technical staffing, workforce preparation, customer planning, and zone-conversion design. Do not approve major procurement until the case uses observed network performance.

---

## Phase 3 — Warehouse 1 conversion and Warehouse 5 decision: Months 18–36

Warehouse 1 conversion sequence:

1. Shipping verification and labeling.
2. Fast-moving pick module.
3. Replenishment and internal transport.
4. Additional picking zones.
5. Packing and sortation.
6. Optional goods-to-person expansion.

At months 24–30, make a formal Warehouse 5 continue, limit, or stop decision based on actual utilization, service, workforce, and financial results.

---

## Phase 4 — Optimization: Months 30–48

- Improve slotting and utilization.
- Remove manual workarounds.
- Rebalance labor and shifts.
- Expand internal controls and maintenance capability.
- Review underperforming equipment.
- Renegotiate vendor support where appropriate.
- Relocate, lease, resell, or decommission underutilized assets.
- Conduct a 24-month reliability and benefits review for fixed automation.
- Approve further automation only through the same gates.

# 7. Workforce-transition approach

## Workforce bridge

HR and Finance will produce a monthly 36-month workforce plan for each site covering:

- Required FTEs and paid hours by role.
- Minimum safe staffing by shift.
- Picking, packing, replenishment, inventory, quality, supervision, maintenance, and technical roles.
- Redeployment.
- Vacancies not filled.
- Overtime and agency labor.
- New technical jobs.
- Voluntary exits.
- Involuntary reductions, if any.
- Training offers, completions, and qualifications.
- Time from displacement to placement.

The bridge must distinguish:

- Hours eliminated.
- Hours redeployed.
- Actual cash savings.
- Capacity release.
- New work created by automation.
- Residual workforce gap.

## Workforce sequence

For affected roles:

1. Restrict external hiring where appropriate.
2. Reduce agency and temporary labor without harming service or safety.
3. Use vacancies and natural attrition.
4. Reduce avoidable overtime.
5. Offer transfers across functions, shifts, or sites.
6. Provide paid retraining.
7. Offer voluntary retirement, separation, reduced schedules, or transfers.
8. Provide severance, benefits, outplacement, and education support.
9. Use involuntary reductions only for the documented residual requirement.

The company will not worsen schedules, pace, or working conditions to induce resignations.

## Career pathways

Offer paid pathways into:

- Automation operator.
- Robot or AMR coordinator.
- Exception-resolution associate.
- Quality and scan-verification specialist.
- Inventory-control specialist.
- Trainer or process champion.
- Maintenance trainee.
- Mechanical or electrical technician.
- PLC and controls trainee.
- WMS/WCS support specialist.
- Reliability technician.
- Operations data analyst.

Training includes:

1. Skills and interest assessment.
2. Paid foundational instruction.
3. Equipment and vendor training.
4. Supervised practical work.
5. Qualification testing.
6. Probationary placement and coaching.
7. Wage and progression review.

Training must be available across shifts and accommodate language, disability, accessibility, and legitimate scheduling needs.

## Technical staffing

Before go-live, define:

- Qualified operators and technicians by shift.
- Required certifications.
- Response-time standards.
- Internal versus external hiring.
- Vendor support duration.
- Night and weekend coverage.
- Minimum technical coverage for safe operation.
- Retention premiums where needed.

Automation will not go live without safe technical coverage.

## Fair selection and support

Use standardized, job-related criteria:

- Essential-task capability.
- Safety behavior.
- Problem-solving aptitude.
- Training and practical assessment.
- Structured interview.
- Consistently applied attendance and performance standards.

Use multiple assessors, written rubrics, accommodations, appeals, documentation, and adverse-impact review.

Employees who do not qualify for new roles must receive a documented outcome pathway, including:

- Additional training where reasonable.
- Transfer review.
- Voluntary-program information.
- Severance and outplacement eligibility.
- Education or certification support.
- Access to public and private employment services.
- Feedback on selection outcomes.
- A defined appeal process.

Before workforce reductions, Legal and Labor Relations must verify consultation, bargaining, notice, privacy, discrimination, accommodation, wage, benefits, and severance requirements.

# 8. Warehouse 1 community plan

Before major conversion:

- Complete a local labor-market and community-impact assessment.
- Establish a local technical academy.
- Partner with community colleges and workforce agencies.
- Create apprenticeships and internships.
- Give current employees first opportunity to qualify for technical roles.
- Provide training transportation or schedule flexibility.
- Coordinate with other regional employers for external placement.
- Use local contractors and suppliers where commercially practical.
- Report aggregate retention, training, technical-role, and reduction outcomes.
- Slow conversion if local redeployment capacity is insufficient.

The company will not promise indefinite employment in every existing role. It will commit to maximizing local redeployment, funding paid training, providing advance notice, and supporting employees who cannot be retained.

# 9. Service-continuity plan

## Conversion controls

- Convert one process, zone, or shift at a time.
- Avoid first-time cutovers during peak periods.
- Use approved critical-SKU buffers with aging, release, and working-capital controls.
- Maintain manual fallback.
- Keep redundant scanning, labeling, and shipping capability.
- Apply daily volume caps during ramp-up.
- Retain cross-trained employees and temporary labor.
- Stage critical spares.
- Require vendor technicians during stabilization.
- Operate a command center for the first 30–60 days.
- Include Transportation, Customer Service, and key-account teams.
- Test carrier handoff and cutoff performance end to end.
- Maintain a network order-diversion plan for extended outages.

## Service standards

### Ramp-up

- On-time shipment deterioration must remain within site-specific approved tolerance and duration.
- No critical-account contractual commitment may be missed.
- Backlog and cutoff failures must remain below predefined limits.
- Threshold breaches require daily escalation.
- Customer notification is required when an approved threshold or service-recovery plan is triggered.

### Stabilized operations

- On-time shipment must meet or exceed the locked baseline and be at least 98.5%, unless a stricter contractual target applies.
- Perfect-order rate may not deteriorate.
- Critical-account cutoff compliance may not deteriorate.
- Order-cycle time may not exceed baseline by more than 5%.
- Availability must be at least 98% during required operating windows.
- Peak-cutoff failures are zero unless an explicitly approved contingency applies.

All references to “1% deterioration” mean **one percentage point**.

## Availability and recovery measures

Report separately:

- Scheduled and unscheduled downtime.
- Partial-capacity operation.
- Starvation and blocking.
- Manual workaround hours.
- Availability during peak periods.
- Cutoff-window failures.
- Mean time to detect, repair, and recover.
- Volume affected.
- Vendor-caused and site-caused downtime.
- Recovery-time and recovery-point performance.

## Rollback

Each deployment must specify:

- Trigger thresholds.
- Decision authority.
- Inventory-reconciliation process.
- Manual labor activation.
- Customer-prioritization rules.
- Vendor response time.
- Maximum rollback duration.
- Data recovery and reconciliation.
- Cross-site diversion options.
- Customer and carrier communications.

Rollback is mandatory when service, safety, or data integrity cannot be protected.

# 10. Capital and payback framework

## Benefit classification

Report separately:

1. Productivity improvement.
2. Capacity released.
3. Avoided hiring.
4. Avoided overtime.
5. Avoided agency labor.
6. Eliminated positions.
7. Reduced turnover cost.
8. Quality and claims savings.
9. Supported incremental contribution.
10. Incremental technical, maintenance, software, and transition costs.

Productivity, redeployment, and capacity are not cash savings unless they change payroll, hiring, overtime, agency spending, or contribution.

## Net cash benefit

\[
\text{Annual Net Benefit}
=
\text{Cash Labor Savings}
+
\text{Avoided Overtime and Agency}
+
\text{Quality Savings}
+
\text{Supported Capacity Contribution}
-
\text{Incremental Operating Costs}
\]

Include:

- Equipment and software.
- Integration and commissioning.
- Construction and infrastructure.
- Paid training and release time.
- Temporary labor.
- Maintenance and spare parts.
- Energy and cybersecurity.
- Technical staffing.
- Downtime.
- Inventory buffers.
- Severance and outplacement.
- Contingency.
- Removal, residual value, and obsolescence.
- Working-capital effects.
- Insurance and liability costs where applicable.

## Required project model

Each material project requires monthly cash flows for at least five years, including:

- Benefit ramp.
- Payback start date.
- Discount rate.
- Useful life.
- Taxes and financing where relevant.
- Working-capital impact.
- Residual and removal value.
- Capitalized and expensed costs.
- Probability of success.
- Asset-exit and redeployment assumptions.

The payback clock begins when the company first commits project capital, not when stabilized benefits begin.

Calculate:

- Simple payback.
- Discounted payback.
- NPV.
- IRR.
- Return on invested capital.
- Benefit realization percentage.
- Probability-weighted NPV.

### Fixed-automation approval standard

A fixed-automation project must have:

- Positive base-case NPV.
- Simple payback of no more than three years, including transition costs.
- Positive probability-weighted NPV above the company-approved threshold.
- Capital-at-risk within the approved limit.
- Practical utilization of at least 75% by stabilization, unless a strategic exception is approved.
- No reliance on speculative volume growth.
- Defined exit, redeployment, or residual-value plan.

A negative severe-downside case requires explicit executive approval, documented rationale, and a defined maximum capital-at-risk.

## Required scenarios

Test:

- Volume 20% below plan.
- Productivity benefit 15% below plan.
- Capital cost 15–25% above plan.
- Six-month implementation delay.
- Maintenance 25–50% above estimate.
- Availability below target.
- Error improvement 50% below plan.
- Higher variable SKU mix.
- Slower redeployment.
- Combined severe downside.

## Capital controls

Initial portfolio envelope:

| Category | Allocation |
|---|---:|
| Approved deployment and foundational systems | 65% |
| Contingency and integration reserve | 15% |
| Workforce transition and training | 12% |
| Unallocated reserve | 8% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

These are planning limits, not spending authorization. Every project also requires a dollar budget.

Controls:

- Contingency draws require steering-committee approval.
- Scope changes require documented cost, service, and workforce impact.
- Workforce funds cannot be diverted to equipment.
- Unallocated reserves remain centrally controlled.
- Later-site budgets are not committed before the preceding gate.
- Stop-loss limits apply to each project.
- Leasing, staged procurement, modular systems, and outcome-based pricing must be considered for high-risk fixed automation.

# 14. Decision gates

## Gate 0 — Data readiness

Required:

- Twelve months of reconciled data.
- Peak-capacity and bottleneck model.
- Five site decision packs.
- SKU and process segmentation.
- Workforce bridge.
- Service and quality baseline.
- Facilities, cyber, safety, legal, labor, and insurance assessment.
- Named owners and decision dates.

## Gate 1 — Concept approval

Required:

- At least two technical alternatives.
- Five-year TCO and cash-flow model.
- Risk-adjusted NPV.
- Bottleneck impact.
- Workforce and training plan.
- Technical staffing plan.
- Customer and carrier continuity plan.
- Preliminary rollback design.
- Vendor references and contract protections.
- Asset-exit strategy.
- Implementation critical path.

## Gate 2 — Pilot approval

Required:

- Bounded scope.
- Control zone.
- Measurement protocol.
- Fixed dollar budget.
- Manual fallback.
- Vendor acceptance tests.
- Paid training.
- Supervisor certification.
- Safety, cyber, facilities, customer-service, and labor clearance.
- Recovery and rollback test.
- Customer communication plan.

## Gate 3 — Scale approval

After at least 8–12 stabilized weeks, a stress or peak test, and independent analysis:

- Effective productivity improves at least 10–15%.
- Pilot-process errors decline at least 25%.
- Stabilized service is at or above baseline.
- Availability meets the required-window standard.
- No increase in serious safety events.
- Manual fallback is proven.
- Technical coverage is adequate.
- Finance validates cash benefits.
- Control-zone results remain positive after adjustment.
- No critical legal, labor, cyber, facilities, customer, or workforce issue remains.

## Gate 4 — Workforce-reduction approval

Before involuntary reductions:

- Post-stabilization staffing need is documented.
- Hiring controls, attrition, overtime, agency labor, transfers, retraining, and voluntary options are reviewed.
- Selection passes fairness and legal review.
- Consultation or bargaining is complete.
- Severance, benefits, outplacement, and communications are funded.
- Employees have received appropriate training and placement opportunities.
- Warehouse 1 community impact is reviewed where applicable.
- Independent HR, Finance, Legal, and Operations sign-off is complete.

## Post-implementation reviews

Conduct formal reviews at:

- Three months.

# Final decision rule

A phase proceeds only when all eight conditions pass:

1. **Economics:** verified cash returns and acceptable risk-adjusted value.
2. **Capacity:** demonstrated bottleneck relief or validated future capacity need.
3. **Service:** customer and carrier commitments protected.
4. **Safety:** no unresolved critical risks.
5. **Technical readiness:** qualified operators, maintenance, systems, cybersecurity, and recovery capability.
6. **Workforce readiness:** paid training, redeployment, fair selection, and safe staffing.
7. **Legal compliance:** required consultation, bargaining, privacy, and employment approvals complete.
8. **Governance:** independent cross-functional validation and documented accountability.

The company should earn the right to scale through evidence. A project that meets its payback target but degrades service, creates unsafe work, fails workforce obligations, or depends on unverified assumptions must be paused, redesigned, or stopped.
