# International Innovation and Responsible Experimentation Strategy

## Executive objective

Create a company-wide environment in which employees can identify meaningful problems, challenge assumptions, test solutions responsibly, and help successful approaches reach implementation.

The company should establish:

- Global consistency in safety, access, transparency, decision rights, and learning.
- Local flexibility in communication, problem selection, customer research, scheduling, and execution.
- Protected time, authority, funding, and expertise for experimentation.
- Fair participation for frontline, remote, junior, contract, part-time, and underrepresented employees.
- Clear boundaries for responsible risk-taking.
- A reliable path from local ideas to global learning and implementation.

The objective is **not** to make every office behave identically or to maximize idea volume. It is to ensure that employees in every location can contribute meaningfully and experiment within appropriate safeguards.

Low visible participation should not automatically be interpreted as low creativity. It may reflect workload, limited authority, language barriers, headquarters dominance, weak responses to earlier ideas, or informal improvements that are not formally recognized.

---

# 1. Global standards and local flexibility

## Non-negotiable global standards

These principles should apply in every country and business unit:

1. **Meaningful relevance:** Innovation may address customer, market, operational, employee, safety, sustainability, resilience, compliance, or risk-reduction needs.
2. **Responsible experimentation:** Risk must be bounded, understood, authorized, reversible where possible, and proportionate to potential value.
3. **Psychological safety:** Employees may challenge decisions, report concerns, and share negative results without retaliation.
4. **Fair access:** Employees must have credible ways to participate regardless of role, language, location, seniority, work pattern, or employment status.
5. **Transparent decisions:** Employees should know how proposals are assessed, who decides, and when they will receive a response.
6. **Proportionate governance:** Low-risk experiments should be fast and lightweight; higher-risk work should receive appropriate specialist review.
7. **Fair credit:** Contributors to research, testing, implementation, and learning should be recognized—not only the person who first presents an idea.
8. **Knowledge sharing:** Successful, unsuccessful, and inconclusive experiments should be documented and discoverable.
9. **Legal and ethical compliance:** Innovation objectives never override safety, privacy, cybersecurity, employment, environmental, anti-corruption, or regulatory requirements.
10. **Local voice in scaling:** Local representatives must participate when an initiative developed elsewhere is considered for adoption.
11. **Voluntary participation:** Employees should not be penalized for declining to submit ideas. Innovation expectations should reflect an employee’s agreed role.

## Practices that should vary locally

Offices may adapt:

- Submission channels and meeting formats.
- Written, verbal, visual, anonymous, or facilitated contribution methods.
- Training language and examples.
- Recognition formats.
- Experiment schedules and duration within approved limits.
- Customer research methods.
- Team composition.
- Whether innovation is individual, team-based, or embedded in continuous improvement.

Local adaptation should be based on evidence about access, workload, regulation, and business conditions—not assumptions about national character.

# 2. Minimum viable innovation system

Every office should have the following basic capabilities:

1. A named local sponsor.
2. At least one accessible idea or problem-submission route.
3. Acknowledgment of submissions within five business days.
4. Initial triage within ten business days, unless specialist review is required.
5. Delegated authority for defined low-risk experiments.
6. Scheduled or paid participation time, with backfill where necessary.
7. A trained facilitator or experiment coach.
8. A simple, access-controlled experiment register.
9. A confidential escalation channel.
10. Access to global specialists.
11. Clear credit and intellectual-property rules.
12. A quarterly review of access, outcomes, learning, safety, and workload.

Formal councils, replication grants, advanced dashboards, and cross-office clinics should be enhancements for larger or more experienced offices—not prerequisites for participation. Green experiments should normally bypass formal council approval unless a defined risk trigger applies.

# 3. Create a clear idea-to-implementation process

Employees are more likely to contribute when they know what happens after submission.

| Stage | Purpose | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Problem definition** | Clarify the issue, affected users, and desired outcome | Is the problem relevant and sufficiently clear? |
| **2. Idea development** | Involve users, local experts, technical specialists, and stakeholders | Is there a credible hypothesis? |
| **3. Discovery test** | Test desirability, feasibility, compliance, or commercial potential | Continue, adapt, or stop |
| **4. Controlled pilot** | Test with limited exposure and defined measures | Expand, redesign, pause, or stop |
| **5. Scale decision** | Assess value, risk, capability, local fit, and transferability | Fund, adapt, or close |
| **6. Implementation review** | Confirm benefits and unintended effects | Continue, modify, or retire |

### Process requirements

- Employees may submit a problem without having a complete solution.
- Every accepted proposal has a named owner.
- Each stage has a maximum time and funding limit.
- Proposal status and decision rationale are visible, subject to confidentiality.
- Unanswered proposals are escalated automatically.
- Low-risk ideas do not require unnecessary committee approval.
- A successful pilot is not automatically approved for global rollout.

### Response standards

Separate the response process into three stages:

- **Acknowledgment:** Confirm receipt within five business days.
- **Triage:** Determine whether the submission is relevant, duplicative, incomplete, or ready for development within ten business days.
- **Substantive decision:** Use a longer published standard when research, stakeholder consultation, or specialist review is required.

Every decision should include a meaningful, nonconfidential explanation—for example, insufficient evidence, duplication, lack of current strategic fit, required further development, or a legal or safety constraint.

# 4. Provide capacity, authority, and resources

Innovation should not become unpaid work added to employees’ normal responsibilities.

## Role-appropriate capacity

- **Frontline and shift teams:** Scheduled improvement periods, paid participation, and backfill.
- **Manufacturing and operations:** Tests during planned maintenance or controlled production windows.
- **Customer-service teams:** Short trials with staffing coverage.
- **Professional teams:** Temporary project assignments or explicit removal of lower-priority work.
- **Remote teams:** Asynchronous collaboration and rotating meeting times.
- **Contract and contingent workers:** Clear compensation, credit, participation, and protection against adverse scheduling or renewal consequences.

Limit the number of simultaneous innovation activities per team. Before adding a substantial experiment, managers should identify work that will be removed, deferred, or reassigned.

## Federated funding model

- **Local team budget:** Low-cost, low-risk experiments.
- **Business-unit fund:** Cross-functional or moderate-risk pilots.
- **Global fund:** Strategic initiatives with international potential.

Release funding in stages. Full implementation funding should follow evidence, not precede it.

## Local decision rights

Local offices should be able to:

- Run approved green experiments without headquarters approval.
- Access a defined local budget.
- Work directly with global specialists.
- Escalate promising ideas through a guaranteed route.
- Adapt or reject global solutions that conflict with local law, customer needs, safety, or operating realities.
- Participate in replication and scale decisions.

# 5. Build psychological safety through leadership accountability

Psychological safety is created by repeated evidence that speaking up does not damage careers.

## Expected leader behaviors

Leaders should:

- Invite input before stating their own preference.
- Ask for disconfirming evidence.
- Speak last during ideation sessions.
- Publicly acknowledge incorrect assumptions.
- Thank employees who identify problems early.
- Explain why ideas were declined.
- Protect employees who raise ethical, customer, safety, or operational concerns.
- Share responsibility for experiments they sponsor.
- Ensure junior and local employees do not bear greater career risk than senior sponsors.

At experiment reviews, leaders should ask:

1. What assumption are we testing?
2. What evidence would cause us to stop?
3. What could harm customers, employees, the company, or the environment?
4. Who has not been heard?
5. What result would change our decision?

## Institutional protections

Provide:

- Confidential or anonymous submission and escalation channels.
- Anti-retaliation monitoring.
- Independent review of serious concerns.
- Speak-up audits.
- Documentation of how dissenting evidence affected decisions.
- Analysis of promotion, performance, scheduling, contract renewal, and turnover outcomes for contributors and frequent challengers.

Managers should be evaluated on whether they provide safe, fair, and adequately resourced conditions—not on the number of ideas their teams submit.

## Disciplined learning from failure

An intelligent failure:

- Had a clear hypothesis.
- Stayed within approved scope.
- Used appropriate safeguards.
- Collected relevant evidence.
- Reported negative results honestly.

By contrast, bypassing approvals, ignoring known risks, poor execution, or concealing unfavorable results remains subject to normal accountability.

# 6. Make participation inclusive and culturally adaptable

## Diagnose barriers before prescribing solutions

Each office should assess:

- Whether employees understand their decision authority.
- Whether previous ideas received meaningful responses.
- Whether workload permits experimentation.
- Whether managers filter, dismiss, or appropriate ideas.
- Whether employees have access to customers, data, technical support, and decision-makers.
- Whether global meetings favor headquarters, senior employees, or fluent English speakers.
- Whether local innovations receive funding and credit.
- Whether participation affects performance ratings, shift allocation, or contract renewal.
- Whether innovation is already occurring informally.

Assess both **innovation activity** and **innovation visibility**.

## Offer equivalent contribution routes

Use a combination of:

- Local-language submissions.
- Translation or structured summaries.
- Anonymous digital forms.
- Voice or mobile submissions.
- Small facilitated workshops.
- One-to-one discussions.
- Written input before meetings.
- Demonstrations, sketches, and prototypes.
- Asynchronous participation for remote and shift employees.

All routes should have equivalent tracking, response deadlines, confidentiality, and credit protections.

## Reduce hierarchy and presentation bias

- Use silent brainstorming and anonymous ranking.
- Provide agendas and questions in advance.
- Evaluate evidence separately from presentation fluency.
- Rotate presenters in global forums.
- Use facilitators to prevent one office, function, or language from dominating.
- Rotate meeting times across time zones.
- Make important decisions available in writing.
- Allow employees to contribute as researchers, testers, implementers, or subject-matter experts—not only as idea presenters.

# 7. Encourage disciplined experimentation and responsible risk-taking

## Risk decision tree

Before approval, assess:

1. Who may be affected?
2. Does the experiment involve customers, employees, contractors, vulnerable groups, or communities?
3. Does it use personal, sensitive, or cross-border data?
4. Does it affect safety, employment, pricing, regulated decisions, or financial outcomes?
5. Is it reversible?
6. What is the potential severity of harm?
7. Does it cross legal or jurisdictional boundaries?

When classification is uncertain, use the higher risk tier until a named risk owner reviews it.

### Green: low risk

Examples include:

- Internal workflow changes.
- Prototypes using synthetic or non-sensitive data.
- Small process improvements.
- Internal training or communication experiments.

Approval can normally be delegated to the team or local manager.

### Amber: moderate risk

Examples include:

- Customer-facing pilots.
- Use of personal data.
- Service-process or pricing tests.
- External vendors.
- Changes affecting employee workflows or customer access.

Relevant legal, privacy, security, finance, labor, compliance, or operational specialists must review the work.

### Red: high risk

Examples include:

- Safety-critical changes.
- Regulated or automated decisions.
- Significant data exposure.
- Experiments affecting vulnerable groups.
- Material employment, financial, environmental, or reputational consequences.

Formal approval, documented controls, monitoring, and a defined stop mechanism are required.

## Experimentation contract

Before a test begins, document:

- Problem and target users.
- Hypothesis.
- Scope, duration, and budget.
- Required specialist reviews.
- Success and stop criteria.
- Evidence to collect.
- Accountable owner.
- Potential unintended effects.
- How affected customers or employees will be informed.
- Contributor and intellectual-property arrangements.
- What happens to the result.

# 8. Protect data, stakeholders, confidentiality, and intellectual property

## Cross-border data governance

Experiments involving data should specify:

- Data classification and ownership.
- Purpose and permitted use.
- Consent and notification requirements.
- Cross-border transfer mechanisms.
- Data-localization requirements.
- Security controls.
- Algorithmic impact, bias, and explainability assessments where relevant.
- Retention, deletion, and incident-reporting procedures.
- Responsibilities of global data owners and local business owners.

A named global or regional data-risk owner should determine whether local privacy, labor, or regulatory review is mandatory before data is accessed or transferred.

## Stakeholder mapping

Every amber or red experiment—and any green experiment materially affecting people—should identify relevant:

- Customers and service users.
- Employees and employee representatives.
- Works councils or trade unions.
- Suppliers and external partners.
- Regulators.
- Local communities.
- Accessibility, sustainability, human-rights, or data-protection specialists.
- Boards or audit committees for material risks.

The experiment record should state how affected stakeholders will be consulted, informed, protected, or given a route to complain or object.

Experiments affecting employment conditions, monitoring, compensation, scheduling, staffing, or worker data must comply with applicable labor law and collective agreements.

## Confidentiality and information access

Experiment registers should balance transparency with confidentiality:

- **Company-wide:** Broad problem, status, decision, and learning.
- **Restricted:** Detailed design and results for relevant teams.
- **Confidential:** Sensitive commercial, personal, security, or legal information.
- **Highly restricted:** Legally privileged or safety-sensitive material.

## Credit and intellectual property

Before material experimentation begins, document:

- Contributors and roles.
- Contributions from different offices.
- Contractor and temporary-worker arrangements.
- Intellectual-property ownership.
- Confidentiality requirements.
- Recognition and financial-reward eligibility.
- Local-office credit.
- Participation rights in scale decisions.
- Dispute-resolution procedures.

---

# 9. Align incentives with useful innovation and learning

Recognize:

- Important problems identified.
- Strong customer insight.
- Well-designed experiments.
- Early stopping that prevents waste.
- Cross-office collaboration.
- Reusable methods and tools.
- Successful implementation.
- Ethical risk escalation.
- Learning that changes a business decision.
- Contributions to testing, implementation, and operational adoption.

Potential recognition includes:

- Performance-review and promotion evidence where innovation is part of the role.
- Development opportunities.
- Additional experimentation time or budget.
- Team awards.
- Senior-leader visibility.
- Financial rewards where appropriate.

Do not:

- Base bonuses on raw idea or experiment volume.
- Require a fixed number of successful innovations.
- Reward the number of failed experiments.
- Penalize nonparticipation.
- Give disproportionate credit to headquarters for locally developed work.

# 10. Enable cross-office learning without centralizing innovation

Use a federated model:

- Local offices own market-specific problems and experiments.
- Global teams provide standards, platforms, expertise, and funding.
- A shared portfolio identifies results relevant to other offices.
- Local teams decide how and whether to adapt external solutions.

Useful mechanisms include:

- Searchable experiment repositories.
- Communities of practice.
- Cross-office problem clinics.
- Virtual project teams and short-term exchanges.
- Global challenge themes with locally defined solutions.
- Replication grants.
- Global reviews that include representatives from affected markets.

A successful pilot in one market is evidence for another local test—not automatic proof of global scalability.

## Resolving global-local disagreements

1. The local team documents market, regulatory, customer, operational, or employee evidence.
2. A joint global-local panel reviews the evidence.
3. The panel identifies mandatory and adaptable elements.
4. It issues a reasoned decision within a fixed period.
5. The decision records remaining risks, owners, and responsibilities.

Headquarters should not override documented local legal, safety, or customer risks without explicit justification. Local rejection should likewise be based on evidence rather than preference alone.

---

# 11. Governance and implementation ownership

## Global innovation council

Responsible for:

- Global principles and minimum standards.
- Cross-office portfolio visibility.
- Strategic themes.
- Replication and scale funding.
- Removal of systemic barriers.
- Reviewing whether governance remains proportionate.

## Local sponsor or council

Responsible for:

- Local problem priorities.
- Delegated funding.
- Local support and facilitation.
- Regulatory and operational fit.
- Escalation of ideas with global potential.

Small offices may use a named sponsor rather than a formal council.

## Experiment owner

Responsible for:

- Hypothesis and test design.
- Stakeholder involvement.
- Risk controls.
- Documentation and honest reporting.
- Contributor records.
- Recommendation to continue, adapt, stop, or scale.

## Operational acceptance before scaling

A receiving operational owner must formally accept responsibility before a pilot is scaled. Acceptance should cover:

- Permanent ownership.
- Operating budget.
- Staffing and training.
- Technology and support.
- Procurement.
- Compliance monitoring.
- Customer and employee communications.
- Benefits realization.
- Incident responsibilities.
- Maintenance and retirement criteria.

Governance should be judged by the time it adds and the risks it reduces. Controls that delay low-risk experiments without reducing material risk should be removed.

# 12. Phased implementation

## Phase 1: Diagnose and design — first 90 days

- Appoint an executive sponsor and global program owner.
- Select representative pilot offices, including at least one lower-capacity location.
- Survey frontline, junior, remote, contract, part-time, and nonparticipating employees.
- Identify existing informal innovation.
- Map idea, funding, procurement, data, labor, legal, and implementation bottlenecks.
- Define global principles, risk tiers, decision rights, response standards, credit rules, and confidentiality levels.
- Audit language, accessibility, time-zone, workload, and data-access barriers.
- Establish baseline measures and phase-gate criteria.

## Phase 2: Pilot and learn — months 3–6

- Launch multiple contribution channels.
- Provide delegated green-test budgets.
- Provide scheduled participation time and frontline backfill.
- Coach managers in inquiry, dissent, experiment design, and learning reviews.
- Run problem-focused experiments.
- Publish a simple, access-controlled experiment register.
- Review participation, funding, credit, workload, safety, and influence.
- Remove process steps that add delay without reducing material risk.

## Phase 3: Scale selectively — months 6–18

Expand only when pilots demonstrate:

- Timely decisions.
- Acceptable workload.
- No material control failures.
- Equitable access and recognition.
- Useful learning or implementation.
- Clear operational ownership.
- No persistent retaliation or concealment concerns.

Then:

- Expand delegated authority where controls work.
- Add innovation-supportive leadership behaviors to management evaluations.
- Establish replication funding and adaptation reviews.
- Integrate innovation into budgeting, procurement, technology, and business reviews.
- Build local facilitator and experiment-coach capability.
- Monitor whether headquarters disproportionately controls funding, credit, or scale decisions.

## Phase 4: Institutionalize and refresh — 18 months onward

- Review the portfolio quarterly.
- Retire inactive ideas, councils, and unnecessary processes.
- Compare offices on access, process quality, learning, and outcomes—not raw volume.
- Audit inclusion, credit, promotion, workload, and psychological-safety outcomes.
- Reassess safeguards after incidents or regulatory changes.
- Rotate council membership.
- Fund additional capability where authority or resources remain uneven.
- Pause or redesign programs that create burnout, service deterioration, repeated control failures, or unequal career consequences.

# 13. Measurement framework

Use a small balanced scorecard, not a league table.

## Access and participation

- Participation through any approved channel.
- Participation by office, role, level, language, work pattern, and relevant demographic groups.
- Access to paid or scheduled participation time.
- Access to coaching, funding, data, and decision-makers.
- Employee perception that local evidence is taken seriously.

## Process quality and speed

- Time from submission to acknowledgment.
- Time from submission to triage.
- Time from approval to first test.
- Percentage of proposals with named owners.
- Percentage with hypotheses, risk assessments, and stop criteria.
- Percentage receiving decisions within the service standard.

## Learning and transfer

- Percentage of experiments stopped or adapted based on evidence.
- Percentage with documented learning reviews.
- Reuse of insights across offices.
- Time from validated result to another office’s first local test.
- Percentage of scaled initiatives with written operational acceptance.

## Business and stakeholder outcomes

- Revenue, margin, productivity, quality, or cycle-time improvement.
- Customer adoption and satisfaction.
- Employee experience and safety improvements.
- Sustainability and risk-reduction outcomes.
- Benefits realized after implementation.
- Unintended incidents, complaints, or control breaches.

## Metric safeguards

- Do not reward raw idea or experiment volume.
- Do not require a fixed number of successful innovations.
- Do not penalize nonparticipation.
- Use qualitative evidence alongside quantitative data.
- Report results with context such as capacity, regulation, and market complexity.
- Aggregate or suppress small-group data to protect privacy.
- Review metrics for gaming at least twice a year.
- Treat overwork, service deterioration, hidden harms, and control breaches as counter-metrics.
- Do not identify individual contributors unnecessarily.

---

# 14. Immediate priorities

Within the first 30 days, the company should:

1. Name an executive sponsor and global program owner.
2. Select representative pilot offices, including at least one lower-capacity office.
3. Guarantee acknowledgment and triage of every proposal.
4. Conduct an access, workload, and psychological-safety diagnosis.
5. Identify existing informal improvements.
6. Define delegated authority for green experiments.
7. Provide a small local budget and scheduled participation time.
8. Establish multilingual, anonymous, asynchronous, and facilitated contribution routes.
9. Publish credit, intellectual-property, confidentiality, and anti-retaliation rules.
10. Create a simple experiment register.
11. Train pilot managers through practical coaching.
12. Establish data, stakeholder, labor, and risk-review protocols.
13. Set phase-gate criteria for workload, equity, safety, learning, and implementation readiness.
14. Review results after 60–90 days and simplify or stop elements that add burden without improving access, learning, safety, or implementation.

The desired outcome is not maximum idea volume. It is a reliable international system in which employees in every office can identify meaningful problems, contribute in ways suited to their circumstances, experiment responsibly, receive fair treatment, and see credible evidence that their input can influence decisions.
