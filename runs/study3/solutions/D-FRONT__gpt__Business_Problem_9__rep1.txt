# Global Innovation and Responsible Experimentation Strategy

## Executive intent

The company should build an innovation system in which employees in every office can:

1. Identify meaningful customer, market, and operational opportunities.
2. Raise ideas safely, regardless of seniority or location.
3. Test assumptions through small, controlled experiments.
4. Learn quickly from evidence—including unsuccessful results.
5. Scale proven solutions across markets with appropriate local ownership.

Differences between branches should not be treated as evidence that some national cultures are inherently more or less innovative. Variation may instead reflect leadership practices, workload, decision rights, resources, regulatory requirements, trust, language, or the ease of moving an idea into action. The company should therefore diagnose local barriers before deploying a single global program.

The recommended approach is to standardize a small number of enterprise principles and controls while giving offices meaningful discretion over problems, participation methods, and local experimentation.

---

# Strategic priorities

## Pillar 1: Diagnose, segment, and address the real constraint

**Priority:** Highest  
**Accountable owners:** Global Innovation Enablement Lead, Chief Human Resources Officer, regional and country leaders  
**Supporting owners:** Local employee representatives, Legal, Compliance, IT, Procurement, Finance

### Objective

Determine whether each location’s primary constraint is a lack of ideas, limited psychological safety, insufficient capacity, slow approvals, weak incentives, limited resources, or poor follow-through.

### Actions

Within the first 90 days:

- Conduct a consistent employee survey covering:
  - Ability to speak up and challenge decisions
  - Manager support for experimentation
  - Consequences of failure
  - Access to time, funding, data, customers, and expertise
  - Fairness of idea review and recognition
  - Confidence that ideas will receive a response
- Conduct interviews and focus groups with employees at different levels, functions, employment types, and shifts.
- Map the current path from idea to decision, pilot, and implementation in representative countries.
- Review performance goals, promotion criteria, workload, budgets, procurement, technology, and approval requirements.
- Include Legal, Compliance, Privacy, Works Councils or employee representatives, and local management in the diagnosis.
- Publish a summary of key findings and the actions that will follow.

### Maturity-based segmentation

Use maturity as a guide for support—not as a ranking of countries.

| Maturity | Typical condition | Initial emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| **Foundation** | Low trust, limited employee voice, unclear decision rights, little experimentation | Leadership behavior, psychological safety, local listening, removal of basic barriers |
| **Developing** | Ideas exist but stall before testing | Simple idea-to-experiment process, microfunding, response deadlines, sponsors |
| **Established** | Regular pilots occur but scaling and knowledge transfer are weak | Portfolio management, business ownership, cross-border replication |
| **Advanced** | Repeatable experimentation and strong local capability | Larger strategic bets, ecosystem partnerships, mentoring other branches |

A branch’s support should reflect its operating context and dominant bottleneck. A low-maturity office should not be given an innovation contest when the real issue is that employees do not trust the response process.

## Pillar 2: Establish a minimum viable idea-to-experiment system

**Priority:** Highest  
**Accountable owners:** Business-unit and country leaders  
**Supporting owners:** Global Innovation Enablement, Finance, Legal, Compliance, IT, Procurement

### Objective

Make it straightforward to move from a problem to a bounded, evidence-based test without demanding a fully developed business case at the outset.

### Standard process

Implement a common enterprise process with local flexibility:

1. **Identify an opportunity**  
   Capture customer pain points, operational friction, market shifts, sustainability opportunities, or recurring workarounds.

2. **Submit a concise idea or problem statement**  
   Required information:
   - Problem and affected users
   - Proposed change or hypothesis
   - Expected value
   - Relevant risks
   - Suggested owner

3. **Provide a timely response**  
   Every submission should receive acknowledgment and an initial decision, referral, or request for clarification within a defined service standard.

4. **Conduct a discovery sprint**  
   Validate the problem, users, assumptions, legal considerations, and baseline data.

5. **Run a micro-pilot**  
   Use a small budget, limited population, defined duration, and named sponsor.

6. **Review evidence**  
   Decide transparently to:
   - Continue
   - Adapt
   - Stop
   - Transfer
   - Scale

7. **Assign an operating owner**  
   No pilot should scale without a business owner, implementation plan, and resource commitment.

### Minimum experiment template

Every experiment should document:

- Hypothesis
- Target users or process
- Baseline
- Intervention
- Success measures
- Duration
- Owner and sponsor
- Maximum spend and exposure
- Required approvals
- Stop conditions
- Evidence review date
- Decision and lessons learned

The process should favor small, fast, reversible tests where appropriate. Employees should not be expected to invest months in a business case before the core assumption has been tested.

### Resources

Provide:

- Local or business-unit microfunds
- Protected experimentation capacity
- Access to customer insight and relevant data
- Technical, design, legal, compliance, and procurement advisers
- Internal mentors and subject-matter experts
- A fast escalation channel for ambiguous approval questions

Funding should be staged:

- **Idea grants:** research, interviews, and prototypes
- **Pilot grants:** controlled tests
- **Scale funding:** investment tied to evidence and business ownership

Reserve a portion of funds for smaller, lower-maturity, or underrepresented branches so that access is fair, while allowing funding to vary according to opportunity and readiness.

## Pillar 3: Make leadership behavior and psychological safety measurable

**Priority:** Highest  
**Accountable owners:** Country managers and line managers  
**Supporting owners:** CHRO, HR business partners, regional leaders

### Objective

Ensure that employees can challenge assumptions, report bad news, and test ideas without fear of arbitrary career consequences.

### Required leadership behaviors

Leaders should consistently:

- Ask for alternative views before stating their own preference.
- Separate evaluation of an idea from evaluation of the person proposing it.
- Invite input from frontline, remote, shift-based, and less senior employees.
- Acknowledge their own mistakes and uncertainty.
- Respond constructively when experiments produce unfavorable results.
- Explain why ideas are declined, deferred, or redirected.
- Protect agreed experimentation time.
- Credit local teams when their ideas are adopted elsewhere.
- Stop experiments promptly when risk or evidence warrants it.

These behaviors should be included in leadership assessments, employee feedback, promotion discussions, and succession reviews. Training may support the change, but training alone will not overcome performance systems that punish experimentation.

### Team practices

Managers should use practical methods such as:

- Written idea generation before discussion
- Anonymous input where trust is low or local circumstances warrant it
- Structured turn-taking
- Rotating meeting facilitators
- Regular “what did we learn?” reviews
- Explicit invitations to disagree
- After-action reviews that distinguish poor results from poor discipline or misconduct

### Definition of protected responsible failure

An unsuccessful experiment should be treated as a learning event when it had:

- A documented hypothesis and accountable owner
- Appropriate risk review
- Defined budget, scope, and stop conditions
- Honest reporting of results
- No negligence, concealment, misconduct, or avoidable control breach

Psychological safety means employees can surface uncertainty and mistakes honestly. It does not eliminate accountability.

## Pillar 4: Align incentives, capacity, and recognition

**Priority:** High  
**Accountable owners:** CHRO, CFO, country and business-unit leaders  
**Supporting owners:** Line managers, Finance, HR, Global Innovation Enablement

### Objective

Make innovation a feasible part of work rather than unpaid extra effort or a career risk.

### Actions

- Include role-appropriate innovation contributions in objectives and performance conversations.
- Tailor expectations by role:
  - Frontline teams may identify problems, test process changes, or provide customer insight.
  - Technical and product teams may run prototypes and experiments.
  - Control functions may identify safer or more efficient compliance processes.
  - Leaders may sponsor, remove barriers, and scale solutions.
- Allocate explicit capacity for experimentation. Managers should not be required to add innovation to already full workloads.
- Use team-based recognition to encourage collaboration rather than internal competition.
- Recognize:
  - Useful problem identification
  - Customer insight
  - Disciplined experiments
  - Transparent reporting of results
  - Cross-border collaboration
  - Reusable learning
  - Successful adoption
- Give career credit for responsible innovation contributions.
- Do not reward idea volume, reckless risk-taking, or pilots launched without evidence.
- Define intellectual-property ownership, attribution, confidentiality, and benefit-sharing when local ideas scale globally.

Recognition should be locally appropriate. Some employees may prefer public acknowledgment; others may value development opportunities, project leadership, or direct manager recognition. Local employee input should determine the most credible forms.

## Pillar 5: Build cross-border knowledge sharing and equitable participation

**Priority:** High  
**Accountable owners:** Regional leaders and Global Innovation Enablement Lead  
**Supporting owners:** Country managers, Communications, IT, HR, business-unit owners

### Objective

Allow useful solutions and lessons to move between offices while preserving local ownership and relevance.

### Actions

Create a lightweight global knowledge system containing:

- Problems being explored
- Experiments underway
- Standard experiment summaries
- Results and lessons
- Reusable prototypes, tools, and methods
- Local implementation requirements
- Relevant legal, regulatory, or data considerations
- Contacts for subject-matter experts
- Business owners for solutions ready to transfer

Establish:

- Regional communities of practice
- Cross-country problem-solving teams
- Rotating challenge campaigns linked to strategic priorities
- Peer reviews between offices
- Shared expert pools in technology, design, data, legal, compliance, procurement, and customer research
- Cross-border innovation sprints
- Quarterly showcases featuring both successful and stopped experiments

### Participation safeguards

To prevent headquarters or larger markets from dominating:

- Offer multilingual or plain-language submission and review support.
- Enable asynchronous participation for time zones and shift workers.
- Rotate meeting times.
- Record and document decisions.
- Track participation and funding by location, role, level, and employment type where lawful.
- Publish funding decisions, rejection reasons, and scale outcomes.
- Attribute originating teams and contributors.
- Share benefits and learning with the branch that generated the idea.

The goal is fair access to opportunity, not identical funding or identical innovation activity in every office.

## Pillar 6: Apply proportionate governance and ethical guardrails

**Priority:** Non-negotiable  
**Accountable owners:** General Counsel, Chief Compliance Officer, Chief Risk Officer, CIO or Data Protection Lead  
**Supporting owners:** Country and business-unit leaders, experiment owners

### Objective

Enable responsible risk-taking without compromising legal, ethical, customer, employee, financial, or operational obligations.

### Global risk tiers

#### Tier 1: Low-risk internal tests

Examples may include internal workflow improvements, prototypes using non-sensitive information, or simulated tests.

- Local approval within defined spending limits
- Basic documentation
- Clear duration and stop criteria

#### Tier 2: Managed experiments

Examples may involve customers, operational processes, financial exposure, personal data, external vendors, or production systems.

- Relevant legal, privacy, compliance, technology, procurement, or business review
- Defined safeguards and monitoring
- Explicit customer and employee protections
- Formal evidence review

#### Tier 3: Restricted experiments

Examples may include safety-critical work, regulated products, sensitive personal data, significant financial exposure, material brand risk, or major changes to customer rights.

- Specialist and enterprise approval
- Formal risk assessment
- Documented controls, monitoring, and escalation
- No local bypass of mandatory requirements

Global standards should include:

- Legal and regulatory compliance
- Privacy and information security
- Safety and product quality
- Anti-retaliation for good-faith challenge
- Ethical treatment of customers and employees
- Financial authority and fraud controls
- Intellectual-property and confidentiality requirements
- Accurate reporting of results
- Stop-work and escalation rights

Local offices should adapt approval routes, documentation, language, and thresholds to local law and operating conditions. A fast escalation route should be available when an experiment’s risk classification is unclear.

# What should be standardized globally and what should adapt locally

| Area | Standardized globally | Adapted locally |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Innovation supports customer value, responsiveness, sustainable performance, and responsible risk-taking | Local strategic priorities and market opportunities |
| Ethics and risk | Legal, privacy, safety, integrity, financial, and anti-retaliation requirements | Local review bodies, documentation, and regulatory processes |
| Idea-to-experiment process | Minimum stages, experiment template, feedback commitment, decision definitions | Submission channels, language, meeting format, cadence, and facilitation |
| Psychological safety | Respectful treatment and protection for good-faith challenge | Anonymous versus open participation and local communication practices |
| Leadership | Curiosity, humility, evidence-based decisions, constructive response to bad news | Coaching style and engagement methods |
| Funding | Staged funding logic, transparency, and accountability | Local currency, procurement, approval thresholds, and budget authority |
| Incentives | Recognition for learning, collaboration, responsible experimentation, and impact | Form of rewards and culturally credible recognition |
| Knowledge sharing | Common documentation, searchability, attribution, and core metrics | Time-zone arrangements, translation, local networks, and implementation methods |
| Measurement | Common definitions for core indicators and guardrails | Local outcome measures and qualitative interpretation |
| Employee voice | Access to fair channels and documented responses | Channels suited to local trust, workforce structure, and legal requirements |

Global standards should be limited to what is necessary for fairness, safety, transparency, learning, and enterprise coordination. Local autonomy must include resources and decision rights—not merely permission to customize communications.

# Governance and accountability

## Global Innovation Council

**Chair:** Executive sponsor, such as the COO or CEO-designated leader  
**Members:** Business-unit executives, CHRO, CFO, Legal, Compliance, Risk, IT/Data, regional representatives, and local employee representatives where appropriate

Responsibilities:

- Set enterprise principles and risk boundaries
- Approve major investment and cross-enterprise scaling
- Review portfolio balance and equity of access
- Resolve systemic barriers
- Monitor guardrails and business outcomes
- Ensure local voices are represented in decisions

## Global Innovation Enablement

A small central team should enable rather than own all innovation. It should provide:

- Standards and templates
- Capability building
- Measurement
- Portfolio visibility
- Expert networks
- Cross-border transfer support
- Identification of recurring process barriers

Business units and country teams should own customer problems, pilots, implementation, and benefits.

## Regional networks

Regional teams should coordinate:

- Shared expertise
- Cross-country pilots
- Communities of practice
- Regulatory and market learning
- Peer support
- Regional funding where justified

## Local accountability

Each branch should designate:

- A senior innovation sponsor
- A process coordinator or facilitator
- Experiment owners
- Business owners for solutions intended to scale

Quarterly portfolio reviews should examine where ideas stall, how quickly decisions are made, whether resources are distributed fairly, and whether pilots are producing useful learning or value.

# Phased implementation roadmap

## Phase 0: Diagnose and baseline — 0 to 3 months

**Primary owners:** Global Innovation Enablement, HR, country and regional leaders

Actions:

- Select a representative sample of offices, including different maturity levels and operating contexts.
- Complete employee survey, interviews, and focus groups.
- Map idea-to-decision and approval processes.
- Review incentives, workload, funding, and leadership practices.
- Conduct legal, compliance, privacy, labor, and technology scans.
- Inventory existing innovation programs and remove duplication.
- Establish baseline measures.
- Segment branches by maturity and dominant constraint.
- Form an executive and local stakeholder coalition.

Deliverables:

- Barrier heat map
- Branch maturity profiles
- Baseline scorecard
- Pilot design
- Global minimum framework proposal

## Phase 1: Pilot a minimum viable system — 3 to 6 months

**Primary owners:** Selected country and business-unit leaders  
**Supporting owners:** Global Enablement, HR, Legal, Compliance, Finance

Actions:

- Select two or three pilot locations:
  - At least one Foundation or Developing branch
  - At least one Established or Advanced branch
  - Locations with different operating and regulatory conditions
- Introduce the short experiment template and risk tiers.
- Provide local microfunding and protected capacity.
- Train managers and facilitators in constructive challenge and learning reviews.
- Set response-time commitments for ideas.
- Launch a small number of locally relevant experiments.
- Test different participation formats, including written, anonymous, multilingual, and asynchronous channels.
- Establish an initial knowledge-sharing repository.
- Include at least one pilot that could transfer across borders.

Success at this stage means learning which mechanisms reduce friction—not maximizing idea volume.

## Phase 2: Expand and institutionalize — 6 to 18 months

**Primary owners:** Business-unit executives, regional leaders, CHRO

Actions:

- Expand the minimum viable process based on pilot evidence.
- Integrate innovation contributions into performance and talent discussions.
- Establish regional communities of practice and expert pools.
- Add manager behavior and psychological safety to leadership reviews.
- Introduce quarterly portfolio and equity reviews.
- Standardize experiment reporting and decision records.
- Improve digital tools only where the process has demonstrated value.
- Create formal scale criteria and assign business owners earlier.
- Recognize and transfer successful local practices.

## Phase 3: Scale and continuously improve — 18 to 36 months

**Primary owners:** Global Innovation Council and business-unit leadership

Actions:

- Scale proven solutions with documented benefits and operational ownership.
- Link the innovation portfolio to strategic planning and budgeting.
- Support advanced offices in larger, carefully governed experiments.
- Use mature offices to mentor developing branches.
- Refresh risk thresholds, funding levels, and tools based on evidence.
- Retire programs that create activity without learning or impact.
- Conduct an annual review with local employee input.
- Update global standards as markets, technologies, and regulations change.

# Balanced scorecard

Metrics should support learning and improvement, not simplistic country league tables. Results should be interpreted alongside branch maturity, role mix, workload, regulatory constraints, and access to resources.

## Leading indicators

### Access and capacity

- Percentage of employees with access to an idea or improvement channel
- Percentage of selected teams with protected experimentation capacity
- Funding available and funding used by branch and maturity level
- Number of active sponsors, facilitators, and advisers
- Participation adjusted for role, shift, location, and employment type

### Employee climate and leadership

- Psychological-safety score
- Confidence in challenging managers
- Perceived fairness of funding and recognition
- Belief that responsible innovation supports career progression
- Manager scores for inviting dissent, handling bad news, and protecting capacity
- Repeat participation rate

### Process quality and speed

- Median time from submission to initial decision
- Percentage of ideas receiving documented feedback
- Median time from approval to first experiment
- Percentage of experiments with a hypothesis, owner, risk review, and stop criteria
- Percentage of experiments reaching an evidence-based decision
- Number and age of stalled ideas

### Inclusion and knowledge sharing

- Share of funding and pilots originating outside headquarters
- Cross-country and cross-functional participation
- Reuse of tools, prototypes, or learning
- Number of local ideas reviewed for transfer
- Contributor attribution and benefit-sharing compliance

## Lagging indicators

### Business and customer outcomes

- Revenue or margin from new or improved offerings
- Cost, quality, productivity, or cycle-time improvements
- Customer satisfaction, retention, effort, or service outcomes
- Adoption and sustained-use rate after six and twelve months
- Benefits realized versus forecast
- Speed of response to material market or operational changes

### Organizational outcomes

- Employee engagement and retention
- Reduced duplication across offices
- Increased internal mobility into innovation-related work
- Number of successful solutions scaled across markets
- Business-unit ownership of scaled solutions

### Guardrails

- Compliance, privacy, safety, security, and quality incidents
- Customer or employee harm
- Uncontrolled financial exposure
- Operational disruption or missed service commitments
- Excessive overtime, burnout, or crowding out of core work
- Experiments conducted outside required approvals
- Concentration of funding or recognition among a small group of offices or senior employees

Activity measures such as idea counts, events, training completion, and pilots launched should never be treated as success on their own.

# Immediate next steps

1. Appoint an executive sponsor and establish the Global Innovation Council.
2. Select a cross-country diagnostic sample with both lower- and higher-maturity offices.
3. Launch the employee survey, interviews, and process mapping.
4. Define the global minimum standards for ethics, risk, anti-retaliation, documentation, and feedback.
5. Choose two or three pilot locations and appoint accountable country sponsors.
6. Provide small, staged funds and protected capacity for the first experiments.
7. Set a clear service standard for acknowledging and responding to ideas.
8. Train pilot managers in psychological safety, constructive challenge, and learning reviews.
9. Define the initial scorecard and baseline all measures before pilots begin.
10. Conduct a 90- to 120-day review with local employees and leaders, remove proven barriers, and scale only the practices that demonstrate adoption, learning, responsible governance, and business ownership.
